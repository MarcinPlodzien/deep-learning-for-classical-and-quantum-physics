Lab 043 — Pendulum with unknown friction

A pendulum swinging in air obeys Newton’s equation with two forces. Gravity is known to high accuracy, while the friction depends on the bearing, the shape of the bob and the air, and its form is usually unknown. In this notebook we keep the gravitational term of the equation of motion and let a small neural network \(U_\theta\) represent the unknown friction inside the differential equation. The network is trained through the ODE solver on one measured swing. Afterwards we fit a short formula to the learned function by sparse regression and obtain a closed equation of motion that can be read, checked and used for forecasts far beyond the measured window. A differential equation that combines known terms with a neural network in this way is called a universal differential equation [C. Rackauckas et al., arXiv:2001.04385].

We start from the physics of three friction laws, viscous, quadratic air drag and dry (Coulomb) friction, and show how each of them removes energy from the swing at its own rate. A synthetic measurement with air drag, whose coefficient the models never see, serves as data. Three models are compared on it, gravity alone, a pure neural ordinary differential equation (neural ODE) in which a network represents the whole vector field [R. T. Q. Chen et al., arXiv:1806.07366], and the hybrid model with gravity plus \(U_\theta\). Sequentially thresholded least squares, the regression step of the sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) [S. L. Brunton, J. L. Proctor, J. N. Kutz, PNAS 113, 3932 (2016), doi:10.1073/pnas.1517384113], then turns the learned friction into a formula. At the end the procedure is repeated for the other two friction laws.

The implementation uses JAX. The fourth-order Runge–Kutta integrator, the networks, the training loop with jax.grad and the optax Adam optimiser, and the sparse regression are written as short functions in the notebook. The solutions and gradients of our integrator are compared with the adaptive solvers of the diffrax library.

One pendulum, three models

Suppose a pendulum is filmed while it swings and slowly comes to rest, and only its angle is recorded. Newton’s law says that the angular acceleration is the sum of two contributions, the pull of gravity, \(-\omega_0^2\sin\theta\), and the braking by friction. Gravity is known exactly. The friction is not, since it may grow with the speed, with the square of the speed, or stay constant, and its strength is unknown as well.

Three models of the swing can be built from this knowledge. The first keeps only gravity, \(\ddot\theta=-\omega_0^2\sin\theta\). It has nothing to learn, and it is wrong, since its pendulum swings forever. The second replaces the whole right-hand side by a neural network, as a neural ODE does, and ignores the gravity that we know. It can fit the measured swing, but it has to learn gravity from the data as well, and beyond the measured window it can only guess. The third keeps gravity and gives the network only the missing piece, \[\ddot\theta=-\omega_0^2\sin\theta+U_\theta(\theta,\dot\theta).\] The network now has a much smaller task. It learns how strongly the pendulum is braked at each angle and speed, and it is trained by integrating this equation and comparing the result with the recorded swing. This third model is the hybrid model.

After training, the function \(U_\theta\) can be plotted against the angular velocity, and its shape can be compared with a list of simple candidate formulas, a term proportional to the speed, to the speed times its absolute value, to the sign of the speed, and a few others. Sparse regression picks the shortest combination that matches the learned curve. When it returns a single term such as \(-0.15\,\dot\theta|\dot\theta|\), the network has been turned back into a law of physics, here air drag, and the resulting equation, gravity plus this term, can be used far beyond the data.

1. Pendulum with friction

A rigid pendulum of length \(\ell\) in the gravitational field \(g\) is described by the angle \(\theta\) from the downward vertical and the angular velocity \(\dot\theta\). Per unit moment of inertia the equation of motion reads \[\ddot\theta=-\omega_0^2\sin\theta+F(\dot\theta),\qquad \omega_0^2=\frac{g}{\ell},\] where \(F\) is the angular deceleration caused by friction. We measure time in units of \(1/\omega_0\), so that \(\omega_0=1\) and the period of small oscillations is \(2\pi\). Three friction laws are common in mechanics, \[F_{\rm visc}=-\gamma\,\dot\theta,\qquad F_{\rm quad}=-c\,\dot\theta\,|\dot\theta|,\qquad F_{\rm dry}=-\mu\,\tanh(\dot\theta/\varepsilon).\] Viscous friction is linear in the velocity and describes a bob moving slowly through a fluid or a lubricated bearing. Quadratic drag describes air resistance at large Reynolds number, where the force grows with the square of the speed and always opposes the motion. Dry, or Coulomb, friction has a constant magnitude \(\mu\) and the direction opposite to the velocity, \(F=-\mu\,{\rm sign}(\dot\theta)\). The sign function has a jump at \(\dot\theta=0\) that a standard ODE solver handles badly, so we smooth it with \(\tanh(\dot\theta/\varepsilon)\) and \(\varepsilon=0.05\), a width small compared with the typical angular velocity of order one. The coefficients used throughout are \(\gamma=0.1\), \(c=0.15\) and \(\mu=0.05\).

The mechanical energy per unit moment of inertia \[E=\tfrac12\dot\theta^2+\omega_0^2\,(1-\cos\theta)\] is conserved without friction. With friction its rate of change is \(\dot E=\dot\theta\,F(\dot\theta)\), which is negative for all three laws, since each force opposes the velocity.

Code 
W0SQ = 1.0                      # omega_0^2 = g / l, time in units of 1 / omega_0
EPS = 0.05                      # smoothing width of the sign function in dry friction
FRICTION = {                    # F(thetadot; p), the angular deceleration of each friction law
    "viscous":   lambda w, p: -p * w,
    "quadratic": lambda w, p: -p * w * jnp.abs(w),
    "dry":       lambda w, p: -p * jnp.tanh(w / EPS),
}
COEF = {"viscous": 0.1, "quadratic": 0.15, "dry": 0.05}          # gamma, c, mu
COEF_SYMBOL = {"viscous": r"\gamma", "quadratic": "c", "dry": r"\mu"}
LAWS = tuple(FRICTION)

def pendulum(law, p):
    # vector field f(s, t) of the state s = (theta, thetadot)
    def f(s, t):
        th, w = s[0], s[1]
        return jnp.array([w, -W0SQ * jnp.sin(th) + FRICTION[law](w, p)])
    return f

def energy(S):
    # E = thetadot^2 / 2 + omega_0^2 (1 - cos theta) for an array of states (..., 2)
    S = np.asarray(S)
    return 0.5 * S[..., 1] ** 2 + W0SQ * (1 - np.cos(S[..., 0]))

Fourth-order Runge–Kutta integrator

Every trajectory in this notebook comes from the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) method. For the autonomous or time-dependent vector field \(\dot{\mathbf s}=\mathbf f(\mathbf s,t)\) one step of size \(h\) reads \[\begin{aligned} \mathbf k_1&=\mathbf f(\mathbf s_n,t_n), & \mathbf k_2&=\mathbf f(\mathbf s_n+\tfrac h2\mathbf k_1,t_n+\tfrac h2),\\ \mathbf k_3&=\mathbf f(\mathbf s_n+\tfrac h2\mathbf k_2,t_n+\tfrac h2), & \mathbf k_4&=\mathbf f(\mathbf s_n+h\mathbf k_3,t_n+h),\\ \mathbf s_{n+1}&=\mathbf s_n+\tfrac h6(\mathbf k_1+2\mathbf k_2+2\mathbf k_3+\mathbf k_4), \end{aligned}\] with a local error of order \(h^5\) and a global error of order \(h^4\). The function rk4_solve returns the states on the grid \(t_0,t_0+h,\dots\) and can take sub smaller steps between two stored points. It is a jax.lax.scan over the steps, so jax.grad can differentiate any loss of the trajectory with respect to the parameters inside \(\mathbf f\). This is how the networks below are trained through the solver.

Code 
def rk4_step(f, s, t, h):
    k1 = f(s, t)
    k2 = f(s + 0.5 * h * k1, t + 0.5 * h)
    k3 = f(s + 0.5 * h * k2, t + 0.5 * h)
    k4 = f(s + h * k3, t + h)
    return s + h / 6.0 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)

def rk4_solve(f, s0, t0, n_steps, h, sub=1):
    # states at t0, t0 + h, ..., t0 + n_steps h; each stored interval is split into `sub` RK4 steps
    def body(s, i):
        t = t0 + i * h
        for j in range(sub):
            s = rk4_step(f, s, t + j * h / sub, h / sub)
        return s, s
    _, S = jax.lax.scan(body, s0, jnp.arange(n_steps))
    return jnp.concatenate([s0[None], S])

Energy loss per swing

The friction law leaves a fingerprint in the way the energy decays. Between two turning points, a half swing, the energy changes by \(\Delta E=\int\dot\theta\,F(\dot\theta)\,dt\). For small amplitudes \(A\) the motion inside one half swing is close to \(\theta=A\cos t\), and the integral gives three different laws for the amplitude \(A_n\) at the \(n\)-th turning point, \[\text{viscous}\quad \ln A_{n+1}-\ln A_n=-\frac{\pi\gamma}{2},\qquad \text{quadratic}\quad \frac1{A_{n+1}}-\frac1{A_n}=\frac{4c}{3},\qquad \text{dry}\quad A_n-A_{n+1}=2\mu .\] Viscous friction makes the amplitude decay exponentially, quadratic drag makes \(1/A_n\) grow linearly, so that \(A_n\) falls like \(1/n\), and dry friction removes a fixed amount of amplitude per half swing until the pendulum stops. The turning points are found as the local extrema of \(\theta(t)\) on the stored grid, refined by a parabola through the three neighbouring samples, and the energy at a turning point is \(E_n=\omega_0^2(1-\cos A_n)\). We fit the three predicted straight lines to the half swings with \(A_n<1\), where the small-amplitude formulas apply, and compare the slopes with the predictions.

Code 
def turning_points(t, th, min_amp=0.0):
    # times and amplitudes |theta| of the local extrema of theta(t), refined by a parabola through 3 samples
    t, th = np.asarray(t), np.asarray(th)
    k = np.nonzero(((th[1:-1] > th[:-2]) & (th[1:-1] >= th[2:])) | ((th[1:-1] < th[:-2]) & (th[1:-1] <= th[2:])))[0] + 1
    y0, y1, y2 = th[k - 1], th[k], th[k + 1]
    den = y0 - 2 * y1 + y2
    d = np.where(np.abs(den) > 1e-14, 0.5 * (y0 - y2) / np.where(den == 0, 1, den), 0.0)   # vertex offset in steps
    tp, amp = t[k] + d * (t[1] - t[0]), np.abs(y1 - 0.25 * (y0 - y2) * d)
    keep = amp > min_amp
    return tp[keep], amp[keep]

2. One measured swing

3. Three models

All three models are ODEs for the state \(\mathbf s=(\theta,\dot\theta)\), integrated by RK4 from a measured initial state.

The gravity-only model drops the friction, \(\ddot\theta=-\omega_0^2\sin\theta\). It has no free parameter and conserves the energy.

The neural ODE replaces the whole vector field by a network, \(\dot{\mathbf s}=\mathbf N_\phi(\mathbf s)\), with two inputs and two outputs. It knows neither gravity nor the kinematic relation between \(\theta\) and \(\dot\theta\) and must learn both from the data.

The hybrid model keeps everything we trust and learns only the friction, \[\dot\theta=\dot\theta,\qquad \ddot\theta=-\omega_0^2\sin\theta+U_\theta(\theta,\dot\theta).\] The network \(U_\theta\) receives both components of the state, so that it can in principle also represent a force that depends on the angle. The subscript \(\theta\) of \(U_\theta\) denotes the network weights, following the notation of Rackauckas et al., and is unrelated to the angle.

Both networks are multilayer perceptrons with two hidden layers of 32 tanh units, \[\mathbf h_1=\tanh(W_1\mathbf s+\mathbf b_1),\qquad \mathbf h_2=\tanh(W_2\mathbf h_1+\mathbf b_2),\qquad \text{output}=W_3\mathbf h_2+\mathbf b_3,\] with one output for \(U_\theta\) and two for \(\mathbf N_\phi\). The weights start from a normal distribution of variance \(1/n_{\rm in}\) and the biases from zero. The last layer of \(U_\theta\) is scaled by \(0.1\) at initialisation, so that the hybrid model starts close to the gravity-only model. The drawing shows how the two networks enter the vector field that the solver integrates.

def init_mlp(key, sizes, last_scale=1.0):
    # list of (W, b) for the layer widths `sizes`; W ~ N(0, 1/n_in), b = 0, last layer scaled by last_scale
    params = []
    for i, (n_in, n_out) in enumerate(zip(sizes[:-1], sizes[1:])):
        key, k = jax.random.split(key)
        W = jax.random.normal(k, (n_in, n_out)) / np.sqrt(n_in)
        params.append((W * (last_scale if i == len(sizes) - 2 else 1.0), jnp.zeros(n_out)))
    return params

def mlp(params, x):
    # tanh hidden layers, linear output layer
    for W, b in params[:-1]:
        x = jnp.tanh(x @ W + b)
    W, b = params[-1]
    return x @ W + b

4. Training through the solver

A loss on the trajectory requires integrating the model ODE and differentiating the result with respect to the network weights. Integrating from \(t=0\) over the whole window at once is possible, but a small change of the vector field shifts the phase of all later swings, which makes such a loss hard to minimise from a poor initial guess. We therefore use short segments, an approach known as multiple shooting. Each of the measured states \(\tilde{\mathbf s}_k\) with \(k=0,\dots,170\) starts a segment, which the model integrates over \(H=30\) steps of \(\Delta t\), about half a period. The loss is the mean squared deviation of the integrated states \(\hat{\mathbf s}\) from the measured ones, \[\mathcal L=\frac{1}{2\,n_{\rm seg}(H+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{n_{\rm seg}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{H}\big\|\hat{\mathbf s}(t_{k+j};\tilde{\mathbf s}_k)-\tilde{\mathbf s}_{k+j}\big\|^2,\qquad n_{\rm seg}=171,\] where \(\hat{\mathbf s}(t;\tilde{\mathbf s}_k)\) is the model solution started from \(\tilde{\mathbf s}_k\) at \(t_k\). The noise alone contributes \(\sigma^2=4\times10^{-4}\) per component, the smallest value the loss can reach on average. All segments are integrated in parallel with jax.vmap.

Before training we check the gradient of the loss. jax.grad differentiates through the RK4 steps. An independent value comes from the adaptive Tsit5 solver of diffrax [Ch. Tsitouras, Comput. Math. Appl. 62, 770 (2011), doi:10.1016/j.camwa.2011.06.002] with tolerances \(10^{-12}\), which integrates all segments as one batched state. For a random unit direction \(\mathbf v\) in weight space we compare the directional derivative \(\nabla\mathcal L\cdot\mathbf v\) from jax.grad with the central difference \([\mathcal L_{\rm Tsit5}(\mathbf w+\epsilon\mathbf v)-\mathcal L_{\rm Tsit5}(\mathbf w-\epsilon\mathbf v)]/(2\epsilon)\) with \(\epsilon=10^{-4}\), where \(\mathbf w\) denotes all weights of \(U_\theta\). The difference between them is dominated by the discretisation error of RK4, so it should shrink by about \(4^4=256\) when the RK4 step is divided by four.

def segment_loss(f_maker, data, sub=1, solver="rk4"):
    # multiple-shooting loss: every measured state starts a segment of H_SEG steps
    S0, target = jnp.asarray(data[starts]), jnp.asarray(data[idx])
    ts = jnp.arange(H_SEG + 1) * DT
    def loss(params):
        f = f_maker(params)
        if solver == "rk4":
            traj = jax.vmap(lambda s: rk4_solve(f, s, 0.0, H_SEG, DT, sub=sub))(S0)
        else:   # adaptive Tsit5 of diffrax; all segments form one batched state of shape (n_seg, 2)
            ys = diffrax_solve(lambda S, t: jax.vmap(lambda s: f(s, t))(S), S0, ts, solver=diffrax.Tsit5(), tol=1e-12)
            traj = jnp.swapaxes(ys, 0, 1)
        return jnp.mean((traj - target) ** 2)
    return loss

def train(f_maker, params, data, n_steps=N_STEPS, lr=LR0):
    loss = segment_loss(f_maker, data)
    tx = optax.adam(optax.cosine_decay_schedule(lr, n_steps, alpha=0.05))
    opt_state = tx.init(params)
    @jax.jit
    def update(params, opt_state):
        l, g = jax.value_and_grad(loss)(params)
        u, opt_state = tx.update(g, opt_state, params)
        return optax.apply_updates(params, u), opt_state, l
    hist = np.empty(n_steps)
    for i in range(n_steps):
        params, opt_state, l = update(params, opt_state)
        hist[i] = l
    return params, hist, float(loss(params))

Sparse regression

To turn \(U_\theta\) into a formula we expand its values at the measured states in a library of \(m=8\) candidate functions \(\Theta_j(\theta,\dot\theta)\), \[U_\theta(\tilde{\mathbf s}_k)\approx\sum_{j=1}^{m}\xi_j\,\Theta_j(\tilde{\mathbf s}_k),\qquad \Theta=\big\{1,\ \sin\theta,\ \theta^2,\ \theta\dot\theta,\ \dot\theta,\ \dot\theta|\dot\theta|,\ \tanh(\dot\theta/\varepsilon),\ \dot\theta^3\big\}.\] The library contains the three friction laws, a cubic velocity term and three functions that involve the angle, so the regression has to choose. Most coefficients \(\xi_j\) should vanish, and sequentially thresholded least squares (STLSQ) enforces this sparsity [Brunton, Proctor and Kutz (2016)]. Each library column is divided by its root mean square over the data, so that a coefficient \(\hat\xi_j\) of the scaled problem measures the typical size of term \(j\) in units of the friction. STLSQ first solves the regression with all columns, then sets every \(\hat\xi_j\) with \(|\hat\xi_j|<\lambda\) to zero and solves the regression again on the remaining columns. These two operations are repeated until the set of active terms no longer changes.

The four velocity functions are strongly correlated on the data, since all of them are odd in \(\dot\theta\) and grow with \(|\dot\theta|\). Ordinary least squares then tends to return large coefficients of opposite sign that cancel each other, and no single coefficient falls below the threshold. Each solve inside STLSQ therefore minimises the ridge-regularised squared error \[\sum_k\Big(U_\theta(\tilde{\mathbf s}_k)-\sum_j\hat\xi_j\hat\Theta_{kj}\Big)^2+\alpha\,n\,\|\hat{\boldsymbol\xi}\|^2,\qquad \alpha=0.01,\] with \(n=201\) data points and the scaled columns \(\hat\Theta_{kj}\). The penalty suppresses such cancelling combinations. After the active set has converged, its coefficients are refitted by plain least squares, so that the penalty does not shrink the final values. The threshold is \(\lambda=0.35\,{\rm rms}(U_\theta)\), which drops a term when its typical contribution is below 35 per cent of the typical learned friction.

LIB_NAMES = ["1", r"\sin\theta", r"\theta^2", r"\theta\dot\theta", r"\dot\theta",
             r"\dot\theta|\dot\theta|", r"\tanh(\dot\theta/\varepsilon)", r"\dot\theta^3"]
def library(S, xp=np):
    # columns Theta_j(theta, thetadot) for states S of shape (n, 2); xp = np or jnp
    th, w = S[:, 0], S[:, 1]
    return xp.stack([xp.ones_like(th), xp.sin(th), th ** 2, th * w, w, w * xp.abs(w), xp.tanh(w / EPS), w ** 3], 1)
LAW_TERM = {"viscous": r"\dot\theta", "quadratic": r"\dot\theta|\dot\theta|", "dry": r"\tanh(\dot\theta/\varepsilon)"}

ALPHA, LAM_REL = 0.01, 0.35
def stlsq(Theta, y, lam, alpha=ALPHA, max_iter=20):
    # sequentially thresholded ridge regression on RMS-scaled columns, final plain least-squares refit;
    # returns the coefficients of the unscaled columns
    n = len(y)
    scale = np.sqrt(np.mean(Theta ** 2, 0))
    A = Theta / scale
    ridge = lambda M: np.linalg.solve(M.T @ M + alpha * n * np.eye(M.shape[1]), M.T @ y)
    xi = ridge(A)
    active = np.ones(len(xi), bool)
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        new = active & (np.abs(xi) >= lam)
        xi = np.zeros_like(xi)
        if new.any():
            xi[new] = ridge(A[:, new])
        if np.array_equal(new, active):
            break
        active = new
    xi = np.zeros_like(xi)
    if active.any():
        xi[active] = np.linalg.lstsq(A[:, active], y, rcond=None)[0]
    return xi / scale

def sparse_fit(S, U):
    return stlsq(library(S), U, LAM_REL * np.sqrt(np.mean(U ** 2)))

Sparse regression on raw derivatives

The original SINDy procedure works without a network. It estimates the derivative \(\ddot\theta\) directly from the data by finite differences and regresses it on a library. Here the central difference \((\tilde{\dot\theta}_{k+1}-\tilde{\dot\theta}_{k-1})/(2\Delta t)\) amplifies the measurement noise to a standard deviation \(\sigma/(\sqrt2\,\Delta t)\approx0.14\), comparable with the friction itself. We run the same STLSQ with the same library and threshold on the target \(\ddot\theta+\omega_0^2\sin\tilde\theta\), the part of the acceleration that gravity leaves unexplained, so that both regressions estimate the same function.

7. Other friction laws

A procedure that always returned quadratic drag would be of little use. We therefore repeat the pipeline for the viscous and the dry friction, with new noisy measurements of the same length and noise level, the same training budget and the same library, threshold and ridge penalty.

The library contains the smoothed sign function with the same width \(\varepsilon\) as the simulation, which makes the dry case easier than it would be with measured data, where the width would also have to be scanned. We also test a second form of the hybrid model. Friction in a bearing or in air depends on how fast the bob moves and is independent of where it is, so we may build this into the model and let the network depend on the angular velocity alone, \[\ddot\theta=-\omega_0^2\sin\theta+U_\theta(\dot\theta).\] This network has one input and 1153 parameters. A single swing passes through every velocity between \(-\dot\theta_{\max}\) and \(\dot\theta_{\max}\) many times, so a function of \(\dot\theta\) alone is constrained over that whole interval, whereas a function of two variables is constrained only on the thin spiral of measured states. Summary

We modelled a pendulum whose friction law was hidden in one noisy swing of three periods. A pure neural ODE with 1218 parameters fits the measured swing, but once the swing leaves the region of the phase plane covered by the data its forecast degrades, with an angle error of 0.18 for \(20<t\le100\). The hybrid model keeps the known gravitational term and lets a network of 1185 parameters represent the friction inside the equation of motion. Trained through the RK4 solver with a multiple-shooting loss, it learns a friction function within 7.6 per cent of the true law on the measured states and forecasts with an error of 0.048. Sequentially thresholded ridge regression on the network output selects a single term from a library of eight candidates, the quadratic air drag, with the coefficient 0.1497 for the true 0.15. The recovered equation forecasts with an error of 0.006 and reproduces the energy decay to \(t=100\). The same pipeline selects the correct term for viscous and for dry friction, and a network that depends on the angular velocity alone, a physical assumption about friction, learns the friction more accurately than a network of the whole state. Regressing the library directly on finite-difference derivatives of the noisy data selects no term at all, since differentiation amplifies the noise above the size of the friction, although a fit of the true term alone to the same derivatives gives \(c=0.148\).

These results hold for this setting, a noise level of 0.02, one swing of three periods and a library that contains the true term. The threshold window in which the single correct term survives is narrow, less than a factor of two in \(\lambda\), because the candidate velocity functions are strongly correlated on the data. When the true law is absent from the library, the regression returns the sparse combination of library terms that best approximates it in the measured range, and nothing then guarantees that it extrapolates better than the network.
