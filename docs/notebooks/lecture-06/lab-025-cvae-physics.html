Lab 025 — Spin configurations from a conditional VAE

A conditional variational autoencoder (cVAE, Lab 024) produces new configurations of a spin model at a requested temperature \(T\), or at a requested point \((g,T)\) of a phase diagram. A generated snapshot can look like a Monte Carlo snapshot and still violate the physics fixed by the Hamiltonian. In this lab we therefore test the synthetic data in two independent ways. The first test uses networks trained on real Monte Carlo data, a regressor that reads \(T\) from a snapshot and classifiers of the phase: they tell us whether the synthetic snapshots carry the patterns that a network has learned to associate with a temperature or a phase. The second test uses the Hamiltonian itself: from the snapshots we compute the energy, the magnetisation, the specific heat, the susceptibility, the Binder cumulant, the distributions \(P(e)\) and \(P(m)\) and the correlation function, and compare them with their Monte Carlo values.

What the generator can and cannot get right follows from how it samples. Generating “at temperature \(T\)” means drawing a latent vector \(\mathbf z\) and then every spin independently from its own probability: given \(\mathbf z\) the generator is a product over sites, a mean-field-like distribution, whereas Monte Carlo runs a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is exactly the Boltzmann distribution, with all the correlations between neighbouring spins that the Hamiltonian implies. Section 2 explains this, and Section 3.6 shows the two samplers side by side. It is the reason why the order parameter will be reproduced and the energy will not.

Every test is run first on held-out real data, which fixes the baseline and the statistical noise of the estimate, and then, identically, on synthetic data at the same conditions. Before turning to the physics we make sure that the generator itself is trained properly, by looking at the training curves, the active latent units, a classifier two-sample test and possible memorisation of the training set.

We work with two models and the Monte Carlo data of Lab 018: the 2D Ising model with \(L=30\) (Section 3) and the frustrated \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) Ising model with \(L=32\) (Section 4). Before running the notebook, run Lab 018 and copy ising_L30.npz and j1j2_L32.npz into data/; everything runs on the CPU.

1. Hamiltonian, Boltzmann distribution and observables

We start by recalling what a “correct” sample of a spin model is. A classical spin model assigns an energy \(H(\mathbf s)\) to every configuration \(\mathbf s=(s_1,\dots,s_N)\), \(s_i=\pm1\), on an \(L\times L\) lattice with periodic boundaries (\(N=L^2\)): \[ \text{Ising: } H=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j,\qquad J_1\text{–}J_2:\ H=-J_1\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j+J_2\sum_{\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle}s_is_j , \] with \(\langle ij\rangle\) nearest neighbours and \(\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle\) diagonal neighbours; we set \(J=J_1=1\) and \(k_B=1\). In thermal equilibrium at temperature \(T\) (inverse temperature \(\beta=1/T\)) a configuration occurs with the Boltzmann probability \[ p(\mathbf s)=\frac{e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_{\mathbf s}e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)} . \] At low \(T\) the low-energy, ordered configurations dominate; at high \(T\) all configurations become nearly equally likely and the system is disordered.

Every observable we use is an average over this distribution: for a quantity \(O(\mathbf s)\) of a single configuration, \[ \langle O\rangle=\frac1Z\sum_{\mathbf s}O(\mathbf s)\,e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)} . \] The partition function also generates the thermodynamics: the energy per spin is the first derivative of \(\ln Z\) with respect to \(\beta\), and the specific heat per spin is the second derivative, which equals the variance of the energy, \[ \begin{aligned} \langle e\rangle&=\frac{\langle H\rangle}{N}=-\frac1N\,\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta},\\ c&=\frac{\beta^2}{N}\,\frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2}=\frac{\beta^2}{N}\big(\langle H^2\rangle-\langle H\rangle^2\big) =N\beta^2\big(\langle e^2\rangle-\langle e\rangle^2\big). \end{aligned} \] The sums run over all \(2^N\) configurations and cannot be performed for \(N=900\) or \(N=1024\). Monte Carlo instead draws \(n\) configurations \(\mathbf s^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf s^{(n)}\) with probability \(p(\mathbf s)\) at each temperature, and every average \(\langle O\rangle\) is estimated by the plain sample mean \(\overline{O}=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n O(\mathbf s^{(k)})\). A generator is judged by computing exactly the same sample means over its own configurations. 2. Conditional VAE for spins

The generator is the conditional variational autoencoder (cVAE) of Lab 024, with a spin configuration in place of an image and the temperature in place of the digit. A configuration \(\mathbf s=(s_1,\dots,s_N)\), \(s_i=\pm1\), is stored as bits \(b_i=(s_i+1)/2\in\{0,1\}\), and the condition is \(c=T\) for the Ising model and \(c=(g,T)\) for the \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) model. What we train are three pieces. The prior \(p(\mathbf z)=\mathcal N(0,I)\) over a latent vector \(\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^{d}\), \(d=16\) (later also a learned prior \(p_\psi(\mathbf z|c)\)). The decoder, a network that outputs one logit \(\ell_i(\mathbf z,c)\) per site and treats the spins as independent Bernoulli variables given \(\mathbf z\), \[ p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf z,c)=\prod_{i=1}^N \sigma(\ell_i)^{b_i}\,\big(1-\sigma(\ell_i)\big)^{1-b_i},\qquad \sigma(\ell)=\frac1{1+e^{-\ell}}, \] so that \(\sigma(\ell_i)\) is the probability that spin \(i\) points up. And the encoder \(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf s,c)=\mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu(\mathbf s,c),\mathrm{diag}\,\boldsymbol\sigma^2(\mathbf s,c)\big)\), which proposes the latent vectors that could have produced a given configuration.

Training should make the log-likelihood \(\log p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid c)\) of the Monte Carlo configurations large, but this quantity is an integral over all latent vectors and cannot be computed. We therefore maximise the evidence lower bound (ELBO), a quantity that can be computed and is never larger (“evidence” is the traditional name of \(p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid c)\); the derivation is given in Lab 024). For every training configuration \[ \log p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid c)\;\ge\;\mathbb E_{q_\phi(\mathbf z|\mathbf s,c)}\big[\log p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf z,c)\big]\;-\; \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf s,c)\,\|\,p(\mathbf z)\big)\;=\;\mathrm{ELBO}. \] The first term is minus the binary cross-entropy (BCE) between the bits and the predicted probabilities, \[ -\log p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf z,c)=\mathrm{BCE}=-\sum_{i=1}^N\Big[b_i\log\sigma(\ell_i)+(1-b_i)\log\big(1-\sigma(\ell_i)\big)\Big], \] the number of nats by which the predicted spin probabilities fail to explain the configuration: 0 if every spin is predicted with certainty, \(N\log2=624\) nats for \(N=900\) if every spin is a coin flip. The second term is the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence \(\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)=\mathbb E_q[\log q-\log p]\ge0\) between the encoder cloud and the prior; for two diagonal Gaussians it is \[ \mathrm{KL}=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(\sigma_j^2+\mu_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\big), \] zero when the cloud equals the prior and growing with the information the latent vector carries about the configuration. We minimise \(-\mathrm{ELBO}\) averaged over the data, sampling \(\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\sigma\odot\boldsymbol\epsilon\) with \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) (the reparametrisation trick).

Generating “at temperature \(T\)” uses only the prior and the decoder. We draw \(\mathbf z\) from the prior, compute the logits \(\ell_i(\mathbf z,T)\), and draw every spin independently, \(s_i=+1\) with probability \(\sigma(\ell_i)\). Alternatively, we can take the most likely configuration \(\hat s_i=\mathrm{sign}\,\ell_i\). The model distribution is \(p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid T)=\int d\mathbf z\,p(\mathbf z)\,p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf z,T)\), and it should approximate the Boltzmann distribution \(e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)}/Z\). At fixed \(\mathbf z\), however, the decoder is a product over sites, the same structure as a mean-field ansatz: \(\langle s_i\rangle_{\mathbf z}=\tanh(\ell_i/2)\) and \[ \langle s_is_j\rangle_{\mathbf z}=\tanh(\ell_i/2)\tanh(\ell_j/2)\quad (i\neq j),\qquad \mathrm{Cov}(s_i,s_j)=\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbf z}\big(\tanh(\ell_i/2),\tanh(\ell_j/2)\big). \] All correlations between spins must therefore be carried by the 16 latent variables. A global property such as the magnetisation is a single number per configuration and can be encoded in \(\mathbf z\), which is the macrostate-microstate picture of Lab 024. The energy \(H=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j\) is a sum of nearest-neighbour correlations, and every spin whose probability is not saturated near 0 or 1 is drawn independently of its neighbours and breaks its bonds at random.

Monte Carlo sampling works differently. It runs a Markov chain: starting from some configuration, it repeatedly proposes a change (a single spin flip in the Metropolis algorithm, accepted with probability \(\min(1,e^{-\beta\Delta H})\); a flip of whole clusters in the Swendsen–Wang algorithm that produced the Ising data of this lab; the \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) data of Lab 018 come from Metropolis chains cooled step by step in \(T\)) and accepts or rejects it with rules built from \(H\) itself. The stationary distribution of this chain is exactly \(e^{-\beta H}/Z\), so after equilibration every snapshot is a sample of the Boltzmann distribution, with all the correlations between neighbouring spins that \(H\) implies. The price is time: the chain must be equilibrated, and successive snapshots are correlated. The cVAE needs no \(H\) and produces an independent configuration in one forward pass, but it can only reproduce what its structure can represent. This is why the energy, the specific heat and \(G(r)\) are the sharp tests in this lab, while the order parameter is expected to be easier. Section 3.6 shows the two samplers side by side.

3.2 Observables fixed by the Hamiltonian

We now turn the observables of Section 1 into formulas for a finite set of snapshots. For one snapshot, with each nearest-neighbour bond counted once (right and down neighbour, periodic boundaries), \[ e=\frac{H}{N}=-\frac1N\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j\in[-2,2],\qquad m=\frac1N\sum_i s_i . \] For a set of snapshots at one temperature (\(k_B=J=1\)) we compute \[ \langle e\rangle,\quad \langle|m|\rangle,\quad c=\frac{N}{T^2}\big(\langle e^2\rangle-\langle e\rangle^2\big),\quad \chi=\frac{N}{T}\big(\langle m^2\rangle-\langle|m|\rangle^2\big),\quad U_4=1-\frac{\langle m^4\rangle}{3\langle m^2\rangle^2}, \] together with the correlation function along the lattice axes, \(G(r)=\langle s_{\mathbf x}\,s_{\mathbf x+r\hat{\mathbf e}}\rangle\), averaged over \(\mathbf x\), both axes and the snapshots, for \(r=0,\dots,L/2\).

The function ising_observables(S, T) takes S of shape (n_T, n, L, L) and T of shape (n_T,). It returns the per-temperature averages (shape (n_T,)), the per-snapshot \(e\) and \(m\) (shape (n_T, n)) and \(G\) (shape (n_T, L/2+1)). The cell ends with two configurations whose values we know exactly: the all-up state must give \(e=-2\), \(m=1\), and the chessboard state \(e=+2\), \(m=0\).

Code 
def ising_observables(S, T):
    '''Observables of the 2D Ising model (J = 1) for snapshots S (n_T, n, L, L) at temperatures T (n_T,).'''
    s = S.astype(np.float32)
    n_sites = s.shape[-1] * s.shape[-2]
    # each bond once: right neighbour (axis -1) and down neighbour (axis -2); np.roll implements periodic boundaries
    e = -(s * np.roll(s, 1, axis=-1) + s * np.roll(s, 1, axis=-2)).mean(axis=(-1, -2))     # (n_T, n)
    m = s.mean(axis=(-1, -2))                                                              # (n_T, n)
    T = np.asarray(T, np.float64)
    obs = {"e": e.mean(1),
           "m": np.abs(m).mean(1),
           "c": n_sites * e.var(1) / T**2,
           "chi": n_sites * ((m**2).mean(1) - np.abs(m).mean(1) ** 2) / T,
           "U4": 1.0 - (m**4).mean(1) / (3.0 * (m**2).mean(1) ** 2),
           "e_samples": e, "m_samples": m}
    # G(r): average of s_x s_{x+r} over sites, both axes and snapshots -> (n_T, L/2 + 1)
    obs["G"] = np.stack([0.5 * ((s * np.roll(s, r, -1)).mean((-1, -2, -3)) + (s * np.roll(s, r, -2)).mean((-1, -2, -3)))
                         for r in range(s.shape[-1] // 2 + 1)], axis=1)
    return obs

Averages can agree while the distributions differ, so we also need a distance between distributions. For two samples \(x_1..x_n\) and \(y_1..y_n\) of equal size, the 1D Wasserstein-1 distance is the mean absolute difference of the sorted values, \[ W_1=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\big|x_{(k)}-y_{(k)}\big| , \] which is the average distance one has to move probability mass to turn one histogram into the other, measured in units of \(e\) or \(m\).

A raw difference between a synthetic and a real estimate means little without knowing how large the difference between two real sets would be. For an observable \(O(T)\) we therefore compare the synthetic estimate with S_A and express the result in noise units, normalising by the real-vs-real difference: \[ R_O=\frac{\mathrm{mean}_T\,|O_{\rm syn}(T)-O_A(T)|}{\mathrm{mean}_T\,|O_B(T)-O_A(T)|}. \] A value \(R_O\approx 1\) means that the synthetic set is as close to S_A as an independent Monte Carlo set of the same size; \(R_O\gg1\) signals a real discrepancy. The next cell checks the \(W_1\) implementation on a shifted sample and prints the real-vs-real noise levels that enter the denominators.

def wasserstein1(x, y):
    '''1D Wasserstein-1 distance between equal-size samples along the last axis: mean |sorted(x) - sorted(y)|.'''
    assert x.shape == y.shape
    return np.abs(np.sort(x, axis=-1) - np.sort(y, axis=-1)).mean(axis=-1)

3.3 Evaluator networks trained on real simulations

The first family of tests asks what a network that has only ever seen real Monte Carlo data thinks of the synthetic snapshots. We train two small convolutional neural networks (CNNs) on S_train only. Both consist of two \(3\times3\) convolutions with periodic (CIRCULAR) padding, a \(2\times2\) max pooling between them, global average pooling (so that the network sees local patterns, not positions) and one hidden dense layer. The temperature regressor outputs an estimate \(\hat T\), is trained with the loss \((\hat T-T)^2\) and uses all 37 temperatures. The phase classifier is trained only far from the transition, on \(T\le1.8\) (ordered, label 0) and \(T\ge2.8\) (disordered, label 1), with the cross-entropy loss; its output \(p_1\) is the probability of “disordered”, and \(\max(p_0,p_1)\) is its confidence (Lecture 4). Near the transition the classifier becomes uncertain, so the minimum of the confidence as a function of \(T\) marks the transition it sees.

One generic trainer is used for every network of the lab. One epoch is a single jit-compiled jax.lax.scan over minibatches, optimised with Adam and a cosine-decaying learning rate. The data arrays are passed as arguments rather than captured as constants, and the spins stay int8 and are converted to float32 per minibatch, which keeps the memory small. On the real held-out sets the regressor reaches a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of about 0.12, and the confidence minimum lies at \(T=2.28\) for both S_A and S_B, next to \(T_c=2.269\); these are the baselines for the synthetic data.

def train_model(model, loss_fn, data, epochs, batch, lr, seed, callback=None, every=0):
    '''Minibatch training of an nnx model.
    loss_fn(model, batch_data, key) -> (scalar loss, aux vector);  data: tuple of arrays with the same leading dimension.
    Returns the per-epoch mean of aux, shape (epochs, len(aux)). callback(model, epoch) is called every `every` epochs and after the last one.'''
    n = len(data[0]); n_batches = n // batch
    schedule = optax.cosine_decay_schedule(lr, decay_steps=epochs * n_batches, alpha=0.05)
    opt = nnx.Optimizer(model, optax.adam(schedule), wrt=nnx.Param)
    graphdef, state = nnx.split((model, opt))              # pure pytree state -> usable inside jax.lax.scan
    data = tuple(jnp.asarray(a) for a in data)

    @jax.jit
    def run_epoch(state, data, key):
        k_perm, k_steps = jax.random.split(key)
        idx = jax.random.permutation(k_perm, n)[: n_batches * batch].reshape(n_batches, batch)   # (n_batches, batch)
        def step(state, inputs):
            batch_idx, k = inputs
            model, opt = nnx.merge(graphdef, state)
            (loss, aux), grads = nnx.value_and_grad(loss_fn, has_aux=True)(model, tuple(a[batch_idx] for a in data), k)
            opt.update(model, grads)
            return nnx.state((model, opt)), aux
        state, aux = jax.lax.scan(step, state, (idx, jax.random.split(k_steps, n_batches)))
        return state, aux.mean(axis=0)

    key, history = jax.random.key(seed), []
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        key, k = jax.random.split(key)
        state, aux = run_epoch(state, data, k)
        history.append(np.asarray(aux))
        if callback is not None and ((epoch + 1) % every == 0 or epoch + 1 == epochs):
            nnx.update((model, opt), state)
            callback(model, epoch + 1)
    nnx.update((model, opt), state)                        # write the trained parameters back into `model`
    return np.array(history)

class CNN(nnx.Module):
    '''(B, L, L) spins -> (B, n_out). Two 3x3 periodic convolutions, max pooling, global average pooling, dense head.'''
    def __init__(self, n_out, *, rngs):
        self.conv1 = nnx.Conv(1, 8, (3, 3), padding="CIRCULAR", rngs=rngs)
        self.conv2 = nnx.Conv(8, 16, (3, 3), padding="CIRCULAR", rngs=rngs)
        self.hidden = nnx.Linear(16, 32, rngs=rngs)
        self.out = nnx.Linear(32, n_out, rngs=rngs)
    def __call__(self, x):
        h = nnx.relu(self.conv1(x[..., None]))                           # (B, L, L, 8): add a channel axis
        h = nnx.max_pool(h, (2, 2), strides=(2, 2))                      # (B, L/2, L/2, 8)
        h = nnx.relu(self.conv2(h)).mean(axis=(1, 2))                    # (B, 16): global average pooling
        return self.out(nnx.relu(self.hidden(h)))

3.4 Conditional VAE

The generator has a dense encoder and decoder. The spins are flattened, so x has shape (B, N), and the condition c has shape (B, 1) with \(c=T-2.5\): \[ \text{encoder: } [\mathbf s, c]\;\xrightarrow{\;N+1\to512\to256\;}\;(\boldsymbol\mu,\log\boldsymbol\sigma^2)\in\mathbb R^{d}\times\mathbb R^{d},\qquad \text{decoder: } [\mathbf z, c]\;\xrightarrow{\;d+1\to256\to512\to N\;}\;\boldsymbol\ell , \] with ReLU activations and latent dimension \(d=16\). We train three variants, each of which adds one physics-motivated ingredient to the previous one.

The first variant, called cVAE, is the model of Lab 024 with the prior \(p(\mathbf z)=\mathcal N(0,\mathbb 1)\), trained on the raw data.

The second variant, cVAE+Z2, uses the symmetry of the Hamiltonian. The Ising Hamiltonian is invariant under the global flip \(\mathbf s\to-\mathbf s\), so below \(T_c\) the data consist of two mirror-image families (\(m\approx\pm|m|\)) that the generator would have to place separately in the Gaussian latent space; prior samples that fall between them decode to mixtures of the two. We impose the symmetry by construction instead: every training snapshot is replaced by its representative with \(m\ge0\), and every generated snapshot is flipped with probability 1/2, so that \(p(\mathbf s)=\tfrac12\big[p_+(\mathbf s)+p_+(-\mathbf s)\big]\) holds exactly.

The third variant, cVAE+Z2+p(z|T), adds a conditional prior \(p_\psi(\mathbf z\mid c)=\mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu_\psi(c),\mathrm{diag}\,\boldsymbol\sigma^2_\psi(c)\big)\), computed by a small network \(c\xrightarrow{1\to64\to}(\boldsymbol\mu_\psi,\log\boldsymbol\sigma_\psi^2)\) (the prior network of Sohn, Lee and Yan). The reason is that the encoder sees the configuration, and a configuration itself reveals its temperature: it is ordered or disordered, with large or small domains. The encoded snapshots of different temperatures therefore occupy different regions of \(\mathbf z\)-space. With the fixed prior \(\mathcal N(0,\mathbb 1)\), generation at temperature \(T\) draws \(\mathbf z\) from all these regions and produces a mixture of temperatures; with \(p_\psi(\mathbf z|T)\) it draws from the region that belongs to \(T\). The KL term becomes \[ \mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\|\,p_\psi\big)=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^d\Big[\log\frac{\sigma_{\psi,j}^2}{\sigma_j^2}+\frac{\sigma_j^2+(\mu_j-\mu_{\psi,j})^2}{\sigma_{\psi,j}^2}-1\Big], \] which reduces to the formula of Section 2 for \(\boldsymbol\mu_\psi=0\), \(\boldsymbol\sigma_\psi=1\). The cell below checks the Bernoulli negative log-likelihood and compares this closed-form KL with a Monte Carlo estimate (1.2077 against 1.2124).

class CVAE(nnx.Module):
    '''Dense conditional VAE with a Bernoulli decoder: x (B, N) spins +-1, c (B, n_cond).
    conditional_prior=False: p(z) = N(0, 1);  True: p(z|c) = N(mu_psi(c), sigma_psi(c)^2) from a small prior network.'''
    def __init__(self, n_sites, n_cond, latent=16, hidden=512, conditional_prior=False, *, rngs):
        self.enc1 = nnx.Linear(n_sites + n_cond, hidden, rngs=rngs)
        self.enc2 = nnx.Linear(hidden, 256, rngs=rngs)
        self.enc_mu = nnx.Linear(256, latent, rngs=rngs)
        self.enc_logvar = nnx.Linear(256, latent, rngs=rngs)
        self.dec1 = nnx.Linear(latent + n_cond, 256, rngs=rngs)
        self.dec2 = nnx.Linear(256, hidden, rngs=rngs)
        self.dec3 = nnx.Linear(hidden, n_sites, rngs=rngs)
        self.conditional_prior, self.latent = conditional_prior, latent
        if conditional_prior:
            self.pri1 = nnx.Linear(n_cond, 64, rngs=rngs)
            self.pri_mu = nnx.Linear(64, latent, rngs=rngs)
            self.pri_logvar = nnx.Linear(64, latent, rngs=rngs)
    def encode(self, x, c):
        h = nnx.relu(self.enc2(nnx.relu(self.enc1(jnp.concatenate([x, c], axis=-1)))))
        return self.enc_mu(h), self.enc_logvar(h)                          # (B, d), (B, d)
    def decode(self, z, c):
        h = nnx.relu(self.dec2(nnx.relu(self.dec1(jnp.concatenate([z, c], axis=-1)))))
        return self.dec3(h)                                                # (B, N) logits l_i
    def prior(self, c):
        '''Mean and log-variance of p(z|c), each (B, d); zeros (standard normal) without a prior network.'''
        if not self.conditional_prior:
            zeros = jnp.zeros((c.shape[0], self.latent))
            return zeros, zeros
        h = nnx.tanh(self.pri1(c))
        return self.pri_mu(h), self.pri_logvar(h)

def gaussian_kl(mu_q, logvar_q, mu_p, logvar_p):
    '''KL( N(mu_q, e^logvar_q) || N(mu_p, e^logvar_p) ) per dimension (diagonal Gaussians).'''
    return 0.5 * (logvar_p - logvar_q + (jnp.exp(logvar_q) + (mu_q - mu_p) ** 2) / jnp.exp(logvar_p) - 1.0)

def cvae_terms(model, x, c, key):
    '''Per-configuration terms of -ELBO: reconstruction -log p(s|z,c), shape (B,), and KL per latent dimension, shape (B, d).'''
    mu, logvar = model.encode(x, c)
    z = mu + jnp.exp(0.5 * logvar) * jax.random.normal(key, mu.shape)     # reparametrisation: one z per configuration
    logits = model.decode(z, c)
    bits = (x + 1.0) / 2.0                                                 # s = +-1  ->  b = 1, 0
    rec = optax.sigmoid_binary_cross_entropy(logits, bits).sum(-1)        # -sum_i [b log s(l) + (1-b) log(1-s(l))]
    kl = gaussian_kl(mu, logvar, *model.prior(c))                         # closed form, per dimension
    return rec, kl

def cvae_loss(model, batch, key):
    x, c = batch
    rec, kl = cvae_terms(model, x.astype(jnp.float32), c, key)
    loss = (rec + kl.sum(-1)).mean()
    return loss, jnp.stack([loss, rec.mean(), kl.sum(-1).mean()])

def draw_vae(enc, d, dec, cond=0, cond_name=r"$\mathbf{c}$", x_img=None, xhat_img=None, prior_net=None,
             x_name=r"\mathbf{x}", size=(5.5, 2.25), fontsize=10, gap=0.11, mode="train", region=None, compact=False,
             fig=None):
    """(Conditional) VAE drawn with nodes and edges.
    enc, dec  : widths of the encoder [784, 400, 200] and of the decoder [200, 400, 784]
    d         : latent dimension; the heads mu and log sigma^2 have d nodes each, z = mu + sigma * eps
                is element-wise (orange lines) and the noise eps is a separate input (dashed)
    cond      : number of condition inputs (0: plain VAE); the condition (green) enters the first layer
                of the encoder and of the decoder
    cond_name, x_name : symbols of the condition and of the data, e.g. "$T$" and r"\mathbf{s}" for spins
    x_img, xhat_img : optional pictures of an input and of its reconstruction at the two ends
    prior_net : hidden width of a prior network c -> (mu_psi, log sigma_psi^2), drawn in a lower row
    mode      : "train": encoder, sampling node and decoder, with the loss; "generate": only the decoder, fed by
                z ~ p(z) (and the chosen condition); the encoder is not used
    region    : (x_left, x_right) in inches: the part of the figure used by this drawing (default: all of it)
    compact   : leave out the activation names (for two drawings side by side)
    size, fontsize, gap : figure size (inches), font size (points) and node spacing (inches)
    fig       : draw into this existing figure"""
    W, H = size
    x_lo, x_hi = region if region is not None else (0.0, W)
    if compact:
        gap = min(gap, 0.1)
    yc = H - (1.33 if compact else 1.18)                       # height of the main row of columns
    ylow = yc - (0.7 if compact else 0.62)                     # row of the side inputs eps and c
    left = x_lo + (0.9 if x_img is not None else 0.3)
    right = x_hi - (0.9 if xhat_img is not None else 0.3)
    act = lambda name: "" if compact else name                 # activation names under the columns
    L, E = {}, []
    if mode == "train":
        xs = np.linspace(left, right, len(enc) + len(dec) + 2)     # columns: encoder, heads, z, decoder
        ex, hx, zx, dx = xs[:len(enc)], xs[len(enc)], xs[len(enc) + 1], xs[len(enc) + 2:]
        # ---- encoder: input column (raised if the condition sits under it) and hidden layers
        for i, (n, x) in enumerate(zip(enc, ex)):
            L[f"e{i}"] = dict(x=x, y=yc, n=n, kind="input" if i == 0 else "hidden",
                              bottom="" if i == 0 else act("ReLU"), top=rf"${x_name}$" if i == 0 else "")
            if i: E.append((f"e{i - 1}", f"e{i}", "dense"))
        # ---- the two heads, the noise and the sampled latent vector z = mu + sigma * eps
        L["mu"] = dict(x=hx, y=yc + 0.36, n=d, kind="latent", shown=3, show_n=False,
                       left=r"$\boldsymbol{\mu}$")
        L["lv"] = dict(x=hx, y=yc - 0.36, n=d, kind="latent", shown=3, show_n=False, bottom=r"$\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2$")
        L["eps"] = dict(x=zx - 0.12 if compact else (hx + zx) / 2 + 0.03, y=ylow, n=d, kind="noise", shown=3, show_n=False,
                        bottom=r"$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$")
        E += [(f"e{len(enc) - 1}", "mu", "dense"), (f"e{len(enc) - 1}", "lv", "dense"),
              ("mu", "z", "one"), ("lv", "z", "one"), ("eps", "z", "dashed")]
    else:
        xs = np.linspace(left, right, len(dec) + 2)                # columns: random source, z, decoder
        hx, zx, dx = xs[0], xs[1], xs[2:]
        L["src"] = dict(x=hx, y=yc, n=d, kind="noise", shown=3, show_n=False, bottom="random")
        E.append(("src", "z", "dashed"))
    L["z"] = dict(x=zx, y=yc, n=d, kind="latent", shown=3, top=r"$\mathbf{z}$", show_n=mode == "train")
    # ---- decoder
    for i, (n, x) in enumerate(zip(dec, dx)):

        L[f"d{i}"] = dict(x=x, y=yc, n=n, kind="output" if last else "hidden",

        E.append(("z" if i == 0 else f"d{i - 1}", f"d{i}", "dense"))

    if cond:

        cd = [("cd", zx + 0.12, "d0")] + ([("ce", x_ce, "e1")] if mode == "train" else [])

    # ---- prior network p_psi(z | c), in a lower row under the encoder

        L["pm"] = dict(x=ex[2], y=yp + 0.15, n=d, kind="noise", shown=2, show_n=False)
        L["pl"] = dict(x=ex[2], y=yp - 0.15, n=d, kind="noise", shown=2, show_n=False)
        E += [("cp", "ph", "dense"), ("ph", "pm", "dense"), ("ph", "pl", "dense")]
    if x_img is not None and mode == "train":

        E.append(("xin", "e0", "arrow"))
    fig = draw_network(L, E, size=size, fontsize=fontsize, gap=gap, fig=fig)
    ax = fig.axes[0]
    if xhat_img is not None:                                   # the reconstruction right of the output column
        ax.imshow(xhat_img, cmap="gray_r", extent=(x_hi - 0.65, x_hi - 0.05, yc - 0.3, yc + 0.3), interpolation="nearest")
        ax.annotate("", xy=(x_hi - 0.68, yc), xytext=(dx[-1] + 0.08, yc), arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", lw=0.8))
    # ---- brackets naming the parts; sampling formula, loss or note on the unused encoder
    yb = H - (0.5 if compact else 0.3)
    cnd = ", " + cond_name.strip("$") if cond else ""
    enc_lab = r"encoder $q_\phi$" if compact else rf"encoder $q_\phi(\mathbf{{z}}\,|\,{x_name}{cnd})$"
    dec_lab = r"decoder $p_\theta$" if compact else rf"decoder $p_\theta({x_name}\,|\,\mathbf{{z}}{cnd})$"
    formula = r"$\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$"
    if mode == "train":
        bracket(ax, ex[0] - 0.1, hx + 0.1, yb, enc_lab, color=NODE_COLOR["hidden"], fontsize=fontsize + 1)
        ax.text((hx + dx[0]) / 2, yb - 0.13, formula, fontsize=fontsize, color=NODE_COLOR["latent"],
                ha="center", va="center")                      # sampling formula between the two brackets
        if not compact:                                        # the loss under the formula
            ax.text((hx + dx[0]) / 2, yb - 0.3, r"loss $= \mathrm{BCE} + \mathrm{KL}$", fontsize=fontsize,
                    ha="center", va="center")
    else:

                fontsize=fontsize + 1)
        ax.text(x_lo + 0.02, 0.1, "encoder\nnot used", fontsize=fontsize, color=NODE_COLOR["input"],

                va="center", ha="left")
    return fig

Before generating anything we monitor the health of the training. During training we record the training \(-\)ELBO per epoch and, every 5 epochs, the \(-\)ELBO on the held-out real data S_A + S_B. A held-out value that rises while the training value falls signals overfitting: with 128 snapshots per temperature and \(\sim1.2\times10^6\) parameters this starts after a few tens of epochs, so we train for 40 epochs.

After training we look at the KL per latent dimension on held-out data. A dimension with \(\mathrm{KL}_j\approx0\) is ignored by the decoder (an inactive unit); if all of them are inactive, the model has collapsed to \(p(\mathbf s|c)\) without latent structure (posterior collapse). We also report the fraction of spins that the decoder reproduces from \(\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu(\mathbf s,c)\) with \(\hat s_i=\mathrm{sign}\,\ell_i\). For comparison, uniformly random spins would cost \(N\log 2=623.8\) nats per configuration; the three variants reach a held-out \(-\)ELBO between 387 and 392 nats, keep all 16 latent units active and reproduce about 80% of the spins.

def canonical(S):
    '''Z2 representative: flip every snapshot with m < 0 (the last two axes are the lattice).'''
    sign = np.where(S.sum(axis=(-1, -2), keepdims=True) >= 0, 1, -1).astype(np.int8)
    return S * sign

@nnx.jit
def cvae_eval(model, x, c, key):
    '''Held-out terms: reconstruction (B,), KL per dimension (B, d), and the fraction of spins reproduced by sign(l) at z = mu.'''
    rec, kl = cvae_terms(model, x, c, key)
    mu, _ = model.encode(x, c)
    acc = (jnp.sign(model.decode(mu, c)) == x).mean(-1)
    return rec, kl, acc

def held_out_terms(model, X, C, key, batch=4096):
    '''cvae_eval in batches over X (n, N) int8 and C (n, n_cond); returns three concatenated numpy arrays.'''
    parts = [cvae_eval(model, jnp.asarray(X[i:i + batch], jnp.float32), jnp.asarray(C[i:i + batch]), jax.random.fold_in(key, i))
             for i in range(0, len(X), batch)]
    return [np.concatenate([np.asarray(p[j]) for p in parts]) for j in range(3)]

3.5 Synthetic data at the same conditions

@nnx.jit
def decoder_logits(model, z, c):
    return model.decode(z, c)

def generate(model, cond, n_per_cond, key, side, symmetric=False, batch=8192):
    '''n_per_cond configurations at every condition row of cond (K, n_cond); z ~ p(z|c).
    Returns two int8 arrays (K, n_per_cond, side, side): Bernoulli samples and most likely configurations (same z).'''
    c_all = np.repeat(np.asarray(cond, np.float32), n_per_cond, axis=0)          # (K * n, n_cond)
    bern, ml = [], []
    for i in range(0, len(c_all), batch):
        k_z, k_s, k_f = jax.random.split(jax.random.fold_in(key, i), 3)
        c = jnp.asarray(c_all[i:i + batch])
        mu_p, logvar_p = model.prior(c)                                          # (b, d) each; zeros for N(0, 1)
        z = mu_p + jnp.exp(0.5 * logvar_p) * jax.random.normal(k_z, mu_p.shape)  # z ~ p(z|c)
        logits = decoder_logits(model, z, c)                                     # (b, side*side)
        s_b = jnp.where(jax.random.bernoulli(k_s, jax.nn.sigmoid(logits)), 1, -1)   # independent Bernoulli spins
        s_m = jnp.where(logits > 0, 1, -1)                                        # most likely configuration
        if symmetric:                                                             # global flip with probability 1/2
            flip = jnp.where(jax.random.bernoulli(k_f, 0.5, (len(c), 1)), -1, 1)
            s_b, s_m = s_b * flip, s_m * flip
        bern.append(np.asarray(s_b, np.int8)); ml.append(np.asarray(s_m, np.int8))
    shape = (len(cond), n_per_cond, side, side)
    return np.concatenate(bern).reshape(shape), np.concatenate(ml).reshape(shape)

3.6 Spin sampling and Monte Carlo

Before testing the synthetic data statistically we look at how a single synthetic configuration comes about, for the best model at two temperatures, \(T=1.8\) (ordered) and \(T=2.26\) (near \(T_c\)). Each row of the next figure starts with a Monte Carlo snapshot from S_A for comparison. Then comes the decoder output for one latent vector \(\mathbf z\sim p_\psi(\mathbf z|T)\): the probability \(\sigma(\ell_i)\) that each spin points up, as a grey level (white 0, navy 1). The next two panels are two independent Bernoulli samples from these same probabilities, and the last panel is the most likely configuration \(\mathrm{sign}\,\ell_i\).

The figure is followed by a quantitative check of the mean-field structure. For the synthetic snapshots we compare the nearest-neighbour correlation \(G(1)\) measured on the Bernoulli samples with the value predicted from the probabilities alone, assuming independent spins, \(\frac1{2N}\sum_{\mathbf x,\mu}\overline{\tanh(\ell_{\mathbf x}/2)\tanh(\ell_{\mathbf x+\hat e_\mu}/2)}\), the average over the latent vectors of the products of the mean spins. If the spins are indeed independent given \(\mathbf z\), the two agree; the Monte Carlo value of \(G(1)=-\langle e\rangle/2\) is printed next to them.

def logits_at(model, z, c):
    return model.decode(z, c)

def mean_field_G1(model, T, n, key):
    """G(1) of Bernoulli samples and its independent-spin prediction, both from the same n latent vectors at T."""
    c = jnp.asarray(cond_I(np.full(n, T)))
    mu_p, lv_p = model.prior(c)
    z = mu_p + jnp.exp(0.5 * lv_p) * jax.random.normal(key, mu_p.shape)

    nn = lambda a, b: 0.5 * ((a * np.roll(b, 1, -1)).mean() + (a * np.roll(b, 1, -2)).mean())
    return nn(s, s), nn(m, m), l

The decoder output is a smooth map of probabilities: inside a domain the probabilities are close to 0 or 1, at the domain walls and in the disordered regions they are intermediate. A Bernoulli sample turns every intermediate probability into an independent coin flip, which produces the salt-and-pepper noise inside the domains, and two samples from the same \(\mathbf z\) share the large-scale pattern but differ spin by spin. The most likely configuration removes the noise and keeps only the smooth domains. Neither looks like the Monte Carlo snapshot at short distances, where neighbouring spins are correlated through the bonds of \(H\).

The printed correlations confirm the mean-field structure: the nearest-neighbour correlation of the Bernoulli samples equals the prediction from the product of the mean spins, up to sampling noise, so the generated spins are independent given \(\mathbf z\), exactly as the decoder prescribes. Both numbers lie below the Monte Carlo \(G(1)\) at the same temperature: the generator has fewer satisfied bonds, hence a higher energy, than the Boltzmann distribution. The order parameter, by contrast, is a sum over all spins, and the independent coin flips average out in it. This is the mechanism behind every result of the following sections.

3.9 Two-sample test and memorisation

The physics tests check selected observables; a classifier two-sample test (C2ST) checks the distribution as a whole. If two sets come from the same distribution, no classifier can tell them apart better than chance. At three temperatures we train a fresh small CNN to separate real from synthetic snapshots (balanced classes, 75% for training, 25% held out) and report the held-out accuracy: 0.5 means indistinguishable, 1.0 means trivially separable. As a sanity check, real (S_train) against real (S_A+S_B) must give \(\approx0.5\).

A generator that copies its training set would pass every physics test, so we must also exclude memorisation. For every synthetic and every held-out real snapshot we compute the normalised Hamming distance to the nearest training snapshot at the same \(T\), minimised over the global flip: \[ d_{\rm NN}(\mathbf s)=\min_{\mathbf t\in\text{train}}\ \frac{1-|\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf t|/N}{2}\in[0,\tfrac12]. \] Real held-out snapshots are new draws from the Boltzmann distribution, so their \(d_{\rm NN}\) is the reference; a synthetic \(d_{\rm NN}\) much smaller than the real one would mean copying.

def c2st(X1, X2, seed, epochs=25):
    '''Held-out accuracy of a CNN trained to separate X1 (n1, L, L) from X2 (n2, L, L).
    Both classes are cut to n = min(n1, n2) and split 75 / 25 *within each class* (stratified): with a plain random split a
    class that is over-represented in training is under-represented in the test set, which biases the accuracy below 0.5.'''
    n = min(len(X1), len(X2)); n_tr = int(0.75 * n)
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    p1, p2 = r.permutation(len(X1))[:n], r.permutation(len(X2))[:n]
    X_tr = np.concatenate([X1[p1[:n_tr]], X2[p2[:n_tr]]]); y_tr = np.repeat(np.array([0, 1], np.int32), n_tr)

    model = CNN(2, rngs=nnx.Rngs(seed))

    pred = predict(model, X_te).argmax(-1)

3.10 Pattern-level and Hamiltonian-level tests

Taken together, the tests of this section draw a consistent picture. The training health is similar for the three models: the held-out \(-\)ELBO is close, all latent units are active and about 80% of the spins are reproduced from \(\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu\). The ELBO alone therefore does not tell which model generates the right physics.

The plain cVAE, asked for a fixed \(T\), produces a mixture of temperatures. Its \(\langle|m|\rangle\) is far too small below \(T_c\) and too large above, the regressor reads temperatures that depend only weakly on the requested one, and below \(T_c\) a large fraction of the snapshots are mixtures of the two ordered states. Imposing the \(\mathbb Z_2\) symmetry removes part of these mixtures, but the temperature is still not followed. Only with the conditional prior does the generator follow the temperature: \(\langle|m|\rangle\), \(\chi\), \(P(m)\) and the regressor below \(T_c\) are now close to the Monte Carlo values, within a few noise units.

The local tests nevertheless fail. The energy \(\langle e\rangle\) is too high around and above \(T_c\), by tens of noise units; the specific heat is two to fifteen times too large at the six tabulated temperatures; and \(P(e)\) is far too broad. The reason is the one anticipated in Section 2: given \(\mathbf z\) the spins are drawn independently, and every spin whose logit is not saturated breaks its bonds at random. Taking the most likely configuration \(\hat s_i=\mathrm{sign}\,\ell_i\) removes that noise and over-smooths instead: \(\langle e\rangle\) is too low, the domains are large and compact, \(G(r)\) is too large at all distances and the regressor reads temperatures far too low.

The C2ST separates every synthetic set from the Monte Carlo data, clearly so near and above \(T_c\), while real against real gives 0.5. The generator does not memorise: the nearest-neighbour distances of the best model to the training set are those of new Monte Carlo snapshots.

The lesson of the Ising model is that global, pattern-level quantities (\(\langle|m|\rangle\), the regressor, the classifier) are more forgiving than local, Hamiltonian-level ones (\(\langle e\rangle\), \(c\), \(P(e)\)). A generator that passes only the first kind of test merely looks right.

4. The \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) model

We now move to a model with a richer phase diagram, \[ H=-J_1\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j+J_2\sum_{\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle}s_is_j,\qquad g=J_2/J_1,\quad J_1=1 , \] whose low-temperature phase is a ferromagnet for \(g<1/2\) and a stripe phase for \(g>1/2\) (Lab 018). For every snapshot we compute \(|m|\), the stripe order parameter \(m_{\rm str}=\sqrt{m_{(\pi,0)}^2+m_{(0,\pi)}^2}\) with \(m_{(\pi,0)}=\frac1N\sum_{x,y}(-1)^x s_{x,y}\), and the energy per spin \(e=-\frac1{2N}\sum_i s_i h_i\) with the local field \(h_i=\sum_{\rm nn}s_j-g\sum_{\rm nnn}s_j\); per grid point we compute \(c=N\,\mathrm{Var}(e)/T^2\).

The condition is now \(c=(g,T)\), and this model lets us ask a new question: can the generator produce correct configurations at conditions it has never seen? At every grid point the 100 snapshots are split into training (60), S_A (20) and S_B (20). The cVAE is trained only at every second \(g\) and every second \(T\), a quarter of the grid that we call “seen”, and generates at every grid point, so three quarters of the conditions were never seen in training. The phase classifier, in contrast, is trained on real training snapshots at all points.

def j1j2_observables(S):
    '''Per-snapshot |m|, m_str, e and stripe orientation for S (nG, nT, n, L, L); outputs (nG, nT, n).'''
    phase_x = np.where(np.arange(LJ) % 2 == 0, 1.0, -1.0)[None, :]       # (1, L): (-1)^x along the column index
    phase_y = phase_x.T                                                   # (L, 1): (-1)^y along the row index
    out = {k: np.zeros(S.shape[:3], np.float32) for k in ["m", "m_str", "e", "orient"]}
    for a in range(S.shape[0]):
        s = S[a].astype(np.float32)                                       # (nT, n, L, L)
        nn = sum(np.roll(s, sh, ax) for sh in (1, -1) for ax in (-1, -2))                                 # 4 nearest neighbours
        nnn = sum(np.roll(np.roll(s, sy, -2), sx, -1) for sy in (1, -1) for sx in (1, -1))                # 4 diagonal neighbours
        out["e"][a] = -0.5 * (s * (nn - g_grid[a] * nnn)).mean((-1, -2))   # each bond appears twice in sum_i s_i h_i
        m_pi0, m_0pi = (s * phase_x).mean((-1, -2)), (s * phase_y).mean((-1, -2))
        out["m"][a] = np.abs(s.mean((-1, -2)))
        out["m_str"][a] = np.sqrt(m_pi0**2 + m_0pi**2)
        out["orient"][a] = np.where(np.abs(m_pi0) > np.abs(m_0pi), 1.0, -1.0)
    return out

4.1 Interpolation of the phase diagram

The phase classifier trained on real data assigns the synthetic snapshots to the same phase as the Monte Carlo snapshots at 95.4% of the grid points, compared with 99.7% for real against real, and it does so equally at seen and at unseen \((g,T)\): the generator interpolates the phase diagram. The phase boundary of the synthetic data is smeared, and near \(g=1/2\) and close to the transition lines the two maps differ. The order parameters \(\langle|m|\rangle\) and \(\langle m_{\rm str}\rangle\) are reproduced within a few noise units, and the two stripe orientations are generated with nearly equal frequency (a fraction 0.485 of the stripe snapshots alternate along \(x\), against 0.501 in the Monte Carlo data).

The energy fails as in the Ising case. In the paramagnet the synthetic \(\langle e\rangle\approx0\), the value for independent spins, while the Monte Carlo energy shows the short-range correlations of the paramagnet; the specific heat deviates from the Monte Carlo values by more than a thousand noise units (\(R_c=2279\) at seen and \(1517\) at unseen points). The most likely configurations are ordered almost everywhere, so the classifier sees ferromagnets or stripes deep in the paramagnet and agrees with S_A at only 35% of the grid points.

Summary

A cVAE trained on Monte Carlo data reproduces, within a few noise units, what the networks trained on real data look at: the phase, the order parameter and its distribution, and the position of the transition; for the \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) model it does so also at conditions \((g,T)\) it has never seen, interpolating the phase diagram. Two ingredients were needed for this, and both come from physics: the \(\mathbb Z_2\) symmetry of \(H\) imposed by construction, and a prior \(p_\psi(\mathbf z|T)\) that depends on the condition, so that \(\mathbf z\) is drawn from the region of latent space that belongs to the requested temperature.

The cVAE does not reproduce what the Hamiltonian measures locally: the energy \(\langle e\rangle\), its fluctuations (the specific heat \(c\)) and \(P(e)\). Generating at temperature \(T\) means drawing \(\mathbf z\) and then every spin independently from its probability \(\sigma(\ell_i)\); given \(\mathbf z\) the decoder is a product over sites, a mean-field-like distribution, and nearest-neighbour correlations beyond what the 16 latent variables carry are lost, while Monte Carlo, a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is exactly \(e^{-\beta H}/Z\), keeps them. A classifier two-sample test detects the difference at each of the three temperatures tested (accuracy 0.73 to 0.98, against 0.50 to 0.53 for real against real). Synthetic configurations can therefore be useful for questions about the phase, but they are not samples of \(e^{-\beta H}/Z\) and cannot replace Monte Carlo for thermodynamics.
