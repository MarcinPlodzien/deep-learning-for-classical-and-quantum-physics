Lab 025 — Spin configurations from a conditional VAE

A conditional variational autoencoder (cVAE, Lab 024) produces new configurations of a spin model at a requested temperature \(T\), or at a requested point \((g,T)\) of a phase diagram. A generated snapshot can look like a Monte Carlo snapshot and still violate the physics fixed by the Hamiltonian. In this lab we therefore test the synthetic data in two independent ways. The first test uses networks trained on real Monte Carlo data, a regressor that reads \(T\) from a snapshot and classifiers of the phase, which tell us whether the synthetic snapshots carry the patterns that a network has learned to associate with a temperature or a phase. The second test uses the Hamiltonian itself. From the snapshots we compute the energy, the magnetisation, the specific heat, the susceptibility, the Binder cumulant, the distributions \(P(e)\) and \(P(m)\) and the correlation function, and compare them with their Monte Carlo values.

What the generator can and cannot get right follows from how it samples. Generating “at temperature \(T\)” means drawing a latent vector \(\mathbf z\) and then every spin independently from its own probability. Given \(\mathbf z\) the generator is a product over sites, a mean-field-like distribution, whereas Monte Carlo runs a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is exactly the Boltzmann distribution, with all the correlations between neighbouring spins that the Hamiltonian implies. Section 2 explains this, and Section 3.6 shows the two samplers side by side. It is the reason why the order parameter will be reproduced and the energy will not.

Every test is run first on held-out real data, which fixes the baseline and the statistical noise of the estimate, and then, identically, on synthetic data at the same conditions. Before turning to the physics we make sure that the generator itself is trained properly, by looking at the training curves, the active latent units, a classifier two-sample test and possible memorisation of the training set.

We work with the Monte Carlo data of Lab 018 for two models, the 2D Ising model with \(L=30\) (Section 3) and the frustrated \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) Ising model with \(L=32\) (Section 4). Before running the notebook, run Lab 018, which generates both data sets. Everything runs on the CPU.

Sections 5 to 10 extend the tests. Section 5 relates the ELBO to the variational free energy of statistical mechanics and measures, with Onsager’s exact free energy, how far each product distribution of the decoder lies from the Boltzmann distribution. Section 6 tests the spin-flip symmetry of the generated data and the correlation length. Section 7 starts Metropolis chains from generated configurations and counts the sweeps needed to recover the Monte Carlo values. Section 8 tests the finite-size behaviour of the susceptibility and of the Binder cumulant at \(L=20\), 30 and 50. Section 9 trains the same generator on the Ising lattice gauge theory, a model without a local order parameter, and Section 10 on long-range Ising models, where the mean-field argument predicts a smaller energy failure. Section 11 collects the numbers. Sections 8 and 9 use further Monte Carlo data, the Ising snapshots at \(L=20\) and 50 of Lab 018 and the 3D gauge-theory configurations of Lab 031, and Section 10 generates its own data on the first run.

1. Hamiltonian, Boltzmann distribution and observables

We start by recalling what a “correct” sample of a spin model is. A classical spin model assigns an energy \(H(\mathbf s)\) to every configuration \(\mathbf s=(s_1,\dots,s_N)\), \(s_i=\pm1\), on an \(L\times L\) lattice with periodic boundaries (\(N=L^2\)), \[ \text{Ising: } H=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j,\qquad J_1\text{–}J_2:\ H=-J_1\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j+J_2\sum_{\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle}s_is_j , \] with \(\langle ij\rangle\) nearest neighbours and \(\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle\) diagonal neighbours. We set \(J=J_1=1\) and \(k_B=1\). In thermal equilibrium at temperature \(T\) a configuration occurs with the Boltzmann probability \[ p(\mathbf s)=\frac{e^{-H(\mathbf s)/T}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_{\mathbf s}e^{-H(\mathbf s)/T} . \] At low \(T\) the low-energy, ordered configurations dominate. At high \(T\) all configurations become nearly equally likely and the system is disordered.

Every observable we use is an average over this distribution, which for a quantity \(O(\mathbf s)\) of a single configuration reads \[ \langle O\rangle=\frac1Z\sum_{\mathbf s}O(\mathbf s)\,e^{-H(\mathbf s)/T} . \] The partition function also generates the thermodynamics. The energy per spin follows from the first derivative of \(\ln Z\) with respect to \(1/T\), and the specific heat per spin from the second derivative, which equals the variance of the energy, \[ \begin{aligned} \langle e\rangle&=\frac{\langle H\rangle}{N}=-\frac1N\,\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial(1/T)},\\ c&=\frac{1}{NT^2}\,\frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial(1/T)^2}=\frac{1}{NT^2}\big(\langle H^2\rangle-\langle H\rangle^2\big) =\frac{N}{T^2}\big(\langle e^2\rangle-\langle e\rangle^2\big). \end{aligned} \] The sums run over all \(2^N\) configurations and cannot be performed for \(N=900\) or \(N=1024\). Monte Carlo instead draws \(n\) configurations \(\mathbf s^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf s^{(n)}\) with probability \(p(\mathbf s)\) at each temperature, and every average \(\langle O\rangle\) is estimated by the plain sample mean \(\overline{O}=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n O(\mathbf s^{(k)})\). A generator is judged by computing the same sample means over its own configurations. 2. Conditional VAE for spins

The generator is the conditional variational autoencoder (cVAE) of Lab 024, with a spin configuration in place of an image and the temperature in place of the digit. A configuration \(\mathbf s=(s_1,\dots,s_N)\), \(s_i=\pm1\), is stored as bits \(b_i=(s_i+1)/2\in\{0,1\}\), and the condition is \(\mathbf c=T\) for the Ising model and \(\mathbf c=(g,T)\) for the \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) model. What we train are three pieces. The prior \(p(\mathbf z)=\mathcal N(0,I)\) over a latent vector \(\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^{d}\), \(d=16\) (later also a learned prior \(p_\psi(\mathbf z|\mathbf c)\)). The decoder, a network that outputs one logit \(\ell_i(\mathbf z,\mathbf c)\) per site and treats the spins as independent Bernoulli variables given \(\mathbf z\), \[ p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf z,\mathbf c)=\prod_{i=1}^N \mathrm{sigm}(\ell_i)^{b_i}\,\big(1-\mathrm{sigm}(\ell_i)\big)^{1-b_i},\qquad \mathrm{sigm}(\ell)=\frac1{1+e^{-\ell}}, \] so that \(\mathrm{sigm}(\ell_i)\) is the probability that spin \(i\) points up. And the encoder \(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf s,\mathbf c)=\mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu(\mathbf s,\mathbf c),\mathrm{diag}\,\boldsymbol\sigma^2(\mathbf s,\mathbf c)\big)\), which proposes the latent vectors that could have produced a given configuration.

Training should make the log-likelihood \(\log p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf c)\) of the Monte Carlo configurations large, but this quantity is an integral over all latent vectors and cannot be computed. We therefore maximise the evidence lower bound (ELBO), a quantity that can be computed and is never larger (“evidence” is the traditional name of \(p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf c)\), and Lab 024 gives the derivation). For every training configuration \[ \log p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf c)\;\ge\;\mathbb E_{q_\phi(\mathbf z|\mathbf s,\mathbf c)}\big[\log p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf z,\mathbf c)\big]\;-\; \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf s,\mathbf c)\,\|\,p(\mathbf z)\big)\;=\;\mathrm{ELBO}. \] The first term is minus the binary cross-entropy (BCE) between the bits and the predicted probabilities, \[ -\log p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf z,\mathbf c)=\mathrm{BCE}=-\sum_{i=1}^N\Big[b_i\log\mathrm{sigm}(\ell_i)+(1-b_i)\log\big(1-\mathrm{sigm}(\ell_i)\big)\Big], \] the number of nats by which the predicted spin probabilities fail to explain the configuration. It is 0 if every spin is predicted with certainty and \(N\log2=624\) nats for \(N=900\) if every spin is a coin flip. The second term is the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence \(\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)=\mathbb E_q[\log q-\log p]\ge0\) between the encoder cloud and the prior. For two diagonal Gaussians it is \[ \mathrm{KL}=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(\sigma_j^2+\mu_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\big), \] zero when the cloud equals the prior and growing with the information the latent vector carries about the configuration. We minimise \(-\mathrm{ELBO}\) averaged over the data, sampling \(\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\sigma\odot\boldsymbol\epsilon\) with \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) (the reparametrisation trick).

Generating “at temperature \(T\)” uses only the prior and the decoder. We draw \(\mathbf z\) from the prior, compute the logits \(\ell_i(\mathbf z,T)\), and draw every spin independently, \(s_i=+1\) with probability \(\mathrm{sigm}(\ell_i)\). Alternatively, we can take the most likely configuration \(\tilde s_i=\mathrm{sign}\,\ell_i\). The model distribution is \(p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid T)=\int d\mathbf z\,p(\mathbf z)\,p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf z,T)\), and it should approximate the Boltzmann distribution \(e^{-H(\mathbf s)/T}/Z\). At fixed \(\mathbf z\), however, the decoder is a product over sites, the same structure as a mean-field ansatz, with \(\langle s_i\rangle_{\mathbf z}=\tanh(\ell_i/2)\) and \[ \langle s_is_j\rangle_{\mathbf z}=\tanh(\ell_i/2)\tanh(\ell_j/2)\quad (i\neq j),\qquad \mathrm{Cov}(s_i,s_j)=\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathbf z}\big(\tanh(\ell_i/2),\tanh(\ell_j/2)\big). \] All correlations between spins must therefore be carried by the 16 latent variables. A global property such as the magnetisation is a single number per configuration and can be encoded in \(\mathbf z\), which is the macrostate-microstate picture of Lab 024. The energy \(H=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j\) is a sum of nearest-neighbour correlations, and every spin whose probability is not saturated near 0 or 1 is drawn independently of its neighbours and breaks its bonds at random.

Monte Carlo sampling works differently. It runs a Markov chain. Starting from some configuration, it repeatedly proposes a change and accepts or rejects it with rules built from \(H\) itself. In the Metropolis algorithm the change is a single spin flip, accepted with probability \(\min(1,e^{-\Delta H/T})\), and in the Swendsen–Wang algorithm, which produced the Ising data of this lab, it is a flip of whole clusters. The \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) data of Lab 018 come from Metropolis chains cooled gradually through decreasing \(T\). The stationary distribution of this chain is exactly \(e^{-H/T}/Z\), so after equilibration every snapshot is a sample of the Boltzmann distribution, with all the correlations between neighbouring spins that \(H\) implies. The price is time, because the chain must be equilibrated and successive snapshots are correlated. The cVAE needs no \(H\) and produces an independent configuration in one forward pass, but it can only reproduce what its structure can represent. This is why the energy, the specific heat and \(G(r)\) are the sharp tests in this lab, while the order parameter is expected to be easier. Section 3.6 shows the two samplers side by side.

3. The 2D Ising model

3.1 Monte Carlo data

The Ising data set holds 256 Swendsen–Wang configurations at each of 37 temperatures, \(T\in[1.5,3.5]\), sampled more densely near \(T_c\). At every temperature we split them into three sets. The 128 configurations of S_train are the training set both of the generator and of the evaluator networks. The remaining configurations form two independent held-out real sets, S_A and S_B, of 64 snapshots each. S_A is the reference for every test, while the difference between S_A and S_B measures the statistical noise of a 64-snapshot estimate, which we call the real-vs-real baseline. All arrays have the shape (n_T, n, L, L), that is (temperatures, snapshots, rows, columns).

3.2 Observables fixed by the Hamiltonian

We now turn the observables of Section 1 into formulas for a finite set of snapshots. For one snapshot, with each nearest-neighbour bond counted once (right and down neighbour, periodic boundaries), \[ e=\frac{H}{N}=-\frac1N\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j\in[-2,2],\qquad m=\frac1N\sum_i s_i . \] For a set of snapshots at one temperature (\(k_B=J=1\)) we compute \[ \langle e\rangle,\quad \langle|m|\rangle,\quad c=\frac{N}{T^2}\big(\langle e^2\rangle-\langle e\rangle^2\big),\quad \chi=\frac{N}{T}\big(\langle m^2\rangle-\langle|m|\rangle^2\big),\quad U_4=1-\frac{\langle m^4\rangle}{3\langle m^2\rangle^2}, \] together with the correlation function along the lattice axes, \(G(r)=\langle s_{\mathbf x}\,s_{\mathbf x+r\hat{\mathbf e}}\rangle\), averaged over \(\mathbf x\), both axes and the snapshots, for \(r=0,\dots,L/2\).

The function ising_observables(S, T) takes S of shape (n_T, n, L, L) and T of shape (n_T,). It returns the per-temperature averages (shape (n_T,)), the per-snapshot \(e\) and \(m\) (shape (n_T, n)) and \(G\) (shape (n_T, L/2+1)). The cell ends with two configurations whose values we know exactly. The all-up state must give \(e=-2\), \(m=1\), and the chessboard state \(e=+2\), \(m=0\).

def ising_observables(S, T):
    '''Observables of the 2D Ising model (J = 1) for snapshots S (n_T, n, L, L) at temperatures T (n_T,).'''
    s = S.astype(np.float32)
    n_sites = s.shape[-1] * s.shape[-2]
    # each bond once: right neighbour (axis -1) and down neighbour (axis -2); np.roll implements periodic boundaries
    e = -(s * np.roll(s, 1, axis=-1) + s * np.roll(s, 1, axis=-2)).mean(axis=(-1, -2))     # (n_T, n)
    m = s.mean(axis=(-1, -2))                                                              # (n_T, n)
    T = np.asarray(T, np.float64)
    obs = {"e": e.mean(1),
           "m": np.abs(m).mean(1),
           "c": n_sites * e.var(1) / T**2,
           "chi": n_sites * ((m**2).mean(1) - np.abs(m).mean(1) ** 2) / T,
           "U4": 1.0 - (m**4).mean(1) / (3.0 * (m**2).mean(1) ** 2),
           "e_samples": e, "m_samples": m}
    # G(r): average of s_x s_{x+r} over sites, both axes and snapshots -> (n_T, L/2 + 1)
    obs["G"] = np.stack([0.5 * ((s * np.roll(s, r, -1)).mean((-1, -2, -3)) + (s * np.roll(s, r, -2)).mean((-1, -2, -3)))
                         for r in range(s.shape[-1] // 2 + 1)], axis=1)
    return obs

Averages can agree while the distributions differ, so we also need a distance between distributions. For two samples \(x_1..x_n\) and \(y_1..y_n\) of equal size, the 1D Wasserstein-1 distance is the mean absolute difference of the sorted values, \[ W_1=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n\big|x_{(k)}-y_{(k)}\big| , \] which is the average distance one has to move probability mass to turn one histogram into the other, measured in units of \(e\) or \(m\).

A raw difference between a synthetic and a real estimate means little without knowing how large the difference between two real sets would be. For an observable \(O(T)\) we therefore compare the synthetic estimate with S_A and express the result in noise units, normalising by the real-vs-real difference, \[ R_O=\frac{\mathrm{mean}_T\,|O_{\rm syn}(T)-O_A(T)|}{\mathrm{mean}_T\,|O_B(T)-O_A(T)|}. \] A value \(R_O\approx 1\) means that the synthetic set is as close to S_A as an independent Monte Carlo set of the same size, while \(R_O\gg1\) signals a real discrepancy. The next cell checks the \(W_1\) implementation on a shifted sample and prints the real-vs-real noise levels that enter the denominators.

def wasserstein1(x, y):
    '''1D Wasserstein-1 distance between equal-size samples along the last axis: mean |sorted(x) - sorted(y)|.'''
    assert x.shape == y.shape
    return np.abs(np.sort(x, axis=-1) - np.sort(y, axis=-1)).mean(axis=-1)

3.3 Evaluator networks trained on real simulations

The first family of tests asks what a network that has only ever seen real Monte Carlo data thinks of the synthetic snapshots. We train two small convolutional neural networks (CNNs) on S_train only. Both consist of two \(3\times3\) convolutions with periodic (CIRCULAR) padding, a \(2\times2\) max pooling between them, global average pooling (so that the network sees only local patterns, independent of their position) and one hidden dense layer. The temperature regressor outputs an estimate \(\tilde T\), is trained with the loss \((\tilde T-T)^2\) and uses all 37 temperatures. The phase classifier is trained only far from the transition, on \(T\le1.8\) (ordered, label 0) and \(T\ge2.8\) (disordered, label 1), with the cross-entropy loss. Its output \(p_1\) is the probability of “disordered”, and \(\max(p_0,p_1)\) is its confidence. Near the transition the classifier becomes uncertain, so the minimum of the confidence as a function of \(T\) marks the transition it sees.

One generic trainer is used for every network of the lab. One epoch is a single jit-compiled jax.lax.scan over minibatches, optimised with Adam and a cosine-decaying learning rate. The data arrays are passed as arguments of the compiled function and are not captured as constants, and the spins stay int8 and are converted to float32 per minibatch, which keeps the memory small. On the real held-out sets the regressor reaches a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of about 0.12, and the confidence minimum lies at \(T=2.28\) for both S_A and S_B, next to \(T_c=2.269\). These are the baselines for the synthetic data.

def train_model(model, loss_fn, data, epochs, batch, lr, seed, callback=None, every=0):
    '''Minibatch training of an nnx model.
    loss_fn(model, batch_data, key) -> (scalar loss, aux vector);  data: tuple of arrays with the same leading dimension.
    Returns the per-epoch mean of aux, shape (epochs, len(aux)). callback(model, epoch) is called every `every` epochs and after the last one.'''
    n = len(data[0]); n_batches = n // batch
    schedule = optax.cosine_decay_schedule(lr, decay_steps=epochs * n_batches, alpha=0.05)
    opt = nnx.Optimizer(model, optax.adam(schedule), wrt=nnx.Param)
    graphdef, state = nnx.split((model, opt))              # pure pytree state -> usable inside jax.lax.scan
    data = tuple(jnp.asarray(a) for a in data)

    @jax.jit
    def run_epoch(state, data, key):
        k_perm, k_steps = jax.random.split(key)
        idx = jax.random.permutation(k_perm, n)[: n_batches * batch].reshape(n_batches, batch)   # (n_batches, batch)
        def step(state, inputs):
            batch_idx, k = inputs
            model, opt = nnx.merge(graphdef, state)
            (loss, aux), grads = nnx.value_and_grad(loss_fn, has_aux=True)(model, tuple(a[batch_idx] for a in data), k)
            opt.update(model, grads)
            return nnx.state((model, opt)), aux
        state, aux = jax.lax.scan(step, state, (idx, jax.random.split(k_steps, n_batches)))
        return state, aux.mean(axis=0)

    key, history = jax.random.key(seed), []
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        key, k = jax.random.split(key)
        state, aux = run_epoch(state, data, k)
        history.append(np.asarray(aux))
        if callback is not None and ((epoch + 1) % every == 0 or epoch + 1 == epochs):
            nnx.update((model, opt), state)
            callback(model, epoch + 1)
    nnx.update((model, opt), state)                        # write the trained parameters back into `model`
    return np.array(history)

class CNN(nnx.Module):
    '''(B, L, L) spins -> (B, n_out). Two 3x3 periodic convolutions, max pooling, global average pooling, dense head.'''
    def __init__(self, n_out, *, rngs):
        self.conv1 = nnx.Conv(1, 8, (3, 3), padding="CIRCULAR", rngs=rngs)
        self.conv2 = nnx.Conv(8, 16, (3, 3), padding="CIRCULAR", rngs=rngs)
        self.hidden = nnx.Linear(16, 32, rngs=rngs)
        self.out = nnx.Linear(32, n_out, rngs=rngs)
    def __call__(self, x):
        h = nnx.relu(self.conv1(x[..., None]))                           # (B, L, L, 8): add a channel axis
        h = nnx.max_pool(h, (2, 2), strides=(2, 2))                      # (B, L/2, L/2, 8)
        h = nnx.relu(self.conv2(h)).mean(axis=(1, 2))                    # (B, 16): global average pooling
        return self.out(nnx.relu(self.hidden(h)))

def cnn_forward(model, x):

3.4 Conditional VAE

The generator has a dense encoder and decoder. The spins are flattened, so x has shape (B, N), and the condition c has shape (B, 1) with \(\mathbf c=T-2.5\), \[ \text{encoder: } [\mathbf s, \mathbf c]\;\xrightarrow{\;N+1\to512\to256\;}\;(\boldsymbol\mu,\log\boldsymbol\sigma^2)\in\mathbb R^{d}\times\mathbb R^{d},\qquad \text{decoder: } [\mathbf z, \mathbf c]\;\xrightarrow{\;d+1\to256\to512\to N\;}\;\boldsymbol\ell , \] with ReLU activations and latent dimension \(d=16\). We train three variants, each of which adds one physics-motivated ingredient to the previous one.

The first variant, called cVAE, is the model of Lab 024 with the prior \(p(\mathbf z)=\mathcal N(0,I)\), trained on the raw data.

The second variant, cVAE+Z2, uses the symmetry of the Hamiltonian. The Ising Hamiltonian is invariant under the global flip \(\mathbf s\to-\mathbf s\), so below \(T_c\) the data consist of two mirror-image families (\(m\approx\pm|m|\)) that the generator would have to place separately in the Gaussian latent space. Prior samples that fall between them decode to mixtures of the two. We impose the symmetry by construction instead. Every training snapshot is replaced by its representative with \(m\ge0\), and every generated snapshot is flipped with probability 1/2, so that \(p(\mathbf s)=\tfrac12\big[p_+(\mathbf s)+p_+(-\mathbf s)\big]\) holds exactly.

The third variant, cVAE+Z2+p(z|T), adds a conditional prior \(p_\psi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf c)=\mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu_\psi(\mathbf c),\mathrm{diag}\,\boldsymbol\sigma^2_\psi(\mathbf c)\big)\), computed by a small network \(\mathbf c\xrightarrow{1\to64\to}(\boldsymbol\mu_\psi,\log\boldsymbol\sigma_\psi^2)\) (the prior network of Sohn, Lee and Yan). The reason is that the encoder sees the configuration, and a configuration itself reveals its temperature, because it is ordered or disordered, with large or small domains. The encoded snapshots of different temperatures therefore occupy different regions of \(\mathbf z\)-space. With the fixed prior \(\mathcal N(0,I)\), generation at temperature \(T\) draws \(\mathbf z\) from all these regions and produces a mixture of temperatures. With \(p_\psi(\mathbf z|T)\) it draws from the region that belongs to \(T\). The KL term becomes \[ \mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\|\,p_\psi\big)=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^d\Big[\log\frac{\sigma_{\psi,j}^2}{\sigma_j^2}+\frac{\sigma_j^2+(\mu_j-\mu_{\psi,j})^2}{\sigma_{\psi,j}^2}-1\Big], \] which reduces to the formula of Section 2 for \(\boldsymbol\mu_\psi=0\), \(\boldsymbol\sigma_\psi=1\). The cell below checks the Bernoulli negative log-likelihood and compares this closed-form KL with a Monte Carlo estimate (1.2077 against 1.2124).

class CVAE(nnx.Module):
    '''Dense conditional VAE with a Bernoulli decoder: x (B, N) spins +-1, c (B, n_cond).
    conditional_prior=False: p(z) = N(0, 1);  True: p(z|c) = N(mu_psi(c), sigma_psi(c)^2) from a small prior network.'''
    def __init__(self, n_sites, n_cond, latent=16, hidden=512, conditional_prior=False, *, rngs):
        self.enc1 = nnx.Linear(n_sites + n_cond, hidden, rngs=rngs)
        self.enc2 = nnx.Linear(hidden, 256, rngs=rngs)
        self.enc_mu = nnx.Linear(256, latent, rngs=rngs)
        self.enc_logvar = nnx.Linear(256, latent, rngs=rngs)
        self.dec1 = nnx.Linear(latent + n_cond, 256, rngs=rngs)
        self.dec2 = nnx.Linear(256, hidden, rngs=rngs)
        self.dec3 = nnx.Linear(hidden, n_sites, rngs=rngs)
        self.conditional_prior, self.latent = conditional_prior, latent
        if conditional_prior:
            self.pri1 = nnx.Linear(n_cond, 64, rngs=rngs)
            self.pri_mu = nnx.Linear(64, latent, rngs=rngs)
            self.pri_logvar = nnx.Linear(64, latent, rngs=rngs)
    def encode(self, x, c):
        h = nnx.relu(self.enc2(nnx.relu(self.enc1(jnp.concatenate([x, c], axis=-1)))))
        return self.enc_mu(h), self.enc_logvar(h)                          # (B, d), (B, d)
    def decode(self, z, c):
        h = nnx.relu(self.dec2(nnx.relu(self.dec1(jnp.concatenate([z, c], axis=-1)))))
        return self.dec3(h)                                                # (B, N) logits l_i
    def prior(self, c):
        '''Mean and log-variance of p(z|c), each (B, d); zeros (standard normal) without a prior network.'''
        if not self.conditional_prior:
            zeros = jnp.zeros((c.shape[0], self.latent))
            return zeros, zeros
        h = nnx.tanh(self.pri1(c))
        return self.pri_mu(h), self.pri_logvar(h)

def gaussian_kl(mu_q, logvar_q, mu_p, logvar_p):
    '''KL( N(mu_q, e^logvar_q) || N(mu_p, e^logvar_p) ) per dimension (diagonal Gaussians).'''
    return 0.5 * (logvar_p - logvar_q + (jnp.exp(logvar_q) + (mu_q - mu_p) ** 2) / jnp.exp(logvar_p) - 1.0)

def cvae_terms(model, x, c, key):
    '''Per-configuration terms of -ELBO: reconstruction -log p(s|z,c), shape (B,), and KL per latent dimension, shape (B, d).'''
    mu, logvar = model.encode(x, c)
    z = mu + jnp.exp(0.5 * logvar) * jax.random.normal(key, mu.shape)     # reparametrisation: one z per configuration
    logits = model.decode(z, c)
    bits = (x + 1.0) / 2.0                                                 # s = +-1  ->  b = 1, 0
    rec = optax.sigmoid_binary_cross_entropy(logits, bits).sum(-1)        # -sum_i [b log s(l) + (1-b) log(1-s(l))]
    kl = gaussian_kl(mu, logvar, *model.prior(c))                         # closed form, per dimension
    return rec, kl

def cvae_loss(model, batch, key):
    x, c = batch
    rec, kl = cvae_terms(model, x.astype(jnp.float32), c, key)
    loss = (rec + kl.sum(-1)).mean()
    return loss, jnp.stack([loss, rec.mean(), kl.sum(-1).mean()])

               top, bottom (optional) text written above / below the column; the true width n is
                         written below the column unless show_n=False
               left      (optional) text written left of the column
               shown     (optional) at most this many circles (keeps side columns such as mu, eps small)
               image     (optional) a 2D array drawn as a square picture instead of a column of nodes;
                         its side is given by `side` (inches)
    edges    : list of (a, b, style): lines from every drawn node of layer a to every drawn node of layer b
               ("dense": one line per weight), or from node j to node j only ("one": an element-wise
               operation, e.g. z = mu + sigma * eps), or "dashed": element-wise, dashed (random input),
               or "arrow": one arrow from the picture or column a to column b
    size     : figure size (inches); for a slide, 5.5 in wide = the full text width, so fonts are true sizes
    max_nodes, growth : a layer of width n <= max_nodes is drawn node by node; a wider layer gets
               max_nodes + growth * log2(n / max_nodes) circles and an ellipsis, so the height of a
               column grows with the width (784 nodes: 11 circles; 16 nodes: 6 circles)
    gap, r   : vertical distance between neighbouring circles and their radius (inches)
    fontsize : font size of all labels (points, as printed)
    notes    : extra texts, tuples (x, y, text, dict of matplotlib text options)
    fig      : draw into this figure (made by an earlier call) instead of a new one, e.g. two networks side by side
    Returns the figure.
    """
    if fig is None:                                            # one axes covering the figure, 1 data unit = 1 inch
        fig = plt.figure(figsize=size)
        ax = fig.add_axes([0, 0, 1, 1])
        ax.set_xlim(0, size[0]); ax.set_ylim(0, size[1]); ax.set_aspect("equal"); ax.axis("off")
    ax = fig.axes[0]
    ys = {}                                                    # heights of the drawn nodes of every column

    # ---- the columns: circles (or a picture), an ellipsis for the omitted nodes, labels above and below
    for name, L in layers.items():
        x, y0 = L["x"], L["y"]
        if "image" in L:                                       # a picture, e.g. the input digit
            s = L.get("side", 0.55)
            ax.imshow(L["image"], cmap=L.get("cmap", "gray_r"), extent=(x - s / 2, x + s / 2, y0 - s / 2, y0 + s / 2),
                      interpolation="nearest", zorder=2)
            ys[name], half = np.array([y0]), s / 2
        else:
            n = L["n"]
            k = n if n <= max_nodes else max_nodes + int(round(growth * np.log2(n / max_nodes)))
            k = min(k, L.get("shown", k))                     # optional cap for small side columns
            capped = k < n
            slots = k + 1 if capped else k                     # one slot for the ellipsis if nodes are omitted
            y = y0 + gap * ((slots - 1) / 2 - np.arange(slots))
            if capped:                                         # three dots in the middle slot
                mid = slots // 2
                ax.scatter([x] * 3, y[mid] + gap * np.array([-0.28, 0, 0.28]), s=1.2, color="k", zorder=3)
                y = np.delete(y, mid)
            for yi in y:
                ax.add_patch(Circle((x, yi), r, facecolor=NODE_COLOR[L["kind"]], edgecolor="k", lw=0.4, zorder=3))
            ys[name], half = y, gap * (slots - 1) / 2 + r
        below = "\n".join(t for t in [str(L["n"]) if L.get("show_n", True) and "n" in L else "", L.get("bottom", "")] if t)
        if below:
            ax.text(x, y0 - half - 0.03, below, ha="center", va="top", fontsize=fontsize, linespacing=1.0)
        if L.get("left"):
            ax.text(x - r - 0.05, y0, L["left"], ha="right", va="center", fontsize=fontsize)
        if L.get("top"):
            ax.text(x, y0 + half + 0.03, L["top"], ha="center", va="bottom", fontsize=fontsize, linespacing=1.0)

    # ---- the edges: drawn below the circles, thin and semi-transparent, so that they end at the circles
    for a, b, style in edges:
        xa, xb = layers[a]["x"], layers[b]["x"]
        if style == "arrow":
            ax.annotate("", xy=(xb - 0.08, layers[b]["y"]), xytext=(xa + layers[a].get("side", 0) / 2 + 0.03, layers[a]["y"]),
                        arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", lw=0.8, color="k"))
            continue
        if style == "dense":
            segs = [[(xa, p), (xb, q)] for p in ys[a] for q in ys[b]]
            kw = dict(colors="0.5", alpha=0.35, linewidths=0.3)
        else:                                                  # element-wise: node j -> node j
            segs = [[(xa, p), (xb, q)] for p, q in zip(ys[a], ys[b])]
            kw = dict(colors=NODE_COLOR["latent"], alpha=0.9, linewidths=0.6,
                      linestyles="--" if style == "dashed" else "-")
        ax.add_collection(LineCollection(segs, zorder=1, **kw))

    # ---- free texts: formulas, brackets naming encoder and decoder, ...
    for x, y, text, kw in notes:
        ax.text(x, y, text, fontsize=kw.pop("fontsize", fontsize), **kw)
    return fig


def bracket(ax, x0, x1, y, text, color="k", fontsize=10):
    """A horizontal bracket from x0 to x1 at height y (inches) with a text above it, naming a block of layers."""
    ax.plot([x0, x0, x1, x1], [y - 0.05, y, y, y - 0.05], color=color, lw=0.8)
    ax.text((x0 + x1) / 2, y + 0.03, text, ha="center", va="bottom", fontsize=fontsize, color=color)


def draw_ae(widths, d, x_img=None, xhat_img=None, size=(5.5, 1.95), fontsize=10):
    """Dense autoencoder: encoder widths [784, 256, 64] -> latent d -> mirrored decoder, with optional pictures."""
    cols = list(widths) + [d] + list(widths)[::-1]
    x0, x1 = 0.95, size[0] - 0.95                              # room for the two pictures
    xs = np.linspace(x0, x1, len(cols)); yc = 0.95
    m = len(widths)
    layers = {}
    for i, (n, x) in enumerate(zip(cols, xs)):
        kind = "input" if i == 0 else "output" if i == len(cols) - 1 else "latent" if i == m else "hidden"
        act = "" if i == 0 or i == m else "sigmoid" if i == len(cols) - 1 else "ReLU"
        top = r"$\mathbf{x}$" if i == 0 else r"$\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$" if i == len(cols) - 1 else r"$\mathbf{z}$" if i == m else ""
        layers[f"l{i}"] = dict(x=x, y=yc, n=n, kind=kind, bottom=act, top=top)
    edges = [(f"l{i}", f"l{i + 1}", "dense") for i in range(len(cols) - 1)]
    if x_img is not None:
        layers["xin"] = dict(x=0.35, y=yc, image=np.asarray(x_img).reshape(28, 28), side=0.6)
        edges += [("xin", "l0", "arrow")]
    fig = draw_network(layers, edges, size=size, fontsize=fontsize, gap=0.11)
    ax = fig.axes[0]
    if xhat_img is not None:                                   # the reconstruction right of the output column
        ax.imshow(np.asarray(xhat_img).reshape(28, 28), cmap="gray_r", extent=(size[0] - 0.65, size[0] - 0.05, yc - 0.3, yc + 0.3))
        ax.annotate("", xy=(size[0] - 0.68, yc), xytext=(xs[-1] + 0.08, yc), arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", lw=0.8))
    ytop = size[1] - 0.22
    bracket(ax, xs[0] - 0.1, xs[m] - 0.05, ytop, r"encoder $E$", color=NODE_COLOR["hidden"], fontsize=fontsize + 1)
    bracket(ax, xs[m] + 0.05, xs[-1] + 0.1, ytop, r"decoder $G$", color=NODE_COLOR["output"], fontsize=fontsize + 1)
    return fig


def draw_vae(enc, d, dec, cond=0, cond_name=r"$\mathbf{c}$", x_img=None, xhat_img=None, prior_net=None,
             x_name=r"\mathbf{x}", size=(5.5, 2.25), fontsize=10, gap=0.11, mode="train", region=None, compact=False,
             fig=None):
    """(Conditional) VAE drawn with nodes and edges.
    enc, dec  : widths of the encoder [784, 400, 200] and of the decoder [200, 400, 784]
    d         : latent dimension; the heads mu and log sigma^2 have d nodes each, z = mu + sigma * eps
                is element-wise (orange lines) and the noise eps is a separate input (dashed)
    cond      : number of condition inputs (0: plain VAE); the condition (green) enters the first layer
                of the encoder and of the decoder
    cond_name, x_name : symbols of the condition and of the data, e.g. "$T$" and r"\mathbf{s}" for spins
    x_img, xhat_img : optional pictures of an input and of its reconstruction at the two ends
    prior_net : hidden width of a prior network c -> (mu_psi, log sigma_psi^2), drawn in a lower row
    mode      : "train": encoder, sampling node and decoder, with the loss; "generate": only the decoder, fed by
                z ~ p(z) (and the chosen condition); the encoder is not used
    region    : (x_left, x_right) in inches: the part of the figure used by this drawing (default: all of it)
    compact   : leave out the activation names (for two drawings side by side)
    size, fontsize, gap : figure size (inches), font size (points) and node spacing (inches)
    fig       : draw into this existing figure"""
    W, H = size
    x_lo, x_hi = region if region is not None else (0.0, W)
    if compact:
        gap = min(gap, 0.1)
    yc = H - (1.33 if compact else 1.18)                       # height of the main row of columns
    ylow = yc - (0.7 if compact else 0.62)                     # row of the side inputs eps and c
    left = x_lo + (0.9 if x_img is not None else 0.3)
    right = x_hi - (0.9 if xhat_img is not None else 0.3)
    act = lambda name: "" if compact else name                 # activation names under the columns
    L, E = {}, []
    if mode == "train":
        xs = np.linspace(left, right, len(enc) + len(dec) + 2)     # columns: encoder, heads, z, decoder
        ex, hx, zx, dx = xs[:len(enc)], xs[len(enc)], xs[len(enc) + 1], xs[len(enc) + 2:]
        # ---- encoder: input column (raised if the condition sits under it) and hidden layers
        for i, (n, x) in enumerate(zip(enc, ex)):
            L[f"e{i}"] = dict(x=x, y=yc, n=n, kind="input" if i == 0 else "hidden",
                              bottom="" if i == 0 else act("ReLU"), top=rf"${x_name}$" if i == 0 else "")
            if i: E.append((f"e{i - 1}", f"e{i}", "dense"))
        # ---- the two heads, the noise and the sampled latent vector z = mu + sigma * eps
        L["mu"] = dict(x=hx, y=yc + 0.36, n=d, kind="latent", shown=3, show_n=False,
                       left=r"$\boldsymbol{\mu}$")
        L["lv"] = dict(x=hx, y=yc - 0.36, n=d, kind="latent", shown=3, show_n=False, bottom=r"$\log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2$")
        L["eps"] = dict(x=zx - 0.12 if compact else (hx + zx) / 2 + 0.03, y=ylow, n=d, kind="noise", shown=3, show_n=False,
                        bottom=r"$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$")
        E += [(f"e{len(enc) - 1}", "mu", "dense"), (f"e{len(enc) - 1}", "lv", "dense"),
              ("mu", "z", "one"), ("lv", "z", "one"), ("eps", "z", "dashed")]
    else:
        xs = np.linspace(left, right, len(dec) + 2)                # columns: random source, z, decoder
        hx, zx, dx = xs[0], xs[1], xs[2:]
        L["src"] = dict(x=hx, y=yc, n=d, kind="noise", shown=3, show_n=False, bottom="random")
        E.append(("src", "z", "dashed"))
    L["z"] = dict(x=zx, y=yc, n=d, kind="latent", shown=3, top=r"$\mathbf{z}$", show_n=mode == "train")
    # ---- decoder
    for i, (n, x) in enumerate(zip(dec, dx)):
        last = i == len(dec) - 1
        L[f"d{i}"] = dict(x=x, y=yc, n=n, kind="output" if last else "hidden",
                          bottom=act("sigmoid") if last else act("ReLU"),
                          top=(rf"$\tilde{{{x_name}}}$" if mode == "train" else rf"new ${x_name}$") if last else "")
        E.append(("z" if i == 0 else f"d{i - 1}", f"d{i}", "dense"))
    # ---- the condition, an input of the encoder (training only) and of the decoder
    if cond:
        x_ce = (ex[0] + ex[1]) / 2 if mode == "train" else 0     # between the input and the first hidden layer
        cd = [("cd", zx + 0.12, "d0")] + ([("ce", x_ce, "e1")] if mode == "train" else [])
        for key, x, to in cd:
            L[key] = dict(x=x, y=ylow - (0.05 if key == "ce" else 0), n=cond, kind="cond", shown=3,
                          show_n=cond > 1 and not compact, **{"top" if key == "ce" else "left": cond_name})
            E.append((key, to, "dense"))
    # ---- prior network p_psi(z | c), in a lower row under the encoder
    if prior_net and mode == "train":
        yp = 0.3
        L["cp"] = dict(x=ex[0], y=yp, n=cond, kind="cond", show_n=False, left=cond_name)
        L["ph"] = dict(x=ex[1], y=yp, n=prior_net, kind="hidden", shown=3, show_n=False, bottom=f"{prior_net}, tanh")
        L["pm"] = dict(x=ex[2], y=yp + 0.15, n=d, kind="noise", shown=2, show_n=False)
        L["pl"] = dict(x=ex[2], y=yp - 0.15, n=d, kind="noise", shown=2, show_n=False)
        E += [("cp", "ph", "dense"), ("ph", "pm", "dense"), ("ph", "pl", "dense")]
    if x_img is not None and mode == "train":
        L["xin"] = dict(x=x_lo + 0.35, y=L["e0"]["y"], image=x_img, side=0.6)
        E.append(("xin", "e0", "arrow"))
    fig = draw_network(L, E, size=size, fontsize=fontsize, gap=gap, fig=fig)
    ax = fig.axes[0]
    if xhat_img is not None:                                   # the reconstruction right of the output column
        ax.imshow(xhat_img, cmap="gray_r", extent=(x_hi - 0.65, x_hi - 0.05, yc - 0.3, yc + 0.3), interpolation="nearest")
        ax.annotate("", xy=(x_hi - 0.68, yc), xytext=(dx[-1] + 0.08, yc), arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", lw=0.8))
    # ---- brackets naming the parts; sampling formula, loss or note on the unused encoder
    yb = H - (0.5 if compact else 0.3)
    cnd = ", " + cond_name.strip("$") if cond else ""
    enc_lab = r"encoder $q_\phi$" if compact else rf"encoder $q_\phi(\mathbf{{z}}\,|\,{x_name}{cnd})$"
    dec_lab = r"decoder $p_\theta$" if compact else rf"decoder $p_\theta({x_name}\,|\,\mathbf{{z}}{cnd})$"
    formula = r"$\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\sigma}\odot\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$"
    if mode == "train":
        bracket(ax, ex[0] - 0.1, hx + 0.1, yb, enc_lab, color=NODE_COLOR["hidden"], fontsize=fontsize + 1)
        ax.text((hx + dx[0]) / 2, yb - 0.17, formula, fontsize=fontsize, color=NODE_COLOR["latent"],
                ha="center", va="center")                      # sampling formula between the two brackets
        if not compact:                                        # the loss above the brackets, clear of the mu column
            ax.text((hx + dx[0]) / 2, H + 0.02, r"loss $= \mathrm{BCE} + \mathrm{KL}$", fontsize=fontsize,
                    ha="center", va="bottom")
    else:
        bracket(ax, hx - 0.15, zx + 0.1, yb, r"prior $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$", color=NODE_COLOR["latent"],
                fontsize=fontsize + 1)
        ax.text(x_lo + 0.02, 0.1, "encoder\nnot used", fontsize=fontsize, color=NODE_COLOR["input"],
                ha="left", va="bottom", style="italic", linespacing=1.0)
    bracket(ax, dx[0] - 0.1, dx[-1] + 0.1, yb, dec_lab, color=NODE_COLOR["output"], fontsize=fontsize + 1)
    if prior_net and mode == "train":
        ax.text(ex[2] + 0.12, 0.3, r"$\boldsymbol{\mu}_\psi, \log\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2_\psi$: prior network $p_\psi(\mathbf{z}\,|\,"
                + cond_name.strip("$") + r")$," + "\n" + r"compared with $q_\phi$ in the KL term", fontsize=fontsize,
                va="center", ha="left")
    return fig


def draw_vae_pair(enc, d, dec, cond=0, size=(5.5, 2.4), split=3.45, fontsize=10):
    """Training architecture (left) and generation architecture (right) of a (conditional) VAE in one figure.
    split: the x position (inches) where the generation drawing starts."""
    fig = draw_vae(enc, d, dec, cond=cond, mode="train", region=(0.0, split), compact=True, size=size, fontsize=fontsize)
    draw_vae(enc, d, dec, cond=cond, mode="generate", region=(split + 0.05, size[0]), compact=True, size=size,
             fontsize=fontsize, fig=fig)
    ax = fig.axes[0]
    ax.plot([split, split], [0.05, size[1] - 0.05], color="0.6", lw=0.8, ls=":")
    ax.text(split / 2, size[1] - 0.02, r"training: loss $= \mathrm{BCE} + \mathrm{KL}$", ha="center", va="top",
            fontsize=fontsize + 1, weight="bold")
    ax.text((split + size[0]) / 2, size[1] - 0.02, "generation", ha="center", va="top", fontsize=fontsize + 1, weight="bold")
    return fig

Before generating anything we monitor the health of the training. During training we record the training \(-\)ELBO per epoch and, every 5 epochs, the \(-\)ELBO on the held-out real data S_A + S_B. A held-out value that rises while the training value falls signals overfitting. With 128 snapshots per temperature and \(\sim1.2\times10^6\) parameters this starts after a few tens of epochs, so we train for 40 epochs.

After training we look at the KL per latent dimension on held-out data. A dimension with \(\mathrm{KL}_j\approx0\) is ignored by the decoder (an inactive unit). If all of them are inactive, the model has collapsed to \(p(\mathbf s|\mathbf c)\) without latent structure (posterior collapse). We also report the fraction of spins that the decoder reproduces from \(\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu(\mathbf s,\mathbf c)\) with \(\tilde s_i=\mathrm{sign}\,\ell_i\). For comparison, uniformly random spins would cost \(N\log 2=623.8\) nats per configuration. The three variants reach a held-out \(-\)ELBO between 387 and 392 nats, keep all 16 latent units active and reproduce about 80% of the spins.

def canonical(S):
    '''Z2 representative: flip every snapshot with m < 0 (the last two axes are the lattice).'''

@nnx.jit
def decoder_logits(model, z, c):
    return model.decode(z, c)

def generate(model, cond, n_per_cond, key, side, symmetric=False, batch=8192):
    '''n_per_cond configurations at every condition row of cond (K, n_cond); z ~ p(z|c).
    Returns two int8 arrays (K, n_per_cond, side, side): Bernoulli samples and most likely configurations (same z).'''
    c_all = np.repeat(np.asarray(cond, np.float32), n_per_cond, axis=0)          # (K * n, n_cond)
    bern, ml = [], []
    for i in range(0, len(c_all), batch):
        k_z, k_s, k_f = jax.random.split(jax.random.fold_in(key, i), 3)
        c = jnp.asarray(c_all[i:i + batch])
        mu_p, logvar_p = model.prior(c)                                          # (b, d) each; zeros for N(0, 1)
        z = mu_p + jnp.exp(0.5 * logvar_p) * jax.random.normal(k_z, mu_p.shape)  # z ~ p(z|c)
        logits = decoder_logits(model, z, c)                                     # (b, side*side)
        s_b = jnp.where(jax.random.bernoulli(k_s, jax.nn.sigmoid(logits)), 1, -1)   # independent Bernoulli spins
        s_m = jnp.where(logits > 0, 1, -1)                                        # most likely configuration
        if symmetric:                                                             # global flip with probability 1/2
            flip = jnp.where(jax.random.bernoulli(k_f, 0.5, (len(c), 1)), -1, 1)
            s_b, s_m = s_b * flip, s_m * flip
        bern.append(np.asarray(s_b, np.int8)); ml.append(np.asarray(s_m, np.int8))
    shape = (len(cond), n_per_cond, side, side)
    return np.concatenate(bern).reshape(shape), np.concatenate(ml).reshape(shape)

The figure is followed by a quantitative check of the mean-field structure. For the synthetic snapshots we compare the nearest-neighbour correlation \(G(1)\) measured on the Bernoulli samples with the value predicted from the probabilities alone, assuming independent spins, \(\frac1{2N}\sum_{\mathbf x,\mu}\overline{\tanh(\ell_{\mathbf x}/2)\tanh(\ell_{\mathbf x+\hat e_\mu}/2)}\), the average over the latent vectors of the products of the mean spins. def mean_field_G1(model, T, n, key):
    """G(1) of Bernoulli samples and its independent-spin prediction, both from the same n latent vectors at T."""
    c = jnp.asarray(cond_I(np.full(n, T)))
    mu_p, lv_p = model.prior(c)
    z = mu_p + jnp.exp(0.5 * lv_p) * jax.random.normal(key, mu_p.shape)
    l = np.asarray(logits_at(model, z, c)).reshape(n, L, L)

    s = np.where(np.random.default_rng(5).random(l.shape) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-l)), 1.0, -1.0)
    nn = lambda a, b: 0.5 * ((a * np.roll(b, 1, -1)).mean() + (a * np.roll(b, 1, -2)).mean())
    return nn(s, s), nn(m, m), l

The decoder output is a smooth map of probabilities. Inside a domain the probabilities are close to 0 or 1, and at the domain walls and in the disordered regions they are intermediate. A Bernoulli sample turns every intermediate probability into an independent coin flip, which produces the salt-and-pepper noise inside the domains, and two samples from the same \(\mathbf z\) share the large-scale pattern but differ spin by spin. The most likely configuration removes the noise and keeps only the smooth domains. Neither looks like the Monte Carlo snapshot at short distances, where neighbouring spins are correlated through the bonds of \(H\).

The printed correlations confirm the mean-field structure. The nearest-neighbour correlation of the Bernoulli samples equals the prediction from the product of the mean spins, up to sampling noise, so the generated spins are independent given \(\mathbf z\), as the decoder prescribes. Both numbers lie below the Monte Carlo \(G(1)\) at the same temperature, so the generator has fewer satisfied bonds, hence a higher energy, than the Boltzmann distribution. The order parameter, by contrast, is a sum over all spins, and the independent coin flips average out in it. This is the mechanism behind every result of the following sections.

3.9 Two-sample test and memorisation

The physics tests check selected observables, while a classifier two-sample test (C2ST) checks the distribution as a whole. If two sets come from the same distribution, no classifier can tell them apart better than chance. At three temperatures we train a fresh small CNN to separate real from synthetic snapshots (balanced classes, 75% for training, 25% held out) and report the held-out accuracy, where 0.5 means indistinguishable and 1.0 means trivially separable. As a sanity check, real (S_train) against real (S_A+S_B) must give \(\approx0.5\).

A generator that copies its training set would pass every physics test, so we must also exclude memorisation. For every synthetic and every held-out real snapshot we compute the normalised Hamming distance to the nearest training snapshot at the same \(T\), minimised over the global flip, \[ d_{\rm NN}(\mathbf s)=\min_{\mathbf t\in\text{train}}\ \frac{1-|\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf t|/N}{2}\in[0,\tfrac12]. \] Real held-out snapshots are new draws from the Boltzmann distribution, so their \(d_{\rm NN}\) is the reference, and a synthetic \(d_{\rm NN}\) much smaller than the real one would mean copying.

def c2st(X1, X2, seed, epochs=25):
    '''Held-out accuracy of a CNN trained to separate X1 (n1, L, L) from X2 (n2, L, L).
    Both classes are cut to n = min(n1, n2) and split 75 / 25 *within each class* (stratified): with a plain random split a
    class that is over-represented in training is under-represented in the test set, which biases the accuracy below 0.5.'''
    n = min(len(X1), len(X2)); n_tr = int(0.75 * n)
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    p1, p2 = r.permutation(len(X1))[:n], r.permutation(len(X2))[:n]
    X_tr = np.concatenate([X1[p1[:n_tr]], X2[p2[:n_tr]]]); y_tr = np.repeat(np.array([0, 1], np.int32), n_tr)
    X_te = np.concatenate([X1[p1[n_tr:]], X2[p2[n_tr:]]]); y_te = np.repeat(np.array([0, 1], np.int32), n - n_tr)
    model = CNN(2, rngs=nnx.Rngs(seed))
    train_model(model, xent_loss, (X_tr, y_tr), epochs=epochs, batch=32, lr=3e-3, seed=seed)
    pred = predict(model, X_te).argmax(-1)
    return float((pred == y_te).mean()), len(y_te)

3.10 Pattern-level and Hamiltonian-level tests

Taken together, the tests of this section draw a consistent picture. The training health is similar for the three models. The held-out \(-\)ELBO is close, all latent units are active and about 80% of the spins are reproduced from \(\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu\). The ELBO alone therefore does not tell which model generates the right physics.

The plain cVAE, asked for a fixed \(T\), produces a mixture of temperatures. Its \(\langle|m|\rangle\) is far too small below \(T_c\) and too large above, the regressor reads temperatures that depend only weakly on the requested one, and below \(T_c\) a large fraction of the snapshots are mixtures of the two ordered states. Imposing the \(\mathbb Z_2\) symmetry removes part of these mixtures, but the temperature is still not followed. Only with the conditional prior does the generator follow the temperature, and \(\langle|m|\rangle\), \(\chi\), \(P(m)\) and the regressor below \(T_c\) are now close to the Monte Carlo values, within a few noise units.

The local tests nevertheless fail. The energy \(\langle e\rangle\) is too high around and above \(T_c\), by tens of noise units. The specific heat is two to fifteen times too large at the six tabulated temperatures, and \(P(e)\) is far too broad. The reason is the one anticipated in Section 2. Given \(\mathbf z\) the spins are drawn independently, and every spin whose logit is not saturated breaks its bonds at random. Taking the most likely configuration \(\tilde s_i=\mathrm{sign}\,\ell_i\) removes that noise and over-smooths instead. Then \(\langle e\rangle\) is too low, the domains are large and compact, \(G(r)\) is too large at all distances and the regressor reads temperatures far too low.

The C2ST separates every synthetic set from the Monte Carlo data, clearly so near and above \(T_c\), while real against real gives 0.5. The generator does not memorise, since the nearest-neighbour distances of the best model to the training set are those of new Monte Carlo snapshots.

The lesson of the Ising model is that global, pattern-level quantities (\(\langle|m|\rangle\), the regressor, the classifier) are more forgiving than local, Hamiltonian-level ones (\(\langle e\rangle\), \(c\), \(P(e)\)). A generator that passes only the first kind of test merely looks right.

4. The \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) model

We now move to a model with a richer phase diagram, \[ H=-J_1\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j+J_2\sum_{\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle}s_is_j,\qquad g=J_2/J_1,\quad J_1=1 , \] whose low-temperature phase is a ferromagnet for \(g<1/2\) and a stripe phase for \(g>1/2\) (Lab 018). For every snapshot we compute \(|m|\), the stripe order parameter \(m_{\rm str}=\sqrt{m_x^2+m_y^2}\) with \(m_x=\frac1N\sum_{x,y}(-1)^x s_{x,y}\) and \(m_y\) defined with \((-1)^y\), and the energy per spin \(e=-\frac1{2N}\sum_i s_i h_i\) with the local field \(h_i=\sum_{\rm nn}s_j-g\sum_{\rm nnn}s_j\). Per grid point we compute \(c=N\,\mathrm{Var}(e)/T^2\).

The condition is now \(\mathbf c=(g,T)\), and this model tests whether the generator produces correct configurations at conditions it has never seen. At every grid point the 100 snapshots are split into training (60), S_A (20) and S_B (20). The cVAE is trained only at every second \(g\) and every second \(T\), a quarter of the grid that we call “seen”, and generates at every grid point, so three quarters of the conditions were never seen in training. The phase classifier, in contrast, is trained on real training snapshots at all points.

The function j1j2_observables(S) takes S of shape (nG, nT, n, L, L) and returns per-snapshot arrays of shape (nG, nT, n) holding \(|m|\), \(m_{\rm str}\), \(e\) and the stripe orientation, which is \(+1\) if \(|m_x|>|m_y|\) (the spins alternate along \(x\)) and \(-1\) otherwise. The loop runs over \(g\) to keep the memory small. The cell first checks the energy formula on a ferromagnet and on a perfect stripe state at \(g=0.75\), where the exact energies are \(-0.5\) and \(-1.5\).

def j1j2_observables(S):
    '''Per-snapshot |m|, m_str, e and stripe orientation for S (nG, nT, n, L, L); outputs (nG, nT, n).'''
    phase_x = np.where(np.arange(LJ) % 2 == 0, 1.0, -1.0)[None, :]       # (1, L): (-1)^x along the column index
    phase_y = phase_x.T                                                   # (L, 1): (-1)^y along the row index
    out = {k: np.zeros(S.shape[:3], np.float32) for k in ["m", "m_str", "e", "orient"]}
    for a in range(S.shape[0]):
        s = S[a].astype(np.float32)                                       # (nT, n, L, L)
        nn = sum(np.roll(s, sh, ax) for sh in (1, -1) for ax in (-1, -2))                                 # 4 nearest neighbours
        nnn = sum(np.roll(np.roll(s, sy, -2), sx, -1) for sy in (1, -1) for sx in (1, -1))                # 4 diagonal neighbours
        out["e"][a] = -0.5 * (s * (nn - g_grid[a] * nnn)).mean((-1, -2))   # each bond appears twice in sum_i s_i h_i
        m_pi0, m_0pi = (s * phase_x).mean((-1, -2)), (s * phase_y).mean((-1, -2))
        out["m"][a] = np.abs(s.mean((-1, -2)))
        out["m_str"][a] = np.sqrt(m_pi0**2 + m_0pi**2)
        out["orient"][a] = np.where(np.abs(m_pi0) > np.abs(m_0pi), 1.0, -1.0)
    return out

def j1j2_maps(o):

5. ELBO and the variational free energy

The tests of Sections 3 and 4 sort the physical properties into two groups. The order parameter, its distribution and the phase survive generation, while the energy, its fluctuations and the short-range correlations do not. The variational principle of statistical mechanics explains this division. Let \(q(\mathbf s)\) be any normalised distribution over configurations and \(p(\mathbf s)=e^{-H(\mathbf s)/T}/Z\) the Boltzmann distribution. Inserting \(\ln p=-H/T-\ln Z\) into the Kullback–Leibler divergence gives \[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)&=\sum_{\mathbf s}q(\mathbf s)\ln\frac{q(\mathbf s)}{p(\mathbf s)}=\langle H\rangle_q/T-S[q]+\ln Z\;\ge\;0,\\ S[q]&=-\sum_{\mathbf s}q(\mathbf s)\ln q(\mathbf s). \end{aligned} \] With the free energy \(F=-T\ln Z\) this is the Gibbs–Bogoliubov inequality [Isihara 1968], \[ F\;\le\;F_q\equiv\langle H\rangle_q-T\,S[q],\qquad F_q-F=T\,\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p). \] Every trial distribution has a variational free energy \(F_q\) above the true free energy, and the excess is \(T\) times its Kullback–Leibler distance from the Boltzmann distribution.

The ELBO of Section 2 is the same inequality written in the latent space of a single configuration. For fixed \(\mathbf s\) and \(\mathbf c\) the cVAE defines an energy of the latent vector, \(E_\theta(\mathbf z)=-\ln p_\theta(\mathbf s,\mathbf z\mid\mathbf c)\), at unit temperature, whose partition function is \(\int d\mathbf z\,e^{-E_\theta(\mathbf z)}=p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf c)\). The encoder \(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf s,\mathbf c)\) is the trial distribution, and the inequality reads \[ -\ln p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf c)\;\le\;\mathbb E_{q_\phi}\big[E_\theta(\mathbf z)\big]-S[q_\phi]=-\mathrm{ELBO}, \] with the gap \(\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf s,\mathbf c)\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf s,\mathbf c)\big)\). The two inequalities differ in which distribution plays the role of the target. Training lowers \(-\mathrm{ELBO}\) averaged over Monte Carlo configurations and thereby lowers \(-\ln p_\theta(\mathbf s\mid\mathbf c)\) averaged over the data, which equals \(\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,p_\theta)\) up to the entropy of the data. In this divergence the data distribution stands first and the model second. The Hamiltonian never enters, and training does not lower the variational free energy \(F_q\) of the model. Generators trained by minimising \(F_q\) itself, with the Hamiltonian in the loss, also exist [Wu, Wang, Zhang 2019]. The cVAE of this lab learns from data only.

Given \(\mathbf z\) the decoder is a product distribution \(q_{\mathbf z}(\mathbf s)=\prod_iq_i(s_i)\) with \(\langle s_i\rangle=m_i=\tanh(\ell_i/2)\) and \(p_i=\mathrm{sigm}(\ell_i)\) the probability of \(s_i=+1\). For such a distribution both terms of \(F_q\) are exact sums over sites and bonds, \[ \langle H\rangle_{q_{\mathbf z}}=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}m_im_j,\qquad S[q_{\mathbf z}]=\sum_ih(p_i),\qquad h(p)=-p\ln p-(1-p)\ln(1-p). \] Mean-field theory uses the same family of product distributions and chooses the \(m_i\) that minimise \(F_q\). The uniform choice \(m_i=m\) gives \(f(m)=-2m^2-T\,h\big(\tfrac{1+m}2\big)\) per spin, whose minimum obeys the Curie–Weiss equation \(m=\tanh(4m/T)\) with a transition at \(T_c^{\rm MF}=4\). In every product distribution \(\langle s_is_j\rangle=m_im_j\) for \(i\ne j\), so neighbouring spins have no connected correlation. The energy then contains only the part of the nearest-neighbour correlation carried by the mean spins, while the order parameter, a sum of the \(m_i\), is represented fully.

For the 2D Ising model the true free energy is known. Onsager’s result for the infinite lattice, \[ -\frac fT=\ln2+\frac1{8\pi^2}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\ln\Big[\cosh^2\frac2T-\sinh\frac2T\,(\cos\theta_1+\cos\theta_2)\Big]\,d\theta_1\,d\theta_2 , \] with \(f\) the free energy per spin, lets us evaluate the gap \(\mathrm{KL}(q_{\mathbf z}\|p)/N=(F_{q_{\mathbf z}}/N-f)/T\) in nats per spin for every latent vector. The cell below does this for 256 vectors \(\mathbf z\sim p_\psi(\mathbf z\mid T)\) of the best Ising model at each temperature and compares the result with the uniform mean-field distribution. We use the infinite-lattice \(f\) for \(L=30\). Its finite-size correction decreases as \(1/L^2\) at \(T_c\) and exponentially away from it, and the cell asserts that every gap is positive, as the inequality requires.

def onsager_f(T, n=512):
    '''Free energy per spin of the infinite 2D Ising model (J = k_B = 1) from Onsager's double integral, midpoint rule on an n x n grid.
    The integrand is even in both angles, so the mean over [0, pi]^2 equals the mean over [0, 2 pi]^2.'''
    th = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) * np.pi / n
    cos_sum = np.cos(th)[:, None] + np.cos(th)[None, :]
    T = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(T, np.float64))
    mean_log = np.array([np.log(np.cosh(2 / t) ** 2 - np.sinh(2 / t) * cos_sum).mean() for t in T])
    return -T * (np.log(2.0) + 0.5 * mean_log)

def binary_entropy(p):
    '''Entropy (nats) of a spin that points up with probability p.'''
    p = np.clip(p, 1e-12, 1 - 1e-12)
    return -(p * np.log(p) + (1 - p) * np.log(1 - p))

def product_free_energy(model, T, n, key):
    '''For n latent vectors z ~ p(z|T): energy <H>_q / N, entropy S[q] / N and F_q / N of the decoder's product distribution q_z.'''
    c = jnp.asarray(cond_I(np.full(n, T)))
    mu_p, lv_p = model.prior(c)
    z = mu_p + jnp.exp(0.5 * lv_p) * jax.random.normal(key, mu_p.shape)
    l = np.asarray(logits_at(model, z, c), np.float64).reshape(n, L, L)
    m = np.tanh(l / 2)                                                        # <s_i> under q_z
    e_q = -(m * np.roll(m, 1, -1) + m * np.roll(m, 1, -2)).mean((-1, -2))    # independent spins: <s_i s_j> = m_i m_j
    s_q = binary_entropy(1 / (1 + np.exp(-l))).mean((-1, -2))
    return e_q, s_q, e_q - T * s_q

def curie_weiss_f(T):
    '''Best uniform product distribution: minimum over m of f(m) = -2 m^2 - T s(m); returns f_min and the minimising m.'''
    m_grid = np.linspace(0, 1 - 1e-9, 20001)
    f = -2 * m_grid[None, :] ** 2 - np.asarray(T)[:, None] * binary_entropy((1 + m_grid[None, :]) / 2)
    return f.min(1), m_grid[f.argmin(1)]

6. Spin-flip symmetry and correlation length

The Hamiltonian is invariant under the global flip \(\mathbf s\to-\mathbf s\), so the Boltzmann distribution satisfies \(P(m)=P(-m)\) at every temperature, and on a finite lattice without a field both ordered states occur equally often. The two variants with the imposed symmetry flip every generated snapshot with probability 1/2, and for them this test checks the implementation. The plain cVAE has to learn the symmetry from the data. At every temperature we count the fraction \(n_{m>0}/n\) of the \(n=64\) snapshots with \(m>0\) (a snapshot with \(m=0\) counts one half) and express its deviation from 1/2 in binomial standard errors, \[ z=\frac{n_{m>0}/n-\tfrac12}{\sqrt{1/(4n)}} . \] Under exact symmetry \(z\) is approximately a standard normal variable, so the sum of \(z^2\) over the temperatures below \(T_c\) follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with one degree of freedom per temperature, and the cell reports the corresponding p-value. We also report \(W_1\) between \(P(m)\) and \(P(-m)\), which for 64 snapshots is nonzero even for a symmetric distribution, so the Monte Carlo value is the reference.

The correlation function \(G(r)\) is invariant under the flip and carries the second property of this section. Section 3.8 compared only its long-distance value \(G(L/2)\). Above \(T_c\) the correlations decay exponentially, \(G(r)\simeq A\,e^{-r/\xi}\), with the correlation length \(\xi\) and an amplitude \(A\). We fit \(\ln G(r)\) on \(r=1,\dots,4\) at every temperature \(T\ge2.4\), where \(\xi\) is much smaller than \(L\).

7. Monte Carlo started from generated configurations

A generated configuration carries the right order parameter and the wrong bonds. Local Monte Carlo updates repair bonds within a few sweeps, while the slow modes of a Metropolis chain are the large-scale ones, the growth of domains and the relaxation of \(|m|\), which near \(T_c\) become slower with increasing lattice size (critical slowing down, Lab 018). The generator can then serve as a starting point for Monte Carlo. We test this with the Metropolis algorithm [Metropolis et al. 1953] in the checkerboard form of Lab 018. A flip of spin \(i\) changes the energy by \(\Delta H=2s_ih_i\), with \(h_i\) the sum of its four neighbours, and is accepted with probability \(\min(1,e^{-\Delta H/T})\). The code writes the inverse temperature as beta. The four sublattices of sites with fixed parities of \(x\) and \(y\) contain no nearest neighbours, so all spins of one sublattice are updated at once. One sweep updates every spin once.

At \(T=1.8\), \(T=2.26\) and \(T=3.0\) we run 64 chains from each of three starts. Generated starts are 64 Bernoulli samples of the best model at that temperature, hot starts are independent random spins (infinite temperature), and cold starts have all spins up (zero temperature). After \(0,1,2,4,\dots,2048\) sweeps we measure the chain averages of \(e\), \(|m|\) and \(G(r)\) and compare them with the mean over the 128 held-out Monte Carlo snapshots. An observable counts as repaired when its chain average agrees with the Monte Carlo mean within \(3\sigma\sqrt{1/64+1/128}\), where \(\sigma\) is its standard deviation over the Monte Carlo snapshots, at the first checkpoint and at every later one, and for \(G(r)\) this must hold for all \(r=1,\dots,15\) at once. The resolution is a factor of 2 in the number of sweeps.

The repaired configurations are Metropolis samples of \(e^{-H/T}/Z\). Their correctness comes from the Markov chain, and the generator contributes only the starting point and with it the number of sweeps needed.

from functools import partial
from jax import lax

def nn_field(s):
    '''Sum of the four nearest neighbours, periodic; s: (..., L, L).'''
    return jnp.roll(s, 1, -2) + jnp.roll(s, -1, -2) + jnp.roll(s, 1, -1) + jnp.roll(s, -1, -1)

def metropolis_sweep(s, key, beta):
    '''One checkerboard Metropolis sweep of a single L x L lattice (four sub-lattices updated in turn).'''
    for a, b in ((0, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (1, 1)):
        key, sub = jax.random.split(key)
        si = s[a::2, b::2]
        dE = 2.0 * si * nn_field(s)[a::2, b::2]
        accept = jax.random.uniform(sub, si.shape) < jnp.exp(-beta * dE)
        s = s.at[a::2, b::2].set(jnp.where(accept, -si, si))
    return s, key

def chain_observables(s):
    '''e, |m| and G(r), r = 0..L/2, of one lattice.'''
    e = -jnp.mean(s * jnp.roll(s, 1, -1) + s * jnp.roll(s, 1, -2))
    G = jnp.stack([0.5 * (jnp.mean(s * jnp.roll(s, r, -1)) + jnp.mean(s * jnp.roll(s, r, -2))) for r in range(s.shape[-1] // 2 + 1)])
    return e, jnp.abs(jnp.mean(s)), G

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("checkpoints",))
def relax(s0, keys, betas, checkpoints):
    '''Metropolis chains from s0 (chains, L, L) at inverse temperatures betas (chains,); observables after each number of sweeps in checkpoints.'''
    def one_chain(s, key, beta):
        out, done = [chain_observables(s)], 0
        for t in checkpoints[1:]:
            (s, key), _ = lax.scan(lambda c, _: (metropolis_sweep(c[0], c[1], beta), None), (s, key), None, length=t - done)
            out.append(chain_observables(s)); done = t
        return [jnp.stack([o[k] for o in out]) for k in range(3)]
    return jax.vmap(one_chain)(s0, keys, betas)

8. Finite-size behaviour

On a finite lattice the transition is rounded. The susceptibility has a maximum at a temperature \(T_c(L)\) that approaches \(T_c\) as \(L\) grows, and its height grows as \(L^{\gamma/\nu}\), with the exact 2D Ising exponents \(\gamma=7/4\) and \(\nu=1\). The Binder cumulant \(U_4\) depends at \(T_c\) only on the shape of \(P(m)\), which there is the same for all large \(L\), so the curves \(U_4(T)\) of different sizes cross close to \(T_c\) [Binder 1981]. A generator reproduces these trends only if it captures how the fluctuations of \(m\) depend on \(L\).

We train the best design, cVAE+Z2+p(z|T), at \(L=20\) and \(L=50\) on the Swendsen–Wang data of Lab 018 at the same 37 temperatures, with the same split into 128 training and 128 held-out snapshots per temperature, and reuse the \(L=30\) model. For each size we generate 128 snapshots per temperature and compare \(\chi\) and \(U_4\) with the 128 held-out Monte Carlo snapshots. The position of the susceptibility maximum comes from a parabola through the largest value and its two neighbours. The crossing of two \(U_4\) curves comes from cubic fits of \(U_4(T)\) on \(2.1\le T\le2.5\) and is the sign change of their difference closest to \(T_c\). Uncertainties are standard deviations over 200 bootstrap resamples of the snapshots.

9. Ising lattice gauge theory

We now apply the same generator design to a model without a local order parameter, the Ising lattice gauge theory of Lab 031 [Wegner 1971]. Ising variables \(\sigma_\mu(x)=\pm1\) live on the links of a periodic cubic lattice with \(L=6\), which has \(3L^3=648\) links. The plaquette variable is the product of the four links around an elementary square, \[ U_p=\sigma_\mu(x)\,\sigma_\nu(x+\hat e_\mu)\,\sigma_\mu(x+\hat e_\nu)\,\sigma_\nu(x),\qquad H=-\sum_pU_p,\qquad \beta_{\rm g}=1/T , \] with \(N_p=3L^3\) plaquettes and the coupling \(\beta_{\rm g}\), written beta in the code and \(\beta\) on the figure axes. The gauge transformation \(\sigma_\mu(x)\to\tau(x)\,\sigma_\mu(x)\,\tau(x+\hat e_\mu)\) with an arbitrary \(\tau(x)=\pm1\) on every site leaves every plaquette and hence \(H\) unchanged. Elitzur’s theorem states that every quantity that is not gauge invariant has zero thermal average, in particular \(\langle\sigma_l\rangle=0\) for every link at every temperature [Elitzur 1975]. Physical statements can be made only with gauge-invariant quantities. We use the plaquette average \(\langle U_p\rangle=-\langle H\rangle/N_p\), which is minus the energy per plaquette, the specific heat \(c=\beta_{\rm g}^2N_p\big(\langle\bar U^2\rangle-\langle\bar U\rangle^2\big)\) with \(\bar U\) the plaquette average of one configuration, and the Wilson loops \(W(R,R)=\big\langle\prod_{l\in C}\sigma_l\big\rangle\) around \(R\times R\) squares \(C\), averaged over positions and planes.

The 3D model has a confinement–deconfinement transition at \(\beta_{\rm g,c}=0.761413\), obtained in Lab 031 from Wegner’s duality to the 3D Ising model. In the confined phase (\(\beta_{\rm g}<\beta_{\rm g,c}\)) Wilson loops decay with the area, \(W\sim e^{-\sigma_{\rm s}R^2}\), and in the deconfined phase (\(\beta_{\rm g}>\beta_{\rm g,c}\)) with the perimeter, \(W\sim e^{-4\mu R}\). The Creutz ratio \(\chi_{\rm C}(R,R)=-\ln\big[W(R,R)\,W(R-1,R-1)/W(R,R-1)^2\big]\) [Creutz 1980] removes the perimeter term and estimates the string tension \(\sigma_{\rm s}\), so it is positive in the confined phase and close to zero in the deconfined phase.

The data are the heat-bath configurations of Lab 031 at 29 couplings \(\beta_{\rm g}\in[0.3,1.2]\), 1024 per coupling from 16 independent chains, each stored after an independent random gauge transformation. We split them by chain, 8 chains (512 configurations) for training and 4 chains each (256 configurations) for U_A and U_B. In these data every link is \(+1\) or \(-1\) with probability 1/2 at every \(\beta_{\rm g}\), and the physics sits in products of four or more links. We therefore train two variants of the conditional-prior cVAE, with condition \(\mathbf c=2(\beta_{\rm g}-0.75)\). The first sees the raw links. The second sees gauge-fixed links, the gauge-theory counterpart of the \(\mathbb Z_2\) representative of Section 3.4. A maximal tree is a set of \(L^3-1=215\) links that touches every site and contains no closed loop, and a gauge transformation can set all of them to \(+1\). We use the tree of links \(\sigma_0(x)\) with \(x_0<L-1\), links \(\sigma_1\) on the plane \(x_0=0\) with \(x_1<L-1\), and links \(\sigma_2\) on the line \(x_0=x_1=0\) with \(x_2<L-1\), the 3D version of the tree gauge of Lab 031. Every gauge orbit contains exactly one tree-fixed configuration, and all gauge-invariant quantities are unchanged. The cell checks both statements on data.

def tree_gauge(U):
    '''Maximal-tree gauge fixing of 3D links U (..., 3, L, L, L): sets sigma_0(x) = 1 for x_0 < L-1, then sigma_1 = 1 on the plane
    x_0 = 0 for x_1 < L-1, then sigma_2 = 1 on the line x_0 = x_1 = 0 for x_2 < L-1 (L^3 - 1 tree links). Plaquettes are unchanged.'''
    U = U.astype(np.int8).copy()
    def transform(U, g):                                                # sigma'_mu(x) = g(x) sigma_mu(x) g(x + e_mu)
        for mu in range(3):
            U[..., mu, :, :, :] = g * U[..., mu, :, :, :] * np.roll(g, -1, axis=g.ndim - 3 + mu)
        return U
    def exclusive_cumprod(a, axis):                                     # g_k = prod_{j<k} a_j along axis, g_0 = 1
        c = np.cumprod(a, axis=axis, dtype=np.int8)
        return np.concatenate([np.ones_like(np.take(c, [0], axis=axis)), np.take(c, range(c.shape[axis] - 1), axis=axis)], axis=axis)
    U = transform(U, exclusive_cumprod(U[..., 0, :, :, :], -3))
    g2 = exclusive_cumprod(U[..., 1, :1, :, :], -2)                     # depends on (x_1, x_2), the same for every x_0

    g3 = exclusive_cumprod(U[..., 2, :1, :1, :], -1)                    # depends on x_2 only
    return transform(U, np.broadcast_to(g3, U[..., 2, :, :, :].shape))

def shift3(a, mu, n=1):

def wilson_table(U, Rmax=3):
    '''W(R,T) for R, T = 1..Rmax of one configuration (3, L, L, L): R x T loop products averaged over positions and the six ordered
    planes (R along mu, T along nu). W(1,1) is the mean plaquette.'''
    Uf = U.astype(jnp.float32)
    def loop(R, T):
        vals = []
        for mu in range(3):
            for nu in range(3):
                if mu != nu:
                    a, c = line_product(Uf[mu], mu, R), line_product(Uf[nu], nu, T)
                    vals.append(jnp.mean(a * shift3(c, mu, R) * shift3(a, nu, T) * c))   # bottom, right, top, left sides
        return jnp.mean(jnp.stack(vals))
    return jnp.stack([jnp.stack([loop(R, T) for T in range(1, Rmax + 1)]) for R in range(1, Rmax + 1)])
wilson_batch = jax.jit(jax.vmap(wilson_table))

def creutz(W, R):
    '''Creutz ratio chi(R,R) = -ln[W(R,R) W(R-1,R-1) / W(R,R-1)^2] from mean loops W (..., Rmax, Rmax), W(0,T) = W(R,0) = 1.'''
    Wp = np.pad(W, [(0, 0)] * (W.ndim - 2) + [(1, 0), (1, 0)], constant_values=1.0)
    with np.errstate(invalid="ignore", divide="ignore"):
        return -np.log(Wp[..., R, R] * Wp[..., R - 1, R - 1] / Wp[..., R, R - 1] ** 2)

10. Long-range Ising model

The argument of Section 5 locates the energy failure in the connected correlations between interacting spins, which a product distribution sets to zero. If every spin interacts with many others, its local field is an average over many spins, its fluctuations are small, and the energy is close to its value for independent spins with the same mean spins. The energy failure should then shrink as the interaction becomes longer-ranged. We test this with the long-range Ising model on a periodic \(L\times L\) lattice, \[ H=-\frac12\sum_{i\neq j}J_{ij}\,s_is_j,\qquad J_{ij}=\frac{J_0}{r_{ij}^{\alpha}},\qquad e=\frac HN , \] where \(r_{ij}\) is the minimal-image distance and \(J_0\) is chosen so that \(\sum_jJ_{ij}=4\) for every \(\alpha\), as for nearest neighbours. Then the ground state has \(e=-2\) and the mean-field transition lies at \(T=4\) for every \(\alpha\), and the limit \(\alpha\to\infty\) leaves only the four nearest neighbours.

In \(d=2\) dimensions the renormalisation-group analysis of Fisher, Ma and Nickel [1972] gives mean-field critical exponents for \(\alpha<3d/2=3\), and exponents that depend on \(\alpha\) above this threshold. Sak [1973] showed that the exponents take their short-range values for \(\alpha>d+2-\eta_{\rm SR}\), where \(\eta_{\rm SR}=1/4\) is the anomalous-dimension exponent of the 2D Ising model, that is \(\alpha>3.75\). We take \(\alpha=2.5\) (mean-field regime), \(\alpha=3.5\) (intermediate regime), \(\alpha=6\) (short-range regime) and \(\alpha=\infty\) (nearest neighbours).

All spins interact, so the checkerboard decomposition of Section 7 does not apply. We sweep the sites in order and propose a flip of each, accepted with probability \(\min(1,e^{-\Delta H/T})\) with \(\Delta H=2s_ih_i\) and the full local field \(h_i=\sum_jJ_{ij}s_j\). After an accepted flip of \(s_i\) every local field changes, \(h_j\to h_j-2s_iJ_{ji}\) with the old value of \(s_i\), so a sweep costs \(O(N^2)\) operations and we use \(L=16\) (\(N=256\)). All chains of all couplings and temperatures run in parallel as one batch. The cell checks the coupling normalisation and compares the sampler with exact enumeration of all \(2^{16}\) states of a \(4\times4\) lattice.

def lr_couplings(alpha, Ll=LR_L):
    '''J_ij = J_0 / r_ij^alpha on the periodic L x L lattice (minimal-image distance), normalised to sum_j J_ij = 4; shape (N, N).'''
    x = np.arange(Ll)
    dx = np.abs(x[:, None] - x[None, :]); dx = np.minimum(dx, Ll - dx)                 # (L, L) minimal-image separations
    r = np.sqrt(dx[:, None, :, None] ** 2 + dx[None, :, None, :] ** 2).reshape(Ll * Ll, Ll * Ll)   # site index = x * L + y
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
        J = np.where(r > 0, (r == 1).astype(float) if np.isinf(alpha) else r ** (-float(alpha)), 0.0)
    return 4.0 * J / J.sum(1, keepdims=True)

J_LR = np.stack([lr_couplings(a) for a in LR_ALPHAS]).astype(np.float32)          # (n_alpha, N, N)

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("n_equil", "n_samples", "n_between"))
def lr_chains(s0, keys, a_idx, betas, J, n_equil, n_samples, n_between):
    '''Sequential single-spin Metropolis with the full local field h_i = sum_j J_ij s_j for C chains at once.
    s0: (C, N), a_idx: (C,) which coupling matrix, betas: (C,). Returns int8 samples (C, n_samples, N).'''
    C, n_sites = s0.shape
    def sweep(carry, key):
        s, h = carry
        u = jax.random.uniform(key, (n_sites, C))
        def flip(i, sh):
            s, h = sh
            dE = 2.0 * s[:, i] * h[:, i]
            ds = jnp.where(u[i] < jnp.exp(-betas * dE), -2.0 * s[:, i], 0.0)          # change of s_i: -2 s_i if accepted, else 0
            return s.at[:, i].add(ds), h + ds[:, None] * J[a_idx, :, i]               # update every local field
        return lax.fori_loop(0, n_sites, flip, (s, h)), None
    h0 = jnp.einsum("cij,cj->ci", J[a_idx], s0)
    carry, _ = lax.scan(sweep, (s0, h0), jax.random.split(keys[0], n_equil))
    def store(carry, key):
        carry, _ = lax.scan(sweep, carry, jax.random.split(key, n_between))
        return carry, carry[0].astype(jnp.int8)
    return lax.scan(store, carry, jax.random.split(keys[1], n_samples))[1].transpose(1, 0, 2)

def lr_energy(S, a, J=None):
    '''Energy per spin e = -(1/2N) sum_ij J_ij s_i s_j for snapshots S (..., N); J = J_LR[a] unless given.'''
    J = J_LR[a] if J is None else J
    s = S.astype(np.float32)
    return -0.5 * np.einsum("...i,ij,...j->...", s, J, s) / s.shape[-1]

Summary

A cVAE trained on Monte Carlo data reproduces, within a few noise units, what the networks trained on real data look at, namely the phase, the order parameter and its distribution, and the position of the transition. For the \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) model it does so also at conditions \((g,T)\) it has never seen, interpolating the phase diagram. This required two ingredients that both come from physics, the \(\mathbb Z_2\) symmetry of \(H\) imposed by construction and a prior \(p_\psi(\mathbf z|T)\) that depends on the condition, so that \(\mathbf z\) is drawn from the region of latent space that belongs to the requested temperature.

The cVAE does not reproduce the local quantities measured by the Hamiltonian, the energy \(\langle e\rangle\), its fluctuations (the specific heat \(c\)) and \(P(e)\). Generating at temperature \(T\) means drawing \(\mathbf z\) and then every spin independently from its probability \(\mathrm{sigm}(\ell_i)\). Given \(\mathbf z\) the decoder is a product over sites, a mean-field-like distribution, and nearest-neighbour correlations beyond what the 16 latent variables carry are lost, while Monte Carlo, a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is exactly \(e^{-H/T}/Z\), keeps them. A classifier two-sample test detects the difference at each of the three temperatures tested (accuracy 0.73 to 0.98, against 0.50 to 0.53 for real against real). Synthetic configurations can therefore be useful for questions about the phase, but they are not samples of \(e^{-H/T}/Z\) and cannot replace Monte Carlo for thermodynamics.

The extended tests sharpen this picture. The ELBO is a variational free energy in latent space, and the decoder’s product distributions, evaluated against Onsager’s exact free energy, lie up to 0.127 nats per spin above the Boltzmann distribution, further than the uniform mean-field distribution at every temperature. The spin-flip symmetry is preserved, learned by the plain cVAE and imposed in the other variants, while above \(T_c\) the correlation length is about four to six times too large and the short-range amplitude of \(G(r)\) about three times too small. The growth of the susceptibility peak with lattice size survives, and the crossing of the Binder cumulants of different sizes does not, because the generator misses the tails of \(P(m)\). For the Ising lattice gauge theory the generator learns nothing from raw, gauge-randomised links. After maximal-tree gauge fixing it reproduces the deconfined phase and the perimeter law, and misses the plaquette energy, the specific heat and the string tension near and below \(\beta_{\rm g,c}\). In long-range Ising models the energy failure shrinks as the interaction range grows, from 18.9 noise units for nearest neighbours to 2.7 for \(J_{ij}\propto r_{ij}^{-2.5}\), as the mean-field argument predicts.

Synthetic configurations are useful as starting points of Monte Carlo chains. Metropolis chains started from them reach the Monte Carlo energy, magnetisation and correlation function within 2 sweeps below and near \(T_c\) on the \(30\times30\) lattice, where a hot start needs 128 to 512 sweeps and a cold start needs 16 sweeps near \(T_c\). The configurations obtained in this way are Monte Carlo samples, and the generator has supplied only the starting point.
