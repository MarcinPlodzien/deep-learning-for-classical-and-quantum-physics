Lab 031 — Ising lattice gauge theory

In Labs 018–020 every phase had an order parameter: a local quantity, such as the magnetisation \(m=\frac1N\sum_i s_i\) or the stripe order parameter \(m_{\rm str}\), which is non-zero in one phase and zero in the other. Knowing it, a physicist plots it against temperature and sees the transition; not knowing it, an unsupervised algorithm can still find the boundary because the raw snapshots of the two phases look different.

There are systems where this picture breaks down completely. In 1971 Wegner introduced Ising models with spins on the links of a lattice and interactions around elementary squares, and showed that they have phase transitions without local order parameters (the phrase is part of the title of his paper, Duality in generalized Ising models and phase transitions without local order parameters, F. J. Wegner, J. Math. Phys. 12, 2259 (1971)). The simplest of them is the Ising (or \(\mathbb Z_2\)) lattice gauge theory, the simplest gauge theory: a theory whose description contains redundant variables that can be changed locally without changing anything physical. The same mathematical structure, with the two-element group \(\mathbb Z_2=\{+1,-1\}\) replaced by continuous groups, underlies the lattice formulation of quantum chromodynamics (QCD, the theory of quarks and gluons) [Wilson 1974; Kogut 1979].

The model also has a place in the history of machine learning in physics. Carrasquilla and Melko (Nature Physics 2017) trained neural networks to tell low- from high-temperature configurations of several spin models. For the 2D Ising lattice gauge theory (\(16\times16\times2\) link variables, ground-state against infinite-temperature configurations) a fully connected network trained on raw configurations did not exceed chance-level test accuracy (50%), while a convolutional neural network (CNN) with \(2\times2\) filters reached 100%, and further tests showed that it relied on the local plaquette constraints of the model. To understand that result we need the physics of a system in which nothing local can be measured, and this notebook develops it.

We begin in Section 2 with the model itself: spins on links, plaquette variables, the energy, and a Monte Carlo sampler in JAX. Section 3 introduces the gauge symmetry, under which flipping all links at one site changes nothing physical, and derives its consequence, Elitzur’s theorem: every link has zero average. Section 4 solves the two-dimensional model exactly. The plaquettes turn out to be independent, \(\langle U_p\rangle=\tanh\beta J\), Wilson loops obey an area law and there is no phase transition at \(T>0\); on the torus the ground states fall into topological sectors. In three dimensions, the subject of Section 5, a genuine confinement–deconfinement transition appears. Wegner’s duality to the 3D Ising model locates it exactly, and Monte Carlo simulations of plaquettes, their fluctuations, Wilson loops and Creutz ratios show it in the data. Section 6 draws the consequences of the gauge redundancy for data analysis: raw links carry no linear signal at all, while gauge-invariant features do. Along the way we save two Monte Carlo data sets for the anomaly-detection study later in Lecture 5.

Every derivation is followed by a symbolic check in SymPy (a Python library for symbolic mathematics) and a numerical check (exact enumeration or Monte Carlo).

The notebook runs in Google Colab: run the cells in order. JAX, SymPy, NumPy and Matplotlib are pre-installed in Colab, and the notebook runs on the CPU. The Monte Carlo data sets are saved once and loaded on later runs; generating them takes several minutes (the generating cells print their running times).

1. Order without a local order parameter

The standard description of phases, due to Landau, rests on symmetry breaking. The Ising Hamiltonian \(H=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j\) does not change under the global flip \(s_i\to-s_i\) of all spins. Below \(T_c\) the system picks one of the two ordered states, and the magnetisation \(m\), a quantity that changes sign under the symmetry, becomes non-zero. One local measurement (a spin, or the average over a small region) tells the phase.

Suppose instead that the energy were unchanged by flipping a small group of variables around one point, at any point, independently. Such a symmetry is called local or gauge symmetry. It is not a symmetry of the physics in the usual sense: two configurations related by it describe the same physical state, expressed in different variables. We will see that a local symmetry can never be broken (Elitzur 1975), so no local quantity that changes under it can ever become non-zero. Yet the 3D model has a sharp phase transition. The phases are distinguished by non-local observables: products of variables around closed loops, the Wilson loops [Wilson 1974], which decay with the area enclosed by the loop in one phase and with its perimeter in the other.

This has a direct consequence for learning from data. A model trained on raw configurations sees the variables as they are given, redundancy included. Section 6 shows that, in the Ising gauge theory, every single variable and every pair of variables is pure noise at every temperature; the physics sits in products of four or more variables arranged in closed loops. This is the situation Carrasquilla and Melko met: their fully connected network, which has no built-in notion of neighbouring links, failed, while a network with \(2\times2\) convolutions, which can read off one plaquette at a time, succeeded.

2. The model

Take a \(d\)-dimensional hypercubic lattice of linear size \(L\) with periodic boundaries (a torus), \(N=L^d\) sites \(x\), and unit vectors \(\hat e_\mu\), \(\mu=0,\dots,d-1\). A link \((x,\mu)\) joins \(x\) to \(x+\hat e_\mu\); there are \(dN\) links. An Ising variable \(\sigma_\mu(x)=\pm1\) lives on every link, not on the sites. An elementary square of the lattice, a plaquette \(p=(x;\mu\nu)\) with \(\mu<\nu\), has four links on its boundary, and we define the plaquette variable as their product: \[ U_p = U_{\mu\nu}(x) = \sigma_\mu(x)\,\sigma_\nu(x+\hat e_\mu)\,\sigma_\mu(x+\hat e_\nu)\,\sigma_\nu(x) = \pm1 . \] There are \(\binom d2N\) plaquettes: \(N\) in 2D, \(3N\) in 3D.

The energy is a sum over plaquettes, and configurations are weighted by the Boltzmann factor: \[ H(\sigma) = -J\sum_p U_p ,\qquad p(\sigma)=\frac{e^{-H(\sigma)/k_BT}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_{\sigma}e^{-H(\sigma)/k_BT}. \] A plaquette with \(U_p=+1\) costs \(-J\), a frustrated plaquette (\(U_p=-1\), an odd number of negative links on its boundary) costs \(+J\).

The figure below shows these objects on a small two-dimensional patch. The Ising variables sit on the links (thin navy segment: \(\sigma=+1\); thick orange segment: \(\sigma=-1\), as in the configuration plots of this notebook), the sites carry no variable, and each plaquette is the product of its four boundary links, labelled here for the plaquette with lower-left corner \(x\). The single flipped link frustrates the two plaquettes that contain it (orange), each of which costs \(+J\) instead of \(-J\).

We set \(J=k_B=1\) and use the single dimensionless coupling \[ \beta \equiv \frac{J}{k_BT}. \] Large \(\beta\) is low temperature. In the lattice-gauge-theory literature this coupling is usually written \(\beta\) (or \(\beta_g\)), and we follow that convention; it is the analogue of \(K=J/k_BT\) of the Ising model (Lab 012). The average of an observable \(A\) is \(\langle A\rangle=\sum_\sigma A(\sigma)p(\sigma)\).

Throughout the notebook, and in the saved data, a configuration is an array U of shape (d, L, ..., L) with \(d\) spatial axes: U[mu][x_0, ..., x_{d-1}] \(=\sigma_\mu(x)\) is the link from site \(x\) to \(x+\hat e_\mu\). The lattice axis \(\mu\) of U[mu] is array axis \(\mu\), so “the field at \(x+\hat e_\mu\)” is jnp.roll(a, -1, axis=mu). Plaquettes are returned with shape (P, L, ..., L), \(P=d(d-1)/2\), planes ordered \((0,1)\) in 2D and \((0,1),(0,2),(1,2)\) in 3D; plaq[k][x] is the plaquette whose lower-left corner is \(x\).

Code 
def shift(a, mu, n=1):
    # the field a (spatial axes only) evaluated at x + n e_mu, periodic boundaries
    return jnp.roll(a, -n, axis=mu)

def plaquettes(U):
    # U: (d, L, ..., L) links  ->  (P, L, ..., L) plaquettes, planes (mu, nu) with mu < nu in lexicographic order
    d = U.shape[0]
    return jnp.stack([U[mu] * shift(U[nu], mu) * shift(U[mu], nu) * U[nu]
                      for mu in range(d) for nu in range(mu + 1, d)])

def energy(U):
    # H = -J sum_p U_p with J = 1
    return -jnp.sum(plaquettes(U))

def energy_2d_loops(U):
    # the same energy for d = 2 with explicit loops over sites: an independent implementation used as a check
    U = np.asarray(U); L = U.shape[1]; E = 0
    for x in range(L):
        for y in range(L):
            E -= U[0, x, y] * U[1, (x + 1) % L, y] * U[0, x, (y + 1) % L] * U[1, x, y]
    return E

2.1 Heat-bath Monte Carlo

To sample the Boltzmann distribution with a heat-bath Monte Carlo (MC) algorithm we need the local field of a link. The link \(l=(x,\mu)\) belongs to \(2(d-1)\) plaquettes: for every direction \(\nu\neq\mu\) one “above” (\((x;\mu\nu)\)) and one “below” (\((x-\hat e_\nu;\mu\nu)\)). Each of these plaquettes equals \(\sigma_l\) times the product of its three other links, a staple. Collecting all terms of \(H\) that contain \(\sigma_l\) gives \[ \begin{aligned} H &= -\sigma_l\,h_l + (\text{terms without }\sigma_l),\\ h_l &= \sum_{\nu\neq\mu}\Big[\sigma_\nu(x+\hat e_\mu)\sigma_\mu(x+\hat e_\nu)\sigma_\nu(x)\\ &\qquad\quad + \sigma_\nu(x+\hat e_\mu-\hat e_\nu)\sigma_\mu(x-\hat e_\nu)\sigma_\nu(x-\hat e_\nu)\Big]. \end{aligned} \] The staple sum \(h_l\in\{-2(d-1),\dots,2(d-1)\}\) plays the role of the local field of the Ising model (Lab 010).

The product of the links of a staple is the value that its plaquette takes when \(\sigma_l=+1\), so \(h_l\) measures how strongly the surrounding links favour \(\sigma_l=+1\). In \(d\) dimensions every link has \(2(d-1)\) staples, four in 3D.

Instead of proposing a flip and accepting it with some probability (Metropolis), the heat-bath algorithm draws the new value of \(\sigma_l\) directly from its conditional distribution given all other links: \[ \begin{aligned} P(\sigma_l=+1\mid\text{rest}) &= \frac{e^{\beta h_l}}{e^{\beta h_l}+e^{-\beta h_l}}\\ &= \frac{1}{1+e^{-2\beta h_l}}. \end{aligned} \] Sampling exactly from a conditional distribution of \(p(\sigma)\) leaves \(p(\sigma)\) invariant, so the chain converges to the Boltzmann distribution.

Updating links one at a time would be slow, so we look for links that can be updated simultaneously. Two links interact only if they lie on a common plaquette. Links of different directions can share plaquettes, so the directions are updated one after the other. Two links of the same direction \(\mu\) share a plaquette only if they are displaced by \(\pm\hat e_\nu\) with \(\nu\neq\mu\). If we colour the links of direction \(\mu\) by the parity of \(\sum_{\nu\neq\mu}x_\nu\), such a displacement changes the parity, so all links of one colour are independent given the rest and can be updated together (this needs even \(L\)). One sweep consists of \(2d\) such half-updates, that is, one update of every link.

The implementation follows the JAX idioms of Lab 018: a pure sweep(U, key, beta) function with explicit random keys; lax.scan repeats sweeps inside one compiled program; jax.vmap runs many independent chains (different \(\beta\) and replicas) at once; jax.jit compiles everything. The function run_gauge returns int8 links of shape (n_beta, n_rep * n_samples, d, L, ..., L): for each coupling, n_rep independent chains each store n_samples configurations separated by n_between sweeps after n_equil equilibration sweeps. Chains start from the cold configuration \(\sigma\equiv+1\) (the reason is discussed in Section 5.4).

def staple_sum(U, mu):
    # h_l for all links of direction mu: shape (L, ..., L)
    h = jnp.zeros_like(U[mu])
    for nu in range(U.shape[0]):
        if nu != mu:
            h = h + shift(U[nu], mu) * shift(U[mu], nu) * U[nu]                                   # plaquette (x; mu nu)
            h = h + shift(shift(U[nu], mu), nu, -1) * shift(U[mu], nu, -1) * shift(U[nu], nu, -1)  # plaquette (x - e_nu; mu nu)
    return h

def parity_masks(d, L):
    # masks[mu, c]: links of direction mu whose parity of sum_{nu != mu} x_nu equals c;  shape (d, 2, L, ..., L)
    X = np.indices((L,) * d)
    return jnp.asarray(np.stack([[(X.sum(0) - X[mu]) % 2 == c for c in (0, 1)] for mu in range(d)]))

def sweep(U, key, beta, masks):
    for mu in range(U.shape[0]):
        for c in (0, 1):
            key, sub = jax.random.split(key)
            p_up = jax.nn.sigmoid(2.0 * beta * staple_sum(U, mu))           # heat-bath probability of sigma = +1
            new = jnp.where(jax.random.uniform(sub, p_up.shape) < p_up, 1.0, -1.0)
            U = U.at[mu].set(jnp.where(masks[mu, c], new, U[mu]))
    return U, key

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("n_equil", "n_samples", "n_between"))
def gauge_chains(U0, keys, betas, masks, n_equil, n_samples, n_between):
    # U0: (chains, d, L..), keys: (chains, 2), betas: (chains,) -> int8 samples (chains, n_samples, d, L..)
    def one_chain(U, key, beta):
        step = lambda c, _: (sweep(c[0], c[1], beta, masks), None)
        carry, _ = lax.scan(step, (U, key), None, length=n_equil)
        def store(c, _):
            c, _ = lax.scan(step, c, None, length=n_between)
            return c, c[0].astype(jnp.int8)
        return lax.scan(store, carry, None, length=n_samples)[1]
    return jax.vmap(one_chain)(U0, keys, betas)

def run_gauge(d, L, betas, n_rep, n_equil, n_samples, n_between, seed):
    # every beta gets n_rep independent chains, all started from sigma = +1;  returns (n_beta, n_rep * n_samples, d, L..) int8
    B = jnp.repeat(jnp.asarray(betas, jnp.float32), n_rep)
    U0 = jnp.ones((B.size, d) + (L,) * d, jnp.float32)
    out = gauge_chains(U0, jax.random.split(jax.random.PRNGKey(seed), B.size), B, parity_masks(d, L),
                       n_equil, n_samples, n_between)
    return np.asarray(out).reshape((len(betas), n_rep * n_samples, d) + (L,) * d)

3. Gauge symmetry

Choose a site \(x_0\) and flip all \(2d\) links that touch it. More generally, choose \(g(x)=\pm1\) on every site and set \[ \sigma_\mu(x)\;\longrightarrow\;\sigma'_\mu(x) = g(x)\,\sigma_\mu(x)\,g(x+\hat e_\mu). \] This transformation leaves every plaquette unchanged. Going around a plaquette, every corner \(y\) is the end of exactly two of its four links, so \(g(y)\) appears twice and \(g(y)^2=1\): \[ \begin{aligned} U'_{\mu\nu}(x) &= \big[g_1\sigma_\mu(x)g_2\big]\big[g_2\sigma_\nu(x+\hat e_\mu)g_3\big]\big[g_4\sigma_\mu(x+\hat e_\nu)g_3\big]\big[g_1\sigma_\nu(x)g_4\big]\\ &= U_{\mu\nu}(x), \end{aligned} \] with \(g_1,\dots,g_4\) the values at the four corners. Every plaquette, hence \(H\) and the Boltzmann weight, is unchanged. There are \(2^N\) choices of \(g\), one independent \(\mathbb Z_2\) symmetry per site: this is what makes the symmetry local. The global choice \(g\equiv-1\) changes no link at all, so \(2^{N-1}\) distinct transformations act on the links.

The sketch below shows a gauge transformation at a single site \(x_0\), with \(g(x_0)=-1\) and \(g=+1\) everywhere else. The four links that touch \(x_0\) change sign, but every plaquette contains either none or exactly two of them, so no plaquette changes: the frustrated plaquettes (orange) are the same before and after, and so is the energy.

3.1 Counting gauge orbits

On a \(3\times3\) torus there are \(2\cdot9=18\) links and \(2^{18}=262\,144\) configurations, few enough to enumerate. We store each as an 18-bit integer; flipping a link is an exclusive or (XOR) with its bit, and the gauge transformation at site \(x\) is an XOR with the 4-bit mask of the links touching \(x\). The group of all transformations is generated by these 9 masks, and the counting above predicts \(2^{N-1}=2^8\) distinct elements and therefore \(2^{18}/2^{8}=2^{10}\) orbits. We find the orbit of every configuration by taking the smallest integer in it as a label.

def link_index(d, L):
    # integer index of link (mu, x): array of shape (d, L, ..., L) with values 0 .. d L^d - 1

def site_masks(d, L):
    # bitmask (Python int) of the 2d links touching each site
    idx, masks = link_index(d, L), []
    for x in itertools.product(range(L), repeat=d):

        for mu in range(d):

            m |= 1 << int(idx[(mu,) + x]) | 1 << int(idx[(mu,) + tuple(xm)])   # outgoing and incoming link of direction mu
        masks.append(m)
    return masks

3.2 Elitzur’s theorem

Elitzur’s theorem states that the thermal average of any quantity that is not gauge invariant vanishes. For a single link the proof takes one line. Pick the gauge transformation \(g\) that is \(-1\) only at the starting site of \(l\). It flips \(\sigma_l\), it maps the set of all configurations one-to-one onto itself, and it does not change the weight. Hence \[ \begin{aligned} \langle\sigma_l\rangle &= \frac1Z\sum_\sigma\sigma_l\,e^{-\beta H(\sigma)} = \frac1Z\sum_{\sigma}(-\sigma_l)\,e^{-\beta H(\sigma)}\\ &= -\langle\sigma_l\rangle = 0 . \end{aligned} \] For the Ising ferromagnet the same argument with the global flip gives \(\langle m\rangle=0\) in every finite system, yet the magnetisation is spontaneously non-zero below \(T_c\): in an infinite system, an infinitesimal field picks one of the two ordered states, and turning the whole lattice around would require a domain wall whose energy grows with the system size. Elitzur showed that for a local symmetry this never happens [S. Elitzur, Phys. Rev. D 12, 3978 (1975), Impossibility of spontaneously breaking local symmetries]: the transformation that flips \(\sigma_l\) changes only the \(2d\) links at one site. If a small symmetry-breaking field \(h\) coupled to the links, \(-h\sum_l\sigma_l\), is added to \(H\), this transformation changes the weight by at most a factor \(e^{\pm2d\beta h}\), independently of \(L\); the bound survives the infinite-volume limit, so \(\langle\sigma_l\rangle\to0\) when \(h\to0\) afterwards. Equivalently, the barrier between \(\sigma_l=+1\) and \(\sigma_l=-1\) stays finite in the infinite system.

The difference between the two kinds of symmetry is visible in the dynamics. We start Monte Carlo from the perfectly “ordered” configuration \(\sigma\equiv+1\) and follow the link average \(\bar\sigma=\frac1{dN}\sum_l\sigma_l\) and the plaquette average \(\bar U=\frac1{N_p}\sum_pU_p\) (\(N_p\) = number of plaquettes) along the chain, for a 3D lattice at \(\beta=0.9\). Section 5 shows that this coupling lies in the ordered (deconfined) phase of the 3D model, where the plaquettes stay strongly ordered. For comparison we do the same with the 3D Ising ferromagnet, \(H_I=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j\), at \(K=J/k_BT=0.3\), which is in its ordered phase (\(K>K_{c}^{I}\), Section 5), starting from \(s\equiv+1\), and follow its magnetisation \(m\).

4. Exact solution in two dimensions

In 2D every plaquette is a “face” of the lattice, and it turns out that the plaquettes behave as independent Ising variables. This makes the model solvable in a few lines. We derive the solution in three steps (gauge fixing, partition function, observables) and check every step.

4.1 Gauge fixing

We start with an open lattice of \(L\times L\) plaquettes: \((L+1)^2\) sites, \(2L(L+1)\) links, no periodic wrap-around. A gauge transformation can set to \(+1\) every link of a maximal tree, a set of links that connects all sites without forming a loop: walk from a root site along the tree and choose \(g\) site by site so that each tree link becomes \(+1\). A tree on \((L+1)^2\) sites has \((L+1)^2-1\) links, so the number of links left free is \[ 2L(L+1)-(L+1)^2+1 = L^2 = \text{number of plaquettes}. \] Adding the free links one at a time in a suitable order, each one closes exactly one new plaquette, so the free links and the plaquettes determine each other one-to-one. Every gauge orbit contains exactly one tree-fixed configuration, and every orbit has \(2^{(L+1)^2-1}\) elements (all transformations except the global one act freely). Therefore \[ Z_{\rm open} = 2^{(L+1)^2-1}\sum_{\{U_p=\pm1\}}\prod_p e^{\beta U_p} = 2^{(L+1)^2-1}\,(2\cosh\beta)^{L^2}. \] With periodic boundaries the torus has \(N\) sites, \(2N\) links and \(N\) plaquettes, and the counting changes in two places. First, every link lies on exactly two plaquettes, so \(\prod_pU_p=\prod_l\sigma_l^2=1\): only \(N-1\) plaquettes are independent. Second, a maximal tree has \(N-1\) links, leaving \(2N-(N-1)=N+1\) free links: \(N-1\) fixed by the plaquettes, plus two more, the products of links along a horizontal and a vertical loop that winds around the torus (holonomies \(W_x\), \(W_y\)), which the plaquettes do not determine. The configurations are counted correctly: \(2^{N-1}\) (gauge) \(\times\,2^{N-1}\) (plaquettes) \(\times\,2^2\) (holonomies) \(=2^{2N}\). The sum over plaquettes with the constraint \(\prod_pU_p=1\) is done with the projector \(\frac12\big(1+\prod_pU_p\big)\): \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{\{U_p\}}\tfrac12\Big(1+\prod_pU_p\Big)\prod_pe^{\beta U_p} &= \tfrac12\Big[(2\cosh\beta)^N + (2\sinh\beta)^N\Big]\\ \Longrightarrow\quad Z_{\rm torus} &= 2^{N-1}\cdot 4\cdot\tfrac12\Big[(2\cosh\beta)^N+(2\sinh\beta)^N\Big]\\ &= 2^N\Big[(2\cosh\beta)^N+(2\sinh\beta)^N\Big]. \end{aligned} \] The cell below performs the maximal-tree gauge fixing on a random torus configuration and checks the counting; the next cell checks both formulas for \(Z\) against exact enumeration, symbolically.

# exact enumeration: histogram of the number of satisfied minus frustrated plaquettes, then Z(beta) as a SymPy expression
def plaquette_sum_histogram(n_links, pmasks, chunk=2**20):
    # for all 2^n_links configurations (bit = 1: sigma = -1) count n_S = number of configurations with sum_p U_p = S
    pm = np.array(pmasks, dtype=np.int64); n_p = len(pm)
    hist = np.zeros(2 * n_p + 1, dtype=np.int64)
    for start in range(0, 2**n_links, chunk):
        c = np.arange(start, min(start + chunk, 2**n_links), dtype=np.int64)
        frustrated = np.zeros(c.shape, np.int64)
        for m in pm:
            frustrated += np.bitwise_count(c & m) & 1          # U_p = -1 iff an odd number of its links are -1
        hist += np.bincount(n_p - 2 * frustrated + n_p, minlength=2 * n_p + 1)   # index S + n_p
    return hist

def open_lattice_masks(Lp):
    # open lattice of Lp x Lp plaquettes: horizontal links (x, y) x < Lp, y <= Lp and vertical links x <= Lp, y < Lp
    h = {(x, y): i for i, (x, y) in enumerate((x, y) for x in range(Lp) for y in range(Lp + 1))}

    return len(h) + len(v), masks

4.2 Thermodynamics

In the thermodynamic limit the term \((2\sinh\beta)^N\) is negligible (\(\tanh\beta<1\)), and \(Z\simeq 2^N(2\cosh\beta)^N\) is the partition function of \(N\) independent two-state variables with energy \(-U_p\). All thermodynamics therefore follows from a single plaquette: \[ \begin{aligned} z_1 &= \sum_{U=\pm1}e^{\beta U} = 2\cosh\beta,\\ \langle U_p\rangle &= \frac{\partial\ln z_1}{\partial\beta} = \tanh\beta,\\ c &= \beta^2\frac{\partial^2\ln z_1}{\partial\beta^2} = \frac{\beta^2}{\cosh^2\beta}, \end{aligned} \] where \(c=\beta^2N_p^{-1}\big(\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2\big)\) is the specific heat per plaquette (as in Lab 012, \(c=\partial u/\partial T\) with \(u\) the energy per plaquette). The free energy per plaquette, \(f=-T[\ln2+\ln(2\cosh\beta)]\), is an analytic function of \(T\) for every \(T>0\): no phase transition. These are exactly the formulas of the 1D Ising chain (Lab 012, Section 2.1) with \(K\to\beta\): the 2D gauge theory is a 1D Ising chain in disguise, the plaquettes playing the role of the bond variables \(s_is_{i+1}\).

On a finite torus we keep the second term and obtain \[ \begin{aligned} \langle U_p\rangle_{L} &= \frac1N\frac{\partial\ln Z_{\rm torus}}{\partial\beta} = \frac{t + t^{N-1}}{1+t^N},\\ t &= \tanh\beta , \end{aligned} \] where the correction \(t^{N-1}\) comes from the global constraint \(\prod_pU_p=1\). SymPy checks the single-plaquette formulas and the finite-torus expression below; at \(\beta=1\) the finite-size correction is large for \(N=4\) and \(9\) and small for \(N=16\) (\(0.7686\) against \(\tanh 1=0.7616\)).

4.3 Monte Carlo and the 2D data set

We now generate (or load) the 2D Monte Carlo data set and compare the measured \(\langle U_p\rangle\) and \(c\) with the exact results. First we check the sampler itself on the \(4\times4\) torus, against the exact finite-size formula \(\langle U_p\rangle_L\).

One detail concerns the stored configurations. Local Monte Carlo moves change the gauge orbit only slowly at large \(\beta\) (Section 3.2), so a chain started from \(\sigma\equiv+1\) would keep an artificial excess of positive links. Since the Boltzmann weight is gauge invariant, applying an independent uniformly random gauge transformation \(g\) to every stored configuration produces an exact sample of the same distribution with the gauge degrees of freedom fully equilibrated. All saved configurations are gauge-randomised in this way.

def gauge_randomise(U, d, seed):
    # U: (batch..., d, L, ..., L) int8 links;  sigma'_mu(x) = g(x) sigma_mu(x) g(x + e_mu) with random g per configuration
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    lat = U.shape[-d:]
    g = r.choice(np.array([-1, 1], np.int8), size=U.shape[:-d - 1] + lat)
    out = np.empty_like(U)
    for mu in range(d):
        idx = (Ellipsis, mu) + (slice(None),) * d                      # selects sigma_mu for every configuration
        out[idx] = g * U[idx] * np.roll(g, -1, axis=g.ndim - d + mu)
    return out

4.4 Wilson loops

For a closed path \(C\) on the lattice the Wilson loop is the product of the links along it, \(W(C)=\big\langle\prod_{l\in C}\sigma_l\big\rangle\). It is gauge invariant, since every site of a closed path is the end of exactly two of its links. (A product along an open path is not invariant, since its end points appear once; by the argument of Section 3.2 its average is zero, see Exercise 1.) For an \(R\times T\) rectangle we write \(W(R,T)\).

Wilson loops are related to plaquettes by a lattice version of Stokes’ theorem. For a contractible loop, the product of links along \(C\) equals the product of the plaquettes of any surface \(A\) bounded by \(C\): \[ \prod_{l\in C}\sigma_l = \prod_{p\in A}U_p . \] The proof is one observation: in the product of plaquettes, every link inside \(A\) belongs to two plaquettes of \(A\) and appears squared, so only the boundary links remain.

The sketch below shows a rectangular loop \(\mathcal C\) of \(R=4\) by \(T=2\) lattice spacings. The product of the links along it equals the product of the eight plaquettes \(U_p\) of the enclosed area \(A\), because each interior link belongs to two of these plaquettes and drops out. How the average of this product decreases with the size of the loop, with its area or only with its perimeter, distinguishes the two phases of the gauge theory.

With independent plaquettes (open lattice, or the torus for \(N\to\infty\)) the Wilson loop therefore obeys an area law: \[ W(R,T) = \prod_{p\in A}\langle U_p\rangle = (\tanh\beta)^{RT} = e^{-\sigma_s\,RT},\qquad \sigma_s = -\ln\tanh\beta>0 . \] On the finite torus the same projector trick as in Section 4.1 gives, with \(|A|\) plaquettes inside the loop, \[ \begin{aligned} W_L(A) &= \frac{(2\sinh\beta)^{|A|}(2\cosh\beta)^{N-|A|} + (2\cosh\beta)^{|A|}(2\sinh\beta)^{N-|A|}}{(2\cosh\beta)^N+(2\sinh\beta)^N}\\ &= \frac{t^{|A|}+t^{N-|A|}}{1+t^N}. \end{aligned} \] The physical meaning of the area law comes from lattice gauge theory, where one lattice direction is (imaginary) time. A rectangle of spatial extent \(R\) and temporal extent \(T\) describes a pair of static external charges created at distance \(R\), held for a time \(T\) and annihilated; for large \(T\), \(W(R,T)\sim e^{-V(R)T}\) with \(V(R)\) the energy of the pair [Wilson 1974; Kogut 1979]. The area law gives \(V(R)=\sigma_sR\): the energy grows linearly with the separation, and the charges can never be pulled apart. This is confinement, and the coefficient \(\sigma_s\) is the string tension. In 2D, \(\sigma_s>0\) at every finite \(\beta\): the theory confines at every temperature.

    # product of n consecutive links of direction mu starting at x:  a = U[mu], shape (L..)

def wilson_loop(U, R, T):
    # average of the R x T loop product over positions and all ordered planes (mu, nu): R along mu, T along nu
    d, vals = U.shape[0], []
    for mu in range(d):
        for nu in range(d):
            if mu != nu:
                a, c = line_prod(U[mu], mu, R), line_prod(U[nu], nu, T)
                vals.append(jnp.mean(a * shift(c, mu, R) * shift(a, nu, T) * c))
    return jnp.mean(jnp.stack(vals))

4.5 Topological sectors

The energy is minimal, \(E_0=-N_p\), when every plaquette is satisfied, \(U_p=+1\). On an open lattice this fixes the configuration up to a gauge transformation: one gauge orbit. On the torus the plaquettes do not fix the two holonomies \(W_x=\prod_{x}\sigma_0(x,y)\) and \(W_y=\prod_y\sigma_1(x,y)\) (Section 4.1). In a ground state, \(W_x\) does not depend on the row \(y\) where it is measured (two rows bound a strip of plaquettes, and by the Stokes argument their ratio is the product of these plaquettes, \(=+1\)), and it is gauge invariant. Hence the ground states split into \(2^2=4\) gauge-inequivalent topological sectors \((W_x,W_y)\in\{\pm1\}^2\), each a gauge orbit of \(2^{N-1}\) configurations: \[ \begin{aligned} \#\{\text{ground states}\} &= 4\cdot 2^{N-1} = 2^{N+1}\ \text{configurations},\\ &\phantom{=}\ 4\ \text{physically distinct ground states}. \end{aligned} \] The number 4 does not depend on \(L\): it counts the independent non-contractible loops of the torus (\(2\) in 2D, \(d\) on a \(d\)-dimensional torus, giving \(2^d\) sectors). No local measurement distinguishes the sectors: every plaquette is \(+1\) in all of them. This degeneracy is the classical counterpart of the topological ground-state degeneracy of Kitaev’s toric code [Kitaev 2003], whose ground states are built from the same closed-loop configurations.

We check the counting by exact enumeration: the \(3\times3\) torus in 2D (\(2^{18}\) configurations) and the \(2\times2\times2\) torus in 3D (\(24\) links, \(2^{24}\approx1.7\times10^{7}\) configurations).

5. The deconfinement transition in three dimensions

5.1 Flux loops

A three-dimensional lattice has elementary cubes, and they constrain the plaquettes. Each link of a cube lies on exactly two of its six faces. Therefore the product of the six face plaquettes is \(\prod\sigma_l^2=1\): every cube has an even number of frustrated faces. In 3D the dual of a plaquette is a link of the dual lattice (the lattice of cube centres) piercing it, so the frustrated plaquettes form closed loops on the dual lattice, called flux loops. The smallest one is created by flipping a single link: it frustrates the \(4\) plaquettes around that link, a dual loop of length \(4\).

The competition between the energy and the entropy of flux loops drives a transition. A flux loop of length \(\ell\) costs \(2J\ell\), and the number of such loops grows exponentially with \(\ell\), as for the domain walls of the 2D Ising model (Lab 012, Section 3.3). At small \(T\) (large \(\beta\)) only small loops occur; at high \(T\) arbitrarily long loops proliferate, and between the two regimes there is a transition. In 2D there is no such constraint (a 2D lattice has no cubes), plaquettes are independent point defects, and nothing happens.

5.2 Wegner duality

The derivation parallels Kramers–Wannier duality (Lab 012, Sections 4–5): two expansions count the same geometric objects.

On the Ising side we use the high-temperature expansion. For \(H_I=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j\) on the cubic lattice at coupling \(K\), with \(e^{Kss'}=\cosh K(1+ss'\tanh K)\) (Lab 012, Section 4.1), \[ Z_I(K) = 2^N(\cosh K)^{3N}\sum_{G\ \text{closed}}(\tanh K)^{|G|}, \] the sum running over closed graphs \(G\) on the cubic lattice (every site touched by an even number of bonds of \(G\)), with \(|G|\) the number of bonds.

On the gauge side we use the low-temperature expansion. Start from all \(U_p=+1\), energy \(-3N\). A configuration whose frustrated plaquettes form the set \(F\) has weight \(e^{3N\beta}e^{-2\beta|F|}\). By Section 5.1, \(F\) is a set of closed loops on the dual lattice, which is again a simple cubic lattice. Every allowed \(F\) is realised by the same number of link configurations (one gauge orbit of size \(2^{N-1}\) times the \(2^3\) holonomy sectors), so \[ Z_g(\beta) = 2^{N-1}\,2^3\,e^{3N\beta}\sum_{F\ \text{closed on the dual lattice}}\big(e^{-2\beta}\big)^{|F|}, \] up to loops that wind around the torus, whose weight per site vanishes for \(N\to\infty\).

Both sums are the same function \(g(y)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\ln\sum_{G\ \rm closed}y^{|G|}\) of the cubic lattice. Hence, per site, \[ \begin{aligned} \varphi_g(\beta) &\equiv \lim\frac{\ln Z_g}{N} = \ln2 + 3\beta + g\big(e^{-2\beta}\big),\\ \varphi_I(K) &= \ln2 + 3\ln\cosh K + g(\tanh K), \end{aligned} \] \[ \boxed{\ \tanh K = e^{-2\beta}\ }\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \varphi_g(\beta) - 3\beta = \varphi_I(K) - 3\ln\cosh K . \] [F. J. Wegner, J. Math. Phys. 12, 2259 (1971); J. B. Kogut, Rev. Mod. Phys. 51, 659 (1979)] The map is decreasing: weak gauge coupling (large \(\beta\), low \(T\)) corresponds to small \(K\), the disordered Ising phase, and vice versa. The 3D model is not self-dual (unlike the 2D Ising model): it is dual to a different model, and the free energies are equal up to analytic terms. So the gauge theory has a singularity exactly where the Ising model has one, of the same type.

The duality therefore maps the critical point of one model onto the critical point of the other. With the 3D Ising critical coupling from high-precision Monte Carlo, \(K_c^{I}=0.221654626(5)\) [Ferrenberg, Xu, Landau, Phys. Rev. E 97, 043301 (2018)], the critical coupling of the gauge theory is \[ \beta_c = -\tfrac12\ln\tanh K_c^{I}, \] which the cell below evaluates to \(\beta_c=0.761413\). Differentiating the boxed identity with respect to \(\beta\) (\(3N\) plaquettes and \(3N\) bonds per \(N\) sites) also gives a relation between quantities that can be measured in the two models: \[ \begin{aligned} \langle U_p\rangle_g(\beta) &= 1 + \frac{dK}{d\beta}\Big(\langle s_is_j\rangle_I(K)-\tanh K\Big),\\ \frac{dK}{d\beta} &= -\frac{1}{\sinh2\beta}, \end{aligned} \] where \(\langle s_is_j\rangle_I\) is the nearest-neighbour correlation of the Ising model. We derive it in SymPy and test it with two independent Monte Carlo simulations.

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("n_equil", "n_samples", "n_between"))
def ising_chains(s0, keys, Ks, chess, n_equil, n_samples, n_between):
    # per chain: nearest-neighbour correlation (1/3N) sum_<ij> s_i s_j of n_samples stored configurations, shape (chains, n_samples)
    def one(s, key, K):
        step = lambda c, _: (ising_sweep(c[0], c[1], K, chess), None)
        carry, _ = lax.scan(step, (s, key), None, length=n_equil)

    return jax.vmap(one)(s0, keys, Ks)

5.5 Area law and perimeter law

The plaquette average shows that something happens at \(\beta_c\); the Wilson loops show what. In the confined phase (\(\beta<\beta_c\), high \(T\)) flux loops proliferate, and every loop of the lattice is pierced by many of them at random. The Wilson loop then decays with the area, \(W(R,T)\sim e^{-\sigma_sRT}\), as in 2D; to leading order at small \(\beta\), \(W\approx(\tanh\beta)^{RT}\) (tile the minimal surface with plaquettes). In the deconfined phase (\(\beta>\beta_c\), low \(T\)) flux loops are small and rare. Only the small loops that pierce the surface close to its boundary contribute (a small loop deep inside pierces the surface twice and contributes \((-1)^2\)), so \(W(R,T)\sim e^{-\mu\,2(R+T)}\): a perimeter law with a constant \(\mu\).

Area and perimeter terms can be separated without fitting by the ratio proposed by Creutz [Phys. Rev. D 21, 2308 (1980)], \[ \chi(R,T) = -\ln\frac{W(R,T)\,W(R-1,T-1)}{W(R,T-1)\,W(R-1,T)} . \] For \(W=e^{-\sigma_sRT-2\mu(R+T)-c_0}\) the perimeter terms and the constant \(c_0\) cancel and \(\chi=\sigma_s\) exactly; with a pure perimeter law \(\chi=0\). With \(W(0,T)=W(R,0)=1\), \(\chi(1,1)=-\ln W(1,1)=-\ln\langle U_p\rangle\).

6. Gauge redundancy

Elitzur’s argument extends to any product of links. Let \(S\) be a set of links and \(\sigma_S=\prod_{l\in S}\sigma_l\). A gauge transformation at site \(x\) multiplies \(\sigma_S\) by \(g(x)^{n_x(S)}\), where \(n_x(S)\) is the number of links of \(S\) touching \(x\). So \(\sigma_S\) is gauge invariant only if every \(n_x(S)\) is even, i.e. \(S\) is a closed set of loops; otherwise the argument of Section 3.2 gives \(\langle\sigma_S\rangle=0\). This has two consequences for data analysis.

The first is that every single link and every pair of links averages to zero: \(\langle\sigma_l\rangle=0\) and \(\langle\sigma_l\sigma_{l'}\rangle=\delta_{ll'}\), because two distinct links never form a closed loop. The covariance matrix of the raw links is the identity matrix at every temperature. Any method that uses only means and covariances of the raw variables (a linear classifier, principal component analysis (PCA), a Gaussian model) sees the same featureless data at every \(\beta\).

The second is that the smallest non-trivial correlations involve four links around a plaquette. A model on raw links has to discover four-point products, and a model that is not built to look at neighbouring links together (a fully connected network has no notion of which links are neighbours) faces a search over a huge number of possible combinations. A convolution with a small kernel sees one plaquette at a time, which is consistent with the CNN result of Carrasquilla and Melko.

Gauge-invariant features avoid the problem. Plaquettes \(U_p\) (local, gauge invariant) and Wilson loops (non-local, gauge invariant) carry all the physical information, and feeding plaquettes instead of links removes the redundancy by construction.

We check the first statement on the 3D data, comparing the covariance of raw links with the covariance of plaquettes, pooled over all couplings.

Exercise 4 — Strong-coupling expansion in 3D

For small \(\beta\) write \(e^{\beta U_p}=\cosh\beta\,(1+tU_p)\), \(t=\tanh\beta\), for every plaquette and expand the product.
