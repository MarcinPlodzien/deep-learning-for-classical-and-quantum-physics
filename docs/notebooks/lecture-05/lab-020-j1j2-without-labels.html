Lab 020 — The \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) model without labels

Lab 018 introduced the frustrated \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) Ising model and produced snapshots on a grid in the \((g,T)\) plane, \(g=J_2/J_1\). We know its physics: a ferromagnet for \(g<1/2\), a stripe phase for \(g>1/2\), a paramagnet at high \(T\), and a transition temperature that drops to zero at the frustrated point \(g=1/2\). We also met the two practical problems of a physicist who maps a phase diagram. Which quantity to plot is not obvious, since the magnetisation is blind to the stripe phase. And a dense scan is expensive, since fluctuation observables such as the specific heat need many samples at every grid point.

Here we treat the model as if we did not know its phases, run two unsupervised methods on the raw snapshots, and compare every output with the known physics. The first is principal component analysis (PCA) of all snapshots, implemented in JAX and cross-checked with scikit-learn: which patterns does it find? The second is autoencoder anomaly detection (Kottmann, Huembeli, Lewenstein, Acín, Phys. Rev. Lett. 125, 170603 (2020)): we train on a small, well-understood corner of the phase diagram, map the reconstruction error over the whole plane, and read off boundaries, for three different training regimes. The three regimes are then combined into one map. Finally we ask how much of this survives a coarse scan with few samples, and where such a scan tells us to measure more.

In Google Colab, run Lab 018 first (it writes data/j1j2_L32.npz), or upload that file to data/. The notebook runs on the CPU (central processing unit), and training the three autoencoders takes a few minutes. jax, flax, optax and scikit-learn are pre-installed in Colab; the stored outputs were produced with scikit-learn 1.6.1.

1. Reference maps from known observables

Every unsupervised result needs something to be judged against. We recompute the three maps of Lab 018 from the same snapshots: magnetisation \(\langle|m|\rangle\), stripe order parameter \(\langle m_{\rm str}\rangle\), and specific heat \(c\). Each is a thermal average over the Boltzmann distribution, \(\langle O\rangle=\frac1Z\sum_{\mathbf s}O(\mathbf s)\,e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)}\) with \(Z=\sum_{\mathbf s}e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)}\) and \(\beta=1/T\), and is estimated from the \(n=100\) snapshots \(\mathbf s^{(k)}\) of each grid point by sample means. With \(e_k=H(\mathbf s^{(k)})/N\) (computed as \(-\frac1{2N}\sum_is_ih_i\) with the local field \(h_i\) of Lab 018), \(m_k=\frac1N\sum_is^{(k)}_i\) and \(\overline{x}=\frac1n\sum_kx_k\), the function observables computes:

observable definition through \(Z\) estimator from \(n\) samples (as in the code)
magnetisation \(\langle\lvert m\rvert\rangle=\frac1Z\sum_{\mathbf s}\lvert m(\mathbf s)\rvert e^{-\beta H}\) \(\overline{\lvert m\rvert}=\frac1n\sum_k\lvert m_k\rvert\)
stripe order \(\langle m_{\rm str}\rangle\), \(m_{\rm str}=\sqrt{m_{(\pi,0)}^2+m_{(0,\pi)}^2}\), \(m_{(\pi,0)}=\frac1N\sum_{\mathbf r}(-1)^{x}s_{\mathbf r}\), \(m_{(0,\pi)}=\frac1N\sum_{\mathbf r}(-1)^{y}s_{\mathbf r}\) \(\overline{m_{\rm str}}=\frac1n\sum_km_{{\rm str},k}\)
specific heat \(c=\frac{\beta^2}{N}\partial^2_\beta\ln Z=N\beta^2\big(\langle e^2\rangle-\langle e\rangle^2\big)\) \(N\beta^2\big(\overline{e^2}-\overline{e}^{\,2}\big)\) (population variance, division by \(n\))

From the specific heat we extract the reference boundary: for every \(g\) the temperature of the maximum of \(c\). These are the known properties against which every unsupervised result is judged.

Code 
def local_field(s, g):
    nn = jnp.roll(s, 1, -2) + jnp.roll(s, -1, -2) + jnp.roll(s, 1, -1) + jnp.roll(s, -1, -1)
    nnn = (jnp.roll(s, (1, 1), (-2, -1)) + jnp.roll(s, (1, -1), (-2, -1))
           + jnp.roll(s, (-1, 1), (-2, -1)) + jnp.roll(s, (-1, -1), (-2, -1)))
    return nn - g * nnn

@jax.jit
def observables(s, g):
    ph = jnp.where(jnp.arange(L) % 2 == 0, 1.0, -1.0)
    m = jnp.abs(s.mean((-2, -1)))
    ms = jnp.sqrt((s * ph[:, None]).mean((-2, -1)) ** 2 + (s * ph[None, :]).mean((-2, -1)) ** 2)
    e = -0.5 * jnp.mean(s * local_field(s, g), axis=(-2, -1))
    return m.mean(-1), ms.mean(-1), e.var(-1)

2. PCA on all snapshots

2.1 PCA implementation in JAX

All snapshots from the whole grid (a subset of 20 per grid point) go into one PCA, with no information about \(g\) or \(T\). We look at the leading principal components (PC 1, PC 2, … in the figures) as images on the lattice, and we measure their overlap with the three candidate ordering patterns of Lab 018: uniform \((0,0)\), stripes \((\pi,0)\) and \((0,\pi)\). An overlap of 1 means that a component is that pattern.

Code 
n_pca = 20
Xp = jnp.asarray(S[:, :, :n_pca].reshape(-1, L * L), jnp.float32)

@jax.jit
def pca(X):
    mu = X.mean(0)
    _, sv, Vt = jnp.linalg.svd(X - mu, full_matrices=False)
    return mu, sv**2 / jnp.sum(sv**2), Vt

The first three principal components are the uniform pattern and the two stripe patterns (overlaps close to 1), and the rest of the spectrum is flat. Projecting on them reproduces both order-parameter maps. PCA has found the stripe order parameter, the very quantity the magnetisation scan missed, without being told it exists.

It works here because both order parameters are linear functions of the spins, \(m=\frac1N\sum_r s_r\) and \(m_{(\pi,0)}=\frac1N\sum_r(-1)^{x}s_r\), and patterns that are linear in the data are what PCA is built to find. Order that is not linear in the spins (vortices in the XY model of planar spins, Wilson loops in gauge theories, the overlap of a spin glass) is outside its reach; Labs 021–023 test this.

One detail of the spectrum is a property of our data rather than of the model. PC 2 and PC 3 carry different variance, although vertical and horizontal stripes are equivalent by symmetry. Each annealed chain picks one orientation when it orders and keeps it; with a finite number of chains the two orientations are not sampled equally.

2.2 PCA with scikit-learn

The same analysis with scikit-learn uses the estimator PCA(n_components=6, svd_solver="full"). Its fit(X) centres the snapshots, performs a complete singular value decomposition and stores the components as the rows of components_ and their fractions of the variance in explained_variance_ratio_; transform(X) would return the projections \(z_a\). The computation is the one our pca performs, so the fractions of variance must agree to float32 precision. Single components are defined only up to sign, and PC 2 and PC 3 carry similar variances, so a small rounding difference could rotate them into each other; we therefore compare the three-dimensional subspace spanned by PC 1–3. Its principal angles with our subspace are the arccosines of the singular values of \(V_{\rm sk}V^{\top}\), where the rows of \(V_{\rm sk}\) and \(V\) are the two sets of components; all three singular values must be 1. The cell asserts both statements.

3. Autoencoder anomaly maps

PCA needed the order to be linear in the spins. Anomaly detection with an autoencoder makes no such assumption, and it lets us ask a different question: where do the snapshots stop looking like those of a region we understand? The procedure has four steps: 1. choose a training region, a small rectangle in the \((g,T)\) plane where we trust our understanding; 2. train an autoencoder on snapshots from that rectangle only; 3. evaluate the reconstruction error \(\varepsilon(\boldsymbol s)=\frac1N\lVert\boldsymbol s-\hat{\boldsymbol s}\rVert^2\) on snapshots from every grid point, and plot its mean as a map; 4. read off a boundary: for every \(g\), the temperature where \(\log\bar\varepsilon\) changes fastest.

We repeat this for three training regions, all in corners of the plane:

regime region what we know there
A: ferromagnet \(g\le0.2\), \(T\le1.0\) nearly saturated ferromagnet (weak \(J_2\), low \(T\))
B: paramagnet \(g\le0.2\), \(T\ge3.5\) high temperature: weakly correlated spins
C: stripes \(g\ge0.8\), \(T\le1.0\) strong \(J_2\), low \(T\): stripe order

The autoencoder is a small convolutional network: two stride-2 convolutions with periodic boundaries, like the lattice (\(32\to16\to8\)), a dense bottleneck of 4 numbers, and the mirror image back, ending with \(\tanh\).

One known symmetry is built into the training data rather than into the network. The energy is unchanged when all spins flip, \(H(\boldsymbol s)=H(-\boldsymbol s)\), so \(\boldsymbol s\) and \(-\boldsymbol s\) are equally likely. A finite training set need not contain both signs equally often (an annealed chain orders into one of them and stays there), so we add every training snapshot also with all spins flipped: the simplest way to build a known symmetry into a network.

Code 
class ConvAE(nnx.Module):
    def __init__(self, latent=4, *, rngs):
        self.c1 = nnx.Conv(1, 16, (3, 3), strides=2, padding="CIRCULAR", rngs=rngs)      # 32 -> 16
        self.c2 = nnx.Conv(16, 32, (3, 3), strides=2, padding="CIRCULAR", rngs=rngs)     # 16 -> 8
        self.fe = nnx.Linear(8 * 8 * 32, latent, rngs=rngs)
        self.fd = nnx.Linear(latent, 8 * 8 * 32, rngs=rngs)
        self.d1 = nnx.ConvTranspose(32, 16, (3, 3), strides=(2, 2), padding="SAME", rngs=rngs)
        self.d2 = nnx.ConvTranspose(16, 1, (3, 3), strides=(2, 2), padding="SAME", rngs=rngs)
    def encode(self, x):
        h = nnx.relu(self.c1(x[..., None])); h = nnx.relu(self.c2(h))
        return self.fe(h.reshape(h.shape[0], -1))
    def __call__(self, x):
        h = nnx.relu(self.fd(self.encode(x))).reshape(-1, 8, 8, 32)
        return jnp.tanh(self.d2(nnx.relu(self.d1(h)))[..., 0])

@nnx.jit
def train_step(model, opt, x):
    loss, grads = nnx.value_and_grad(lambda m: jnp.mean((m(x) - x) ** 2))(model)
    opt.update(model, grads)
    return loss

@nnx.jit
def recon_error(model, x):
    return jnp.mean((model(x) - x) ** 2, axis=(-2, -1))

def train_ae(X, epochs=15, batch=64, seed=0):
    model = ConvAE(rngs=nnx.Rngs(seed))
    opt = nnx.Optimizer(model, optax.adam(1e-3), wrt=nnx.Param)
    X = jnp.asarray(np.concatenate([X, -X]), jnp.float32)  # H(s) = H(-s): every snapshot also with all spins flipped
    key = jax.random.PRNGKey(seed)
    for ep in range(epochs):
        key, k = jax.random.split(key); perm = jax.random.permutation(k, X.shape[0])
        for i in range(0, X.shape[0] - batch + 1, batch):
            loss = train_step(model, opt, X[perm[i:i + batch]])
    return model, float(loss)

N_EVAL = 20                                                # snapshots per grid point used for the maps
def spread(n_total, n):                                    # n snapshot indices spread over all chains
    return np.linspace(0, n_total - 1, n).round().astype(int)

def error_map(model, data=S, n=N_EVAL):
    idx = spread(data.shape[2], n)
    out = np.zeros(data.shape[:2])
    for i in range(data.shape[0]):
        e = recon_error(model, jnp.asarray(data[i][:, idx].reshape(-1, L, L), jnp.float32))
        out[i] = np.asarray(e).reshape(data.shape[1], n).mean(1)
    return out

def boundary_from_map(err, T_axis=T):
    # for every g: temperature of the fastest change of the error along T (midpoint between grid temperatures),
    # and the size of that change relative to the full range of the map
    d = np.abs(np.diff(err, axis=1))
    i = np.argmax(d, axis=1)
    return 0.5 * (T_axis[i] + T_axis[i + 1]), d[np.arange(len(i)), i] / (err.max() - err.min())

The autoencoder of regime A, trained on the ferromagnet, reconstructs the ferromagnetic region almost perfectly; its boundary \(T_c(g)\) for \(g<1/2\) is a sharp edge that falls towards \(g=1/2\). Regime B, trained on nearly uncorrelated spins, learns little more than the average, and its error is large everywhere. Regime C, trained on the stripes, reconstructs the stripe region well, and its boundary \(T_c(g)\) for \(g>1/2\) appears. The ferromagnet and the paramagnet are both anomalous.

4. Combined map

Since each regime recognises only its own phase, the natural next step is to combine them. Each trained autoencoder defines a “normal” level of its reconstruction error, measured on its own training region. For grid point \((g,T)\) and regime \(k\) we compute an anomaly score: how far the mean error lies from that level, in units of its spread, \[ a_k(g,T) = \frac{\left|\,\bar\varepsilon_k(g,T) - \mu_k\right|}{\sigma_k}, \] where \(\mu_k\) and \(\sigma_k\) are the mean and standard deviation of \(\bar\varepsilon_k\) over the grid points of the training region. The absolute value matters: regime B reconstructs ferromagnetic snapshots better than its training data, which is also an anomaly. A grid point is assigned to the regime with the smallest score if that score is below 3; otherwise it is unassigned: it looks like none of the three references.

The three colours reproduce the three known phases, and two further features need the physics to be understood. The unassigned band lies mainly on the ordered side of each transition line. There the system is ordered, but thermal fluctuations (flipped islands, domain walls) make the snapshots atypical for a model trained deep in the phase at \(T\le1\); the band marks where the references stop being representative, which is the critical region and its surroundings. Around \(g=1/2\) at the lowest temperatures the points are assigned to the paramagnet. That is correct physics: at the frustrated point the transition temperature goes to zero and the system does not order within our temperature range.

None of this used an order parameter. What it did use is a choice of reference regions, and the result depends on that choice (regime B alone would have missed the stripe phase entirely). The physicist’s job has moved, not disappeared.

5. Coarse scan and refinement

The maps above used the full grid. Suppose instead we can afford only a coarse scan: every eighth value of \(g\) (\(\Delta g = 0.1\)), every eighth temperature (\(\Delta T = 0.4\)), and 10 snapshots per point. We train regimes A and C on the coarse data only, and look for intervals where the error jumps. Those intervals are where a dense measurement is worth paying for, and the cell compares the total number of snapshots of this strategy with that of the dense scan.

Code

    pts = []

        pts.append((gval, 0.5 * (T[t_idx[j]] + T[t_idx[j + 1]])))
    refined[name] = np.array(pts)

In an experiment this is the useful mode: a cheap survey with raw snapshots, then dense, careful measurements only where the survey says something changes, analysed with the physicist’s tools (order parameters, finite-size scaling) once we know what to look for.

Summary

The table collects what each method was given, what it found, and how that compares with the known physics (AE: autoencoder).

method input what it found compared with the known physics
PCA all snapshots uniform and stripe patterns as PC 1–3 the two order parameters, exactly; works because both are linear in the spins
AE, regime A (ferromagnet) snapshots from \(g\le0.2,T\le1\) the ferromagnetic boundary on the specific-heat ridge; blind to the difference stripes/paramagnet
AE, regime B (paramagnet) snapshots from \(g\le0.2,T\ge3.5\) the ferromagnetic boundary misses the stripe phase completely
AE, regime C (stripes) snapshots from \(g\ge0.8,T\le1\) the stripe boundary on the specific-heat ridge
AE, combined three regimes three phases + an unassigned region unassigned: the frustrated region near \(g=1/2\) and the transition lines
