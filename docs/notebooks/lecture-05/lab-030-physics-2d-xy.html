Lab 030 — The 2D XY model

The two-dimensional (2D) Ising model of Labs 010–012 and 018 has a phase transition of the textbook kind: below \(T_c\) the spins choose “up” or “down”, the magnetisation \(m\) is non-zero, and \(m\) is a local order parameter that tells the two phases apart in a single snapshot.

This notebook studies a model in which that picture fails completely. The spins of the XY model are unit vectors in a plane, free to point in any direction. Their symmetry group is continuous (rotations by any angle), and in two dimensions this has a dramatic consequence: the magnetisation vanishes at every temperature \(T>0\) in the thermodynamic limit. Nevertheless the model has a phase transition, the Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless (BKT) transition, driven not by a local order parameter but by topological defects — vortices — that are bound in pairs at low temperature and unbind at \(T_{\rm BKT}\).

The notebook contains no machine learning. It is the physics foundation of the anomaly-detection study of the XY model later in Lecture 5: it tells us what an unsupervised algorithm could discover in snapshots of the XY model, which naive features it will be tempted to use (the magnetisation of a finite lattice), and which physical quantities define the transition (vortices and the spin stiffness). It also produces and saves the Monte Carlo data set used there.

The story unfolds as follows. Section 1 explains why a continuous symmetry is special in two dimensions and where the physics is realised in nature. Section 2 defines the model and its symmetries, builds a Monte Carlo (MC) sampler in JAX (Metropolis with checkerboard updates, over-relaxation and Wolff cluster updates) and generates the saved data set. Section 3 treats spin waves in the Gaussian approximation: the angle fluctuations grow logarithmically with the system size, which is the content of the Mermin–Wagner theorem, and we check this numerically. Section 4 introduces vortices — the winding number, the energy \(\pi J\ln(L/a)\) and entropy \(2\ln(L/a)\) of a free vortex, the Kosterlitz–Thouless free-energy argument — and counts them in snapshots. Section 5 defines the helicity modulus (spin stiffness) and its estimator; the universal jump \(\Upsilon(T_{\rm BKT})=2T_{\rm BKT}/\pi\) then gives the crossing method for \(T_{\rm BKT}\). Section 6 shows that the correlation function decays as a power law below \(T_{\rm BKT}\) and exponentially above, with \(\eta(T_{\rm BKT})=1/4\), and Section 7 that the specific heat has a smooth peak above \(T_{\rm BKT}\) rather than a divergence. Section 8 draws the consequences of the missing local order parameter for machine learning, Section 9 summarises, and exercises with solutions, references and textbooks close the notebook.

Every derivation is done twice: symbolically with SymPy, and numerically on the lattice or with Monte Carlo.

Run the cells in order; the notebook runs on the CPU (JAX is pre-installed in Google Colab). The Monte Carlo data set is written to data/xy_L{L}.npz; if the files are already present they are loaded instead of regenerated. Figures are written to ../beamer/figures/. 1. Continuous symmetry in two dimensions

We begin by recalling equilibrium statistical mechanics. A configuration \(\boldsymbol\theta\) of a classical system with energy \(H(\boldsymbol\theta)\) occurs at temperature \(T\) with the Boltzmann probability \[ \begin{aligned} p(\boldsymbol\theta)&=\frac{e^{-\beta H(\boldsymbol\theta)}}{Z},\qquad \beta=\frac1T,\\ Z&=\int\! d\boldsymbol\theta\; e^{-\beta H(\boldsymbol\theta)}, \end{aligned} \] with \(k_B=1\). The free energy \(F=-T\ln Z\) balances energy against entropy, \(F=E-TS\): at low \(T\) the energy wins and the system orders, at high \(T\) the entropy wins and it disorders. A phase transition is a temperature at which \(F/N\) (with \(N\) the number of spins, \(N\to\infty\)) is not an analytic function of \(T\).

In the 2D Ising model (\(s_i=\pm1\)) the energy is invariant under the global flip \(s_i\to-s_i\), yet below \(T_c\) the system picks one of the two ordered states and the magnetisation \(\langle m\rangle\) is non-zero: the symmetry is spontaneously broken. To destroy the order one has to create a domain wall, whose energy grows with its length, and at low \(T\) such walls are too expensive.

The situation changes when the symmetry is continuous. If the spins are planar unit vectors \(\boldsymbol s_i=(\cos\theta_i,\sin\theta_i)\) and the energy depends only on relative angles, there is no domain wall: the direction can rotate gradually over a long distance. Turning the spins by \(2\pi\) gradually across a region of linear size \(\ell\) costs \(\frac J2(2\pi/\ell)^2\) per bond, i.e. an energy \(\propto\ell^{d-2}\) for the whole region: independent of \(\ell\) in two dimensions, whereas an Ising domain wall costs \(\propto\ell^{d-1}\) (Section 3 makes the spin-wave argument precise). These long-wavelength twists, spin waves, are the Goldstone modes of the broken continuous symmetry, and they are so cheap in \(d\le2\) that thermal fluctuations destroy the long-range order at every \(T>0\). Mermin and Wagner (1966) proved this rigorously for the isotropic Heisenberg model; Berezinskii (1971) analysed systems with a continuous symmetry group in one and two dimensions. We derive the effect for the XY model in the spin-wave approximation and check it with Monte Carlo.

The sketch below compares the two cases on a strip of twelve columns. In the Ising model (top) the spins change from up to down across a single column of bonds; every bond on that column is broken and costs \(2J\), so a wall across a region of linear size \(\ell\) costs an energy proportional to \(\ell^{d-1}\). In the XY model (bottom) the angle can instead turn by \(2\pi\) gradually, by \(2\pi/\ell\) from one column to the next. Each bond then costs only about \(\frac J2(2\pi/\ell)^2\), and the twist of a whole region costs an energy proportional to \(\ell^{d-2}\), which in two dimensions does not grow with \(\ell\).

Berezinskii (1971, 1972) and Kosterlitz and Thouless (1973) showed that the 2D XY model nevertheless has two distinct phases, and hence a transition without an order parameter. At low \(T\), correlations decay as a power law of distance (“quasi-long-range order”), and the system resists a twist: it has a non-zero spin stiffness. At high \(T\), correlations decay exponentially and the stiffness vanishes. The transition between them is caused by vortices, points around which the angle winds by \(2\pi\). Below \(T_{\rm BKT}\) vortices exist only in tightly bound vortex–antivortex pairs; above, free vortices proliferate. Kosterlitz (1974) worked out the critical behaviour with the renormalisation group, and Nelson and Kosterlitz (1977) predicted that the stiffness drops discontinuously at \(T_{\rm BKT}\) by a universal amount.

The same physics describes any 2D system whose state is a phase: thin films of superfluid \(^4\)He (the phase of the condensate wave function), thin superconducting films, and planar magnets. The Nelson–Kosterlitz prediction refers directly to superfluid films (title: Universal jump in the superfluid density of two-dimensional superfluids), and Bishop and Reppy (1978) studied the superfluid transition in two-dimensional \(^4\)He films experimentally.

The importance of these ideas was recognised by the 2016 Nobel Prize in Physics, awarded “with one half to David J. Thouless, and the other half to F. Duncan M. Haldane and J. Michael Kosterlitz for theoretical discoveries of topological phase transitions and topological phases of matter” (nobelprize.org); the BKT transition is the topological phase transition of that citation.

For the notebooks that follow, the lesson is that a learning algorithm looking at snapshots will find whatever varies most between them. For the XY model the obvious candidate, the magnetisation, is a finite-size effect, and the relevant objects, vortices, are invisible in a single spin’s value: they are a property of the angles around a plaquette. Knowing this physics is the only way to judge what an algorithm has found.

2. Model, symmetries and Monte Carlo sampling

2.1 Definition

On an \(L\times L\) square lattice with periodic boundaries (a torus), \(N=L^2\) planar unit vectors \(\boldsymbol s_i=(\cos\theta_i,\sin\theta_i)\), \(\theta_i\in[0,2\pi)\), interact with their nearest neighbours: \[ \begin{aligned} H(\boldsymbol\theta)&=-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\boldsymbol s_i\cdot\boldsymbol s_j\\ &=-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\cos(\theta_i-\theta_j),\qquad J>0, \end{aligned} \] where \(\langle i,j\rangle\) runs over the \(2N\) nearest-neighbour bonds. We set \(J=1\) and \(k_B=1\); the lattice spacing is \(a=1\).

The figure below shows the model on a small patch: every site carries an arrow of unit length at angle \(\theta_i\), measured from the horizontal axis as on the unit circle on the right, and each bond between nearest neighbours contributes \(-J\cos(\theta_i-\theta_j)\) to the energy. Parallel neighbours give \(-J\) and antiparallel neighbours \(+J\), while a small relative angle \(\delta\) costs only \(\frac J2\delta^2\) above the minimum; this is why the gradual twists of Section 1 are so cheap.

The energy is invariant under several transformations. The global rotation \(\theta_i\to\theta_i+\alpha\) for all \(i\), with any \(\alpha\), is a continuous symmetry, the rotation part \(U(1)\cong SO(2)\) of the group \(O(2)\); together with the global reflection \(\theta_i\to-\theta_i\) it forms \(O(2)\). The periodicity \(\theta_i\to\theta_i+2\pi\) on any single site looks harmless, but it is what allows vortices (Section 4). Finally there are the lattice symmetries: translations, rotations by \(90^\circ\) and mirror reflections.

Every term of \(H\) is at least \(-J\), so the minimum energy \(E_0=-2JN\) is reached when all spins are parallel, \(\theta_i=\theta_0\). The ground state is not unique: it is a continuous family labelled by \(\theta_0\in[0,2\pi)\) (compare the two ground states of Ising).

The basic observables are the magnetisation vector and the energy per spin, \[ \boldsymbol m=\frac1N\sum_i\boldsymbol s_i,\qquad |\boldsymbol m|=\sqrt{m_x^2+m_y^2},\qquad e=\frac{H}{N}. \] Because of the rotation symmetry \(\langle\boldsymbol m\rangle=0\) on any finite lattice; the useful quantities are \(\langle|\boldsymbol m|\rangle\) and \(\langle\boldsymbol m^2\rangle\).

In the code, angles are stored as an array theta of shape (..., L, L), axis -1 is the \(x\) direction (columns) and axis -2 the \(y\) direction (rows). jnp.roll(theta, -1, -1) is the right neighbour of every site, so jnp.roll(theta, -1, -1) - theta lists the angle differences on all \(N\) horizontal bonds at once (and similarly for the \(N\) vertical bonds). The cell below checks the ground-state energy, the energy of a random state, and the invariance of \(H\) under the global rotation, the reflection and a shift by \(2\pi\) on one site. For a random state with independent angles uniform in \([0,2\pi)\) each bond term \(\cos(\theta_i-\theta_j)\) has mean \(0\) and variance \(1/2\), and the \(2N\) bond terms are uncorrelated, so \(e=-(J/N)\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\cos(\theta_i-\theta_j)\) has mean \(0\) and variance \((J/N)^2\cdot 2N\cdot\frac12=J^2/N\): a standard deviation \(J/\sqrt N=0.0625\,J\) for \(N=16^2\).

2.2 Checkerboard Metropolis updates

To sample the Boltzmann distribution we start from the Metropolis algorithm, which Lab 010 derived for Ising spins and which works for any energy. We propose a new angle for spin \(i\), \(\theta_i'=\theta_i+\delta\,(2u-1)\) with \(u\) uniform in \([0,1)\) and a step width \(\delta\), and accept with probability \(\min\!\left(1,e^{-\beta\Delta E_i}\right)\). The proposal is symmetric, so detailed balance holds. The energy change only involves the four neighbours \(j\) of \(i\) and is expressed through the local field \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol h_i&=\sum_{j\in{\rm nn}(i)}\boldsymbol s_j=\Big(\sum_j\cos\theta_j,\ \sum_j\sin\theta_j\Big),\\ \Delta E_i&=-J\,\boldsymbol h_i\cdot(\boldsymbol s_i'-\boldsymbol s_i). \end{aligned} \]

Since nearest neighbours always have different colours on a chessboard (\((x+y)\bmod 2\)), all spins of one colour can be updated simultaneously: none of them appears in another’s local field. One sweep updates all black sites and then all white sites, i.e. one attempted move per spin. In code we propose new angles for all sites and accept only on the current colour (a mask); this wastes half of the random numbers but keeps the code short.

2.3 Wolff cluster updates

Local moves have a weakness. Near \(T_{\rm BKT}\) the correlation length is comparable with \(L\), and below \(T_{\rm BKT}\) it is infinite. Rotating a large correlated region one spin at a time is slow: the autocorrelation time of Metropolis grows with \(L\), as we measure below.

Wolff’s idea (Wolff 1989) is to turn the XY model into an Ising model for one update. Choose a random direction \(\boldsymbol r=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)\) and consider only reflections of spins across the line perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol r\): \[ \begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol s_i\to\boldsymbol s_i-2(\boldsymbol s_i\cdot\boldsymbol r)\,\boldsymbol r\\ \Longleftrightarrow\quad &\theta_i\to\pi+2\varphi-\theta_i . \end{aligned} \] Write \(\sigma_i=\boldsymbol s_i\cdot\boldsymbol r=\cos(\theta_i-\varphi)\). If a spin is reflected and its neighbour is not, the bond energy changes by \(2J\sigma_i\sigma_j\); reflecting both leaves it unchanged. With respect to reflections the XY model is therefore an Ising model with bond-dependent couplings \(J\sigma_i\sigma_j\), and the Swendsen–Wang/Wolff construction of Lab 018 applies: 1. pick a random seed site; 2. activate each bond \(\langle i,j\rangle\) with probability \(p_{ij}=1-\exp\!\big(\min(0,\,-2\beta J\sigma_i\sigma_j)\big)\) (only bonds with \(\sigma_i\sigma_j>0\) can be active); 3. reflect the whole cluster of sites connected to the seed by active bonds.

The update satisfies detailed balance and needs no acceptance step (Wolff 1989). Combined with a random \(\varphi\) it is ergodic together with the Metropolis moves.

The cluster search needs some care in JAX, because a sequential “grow the cluster” loop is awkward to vectorise over many chains. We use the fact that the bond variables are independent: drawing all bonds of the lattice first and then taking the connected component of the seed gives exactly the same cluster distribution as growing it bond by bond. The connected components are found as in Lab 018 by label propagation: every site starts with its own index as label and repeatedly takes the smallest label among itself and its bonded neighbours, followed by pointer jumping (a site adopts the label of its label), until no label changes. The price: under vmap every chain waits until the slowest chain has converged, and at low \(T\) the clusters span the lattice and need many iterations. On a CPU this makes one Wolff update much more expensive than one Metropolis sweep (measured in Section 2.6).

2.4 Over-relaxation

A cheap alternative for speeding up the sampling (Creutz 1987; Brown and Woch 1987) is to reflect each spin about its local field. With \(\varphi_i\) the direction of \(\boldsymbol h_i\), \[ \begin{aligned} &\theta_i\to2\varphi_i-\theta_i\\ \Longrightarrow\quad &\boldsymbol h_i\cdot\boldsymbol s_i\ \text{unchanged},\quad\Delta E_i=0 . \end{aligned} \] The move is deterministic, its own inverse, and conserves the energy, so it is accepted with probability \(1\) and leaves the Boltzmann distribution invariant. It does not change the energy, so on its own it is not ergodic (it samples at fixed energy); combined with Metropolis sweeps it is. It moves spins by large angles at no cost; Section 2.6 measures how much this shortens the autocorrelation times. The checkerboard applies as for Metropolis.

def checkerboard(L):
    # (L, L) array with values 0 / 1 = colour (x + y) mod 2
    return (jnp.arange(L)[:, None] + jnp.arange(L)[None, :]) % 2

def local_field(theta):
    # h_i = sum of the 4 neighbour vectors;  returns (h_x, h_y), each of the same shape as theta (..., L, L)
    c, s = jnp.cos(theta), jnp.sin(theta)
    nbrs = ((1, -2), (-1, -2), (1, -1), (-1, -1))          # (shift, axis) for the up / down / left / right neighbours
    return sum(jnp.roll(c, d, ax) for d, ax in nbrs), sum(jnp.roll(s, d, ax) for d, ax in nbrs)

def metropolis_sweep(theta, key, beta, delta):
    # theta: (L, L);  one sweep = black sites, then white sites.  Returns new theta and the acceptance rate.
    colour = checkerboard(theta.shape[-1])
    k_prop, k_acc = jax.random.split(key)
    # every site is updated exactly once per sweep, so one proposal and one acceptance number per site suffice
    new = theta + delta * (2.0 * jax.random.uniform(k_prop, theta.shape) - 1.0)
    u_acc = jax.random.uniform(k_acc, theta.shape)
    n_acc = 0.0
    for c in (0, 1):                                          # Python loop, unrolled by jit
        hx, hy = local_field(theta)
        dE = -J * (hx * (jnp.cos(new) - jnp.cos(theta)) + hy * (jnp.sin(new) - jnp.sin(theta)))
        accept = (u_acc < jnp.exp(-beta * dE)) & (colour == c)
        theta = jnp.where(accept, jnp.mod(new, TWO_PI), theta)
        n_acc = n_acc + accept.sum()
    return theta, n_acc / theta.size

def overrelaxation_sweep(theta):
    # reflect every spin about its local field, black sites then white sites: theta_i -> 2 phi_i - theta_i,
    # with phi_i = atan2(h_y, h_x) the direction of the local field.  The energy is unchanged; no random numbers.
    colour = checkerboard(theta.shape[-1])
    for c in (0, 1):
        hx, hy = local_field(theta)
        theta = jnp.where(colour == c, jnp.mod(2.0 * jnp.arctan2(hy, hx) - theta, TWO_PI), theta)
    return theta

def connected_labels(bond_x, bond_y):
    # bond_x[y, x]: site (y, x) bonded to (y, x+1);  bond_y[y, x]: site (y, x) bonded to (y+1, x);  periodic lattice.
    # Returns (L, L) int labels: two sites have the same label iff they are connected by active bonds.
    L = bond_x.shape[0]
    big = L * L
    bond_xm, bond_ym = jnp.roll(bond_x, 1, 1), jnp.roll(bond_y, 1, 0)        # bonds to the left / lower neighbour
    def body(carry):
        lab, _ = carry
        new = lab
        new = jnp.minimum(new, jnp.where(bond_x, jnp.roll(lab, -1, 1), big))
        new = jnp.minimum(new, jnp.where(bond_xm, jnp.roll(lab, 1, 1), big))
        new = jnp.minimum(new, jnp.where(bond_y, jnp.roll(lab, -1, 0), big))
        new = jnp.minimum(new, jnp.where(bond_ym, jnp.roll(lab, 1, 0), big))
        new = new.ravel()[new]                                                  # pointer jumping
        return new, jnp.any(new != lab)
    lab, _ = lax.while_loop(lambda c: c[1], body, (jnp.arange(big).reshape(L, L), True))
    return lab

def wolff_update(theta, key, beta):
    # one Wolff single-cluster update of theta (L, L).  Returns new theta and the cluster size / N.
    L = theta.shape[-1]
    k_phi, k_x, k_y, k_seed = jax.random.split(key, 4)
    phi = jax.random.uniform(k_phi, (), maxval=TWO_PI)                         # reflection direction r = (cos phi, sin phi)
    sigma = jnp.cos(theta - phi)                                               # sigma_i = s_i . r
    p_x = 1.0 - jnp.exp(jnp.minimum(0.0, -2.0 * beta * J * sigma * jnp.roll(sigma, -1, 1)))
    p_y = 1.0 - jnp.exp(jnp.minimum(0.0, -2.0 * beta * J * sigma * jnp.roll(sigma, -1, 0)))
    bond_x = jax.random.uniform(k_x, theta.shape) < p_x
    bond_y = jax.random.uniform(k_y, theta.shape) < p_y
    lab = connected_labels(bond_x, bond_y)
    seed = jax.random.randint(k_seed, (), 0, L * L)
    in_cluster = lab == lab.ravel()[seed]
    theta = jnp.where(in_cluster, jnp.mod(jnp.pi + 2.0 * phi - theta, TWO_PI), theta)
    return theta, in_cluster.mean()

After every Monte Carlo step (one Metropolis sweep followed by n_or over-relaxation sweeps and n_wolff Wolff updates) we record ten numbers per chain. With \(\Delta^x_r=\theta_{r+\hat x}-\theta_r\) and \(\Delta^y_r=\theta_{r+\hat y}-\theta_r\) the bond angles, these are the bond sums \[ \begin{aligned} C_x&=\sum_r\cos\Delta^x_r,\quad C_y=\sum_r\cos\Delta^y_r,\\ S_x&=\sum_r\sin\Delta^x_r,\quad S_y=\sum_r\sin\Delta^y_r,\\ E&=-J(C_x+C_y), \end{aligned} \] together with the magnetisation \((m_x,m_y)\), the vortex density \(n_v\) (number of plaquettes with non-zero winding number per site, defined in Section 4), the Metropolis acceptance rate and the Wolff cluster size. \(S_x,S_y\) enter the helicity modulus (Section 5).

The function simulate runs many independent chains at once: jax.vmap over chains (each with its own \(\beta\) and random key), lax.scan over Monte Carlo steps, everything compiled by jax.jit. The input theta0 has shape (C, L, L), keys (C, 2), betas (C,); the output time series has shape (C, n_meas, 10) and the stored snapshots (C, n_store, L, L), taken every n_meas // n_store steps.

def vorticity(theta):
    # winding number of every plaquette (Section 4).  Plaquette (y, x) has corners (y,x) -> (y,x+1) -> (y+1,x+1) -> (y+1,x),
    # traversed counter-clockwise (x to the right, y upwards).  Output: int32 array (..., L, L) with values in {-1, 0, +1} (rarely +-2).
    t00 = theta
    t01 = jnp.roll(theta, -1, -1)                    # (y, x+1)
    t11 = jnp.roll(t01, -1, -2)                      # (y+1, x+1)
    t10 = jnp.roll(theta, -1, -2)                    # (y+1, x)
    circ = wrap(t01 - t00) + wrap(t11 - t01) + wrap(t10 - t11) + wrap(t00 - t10)
    return jnp.round(circ / TWO_PI).astype(jnp.int32)

OBS = ["E", "Cx", "Cy", "Sx", "Sy", "mx", "my", "nv", "acc", "cluster"]       # the ten recorded numbers

def measure(theta):
    dx, dy = bond_differences(theta)
    cx, cy = jnp.cos(dx).sum(), jnp.cos(dy).sum()
    sx, sy = jnp.sin(dx).sum(), jnp.sin(dy).sum()
    mx, my = magnetisation(theta)
    nv = (vorticity(theta) != 0).mean()
    return jnp.stack([-J * (cx + cy), cx, cy, sx, sy, mx, my, nv])

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("n_equil", "n_meas", "n_store", "n_wolff", "n_or"))
def simulate(theta0, keys, betas, delta, n_equil, n_meas, n_store, n_wolff, n_or):
    gap = n_meas // n_store
    def one_chain(theta, key, beta):
        def mc_step(carry, _):
            theta, key = carry
            key, k_m, k_w = jax.random.split(key, 3)
            theta, acc = metropolis_sweep(theta, k_m, beta, delta)
            for _ in range(n_or):                                           # n_or over-relaxation sweeps
                theta = overrelaxation_sweep(theta)
            size = 0.0
            for k in jax.random.split(k_w, max(n_wolff, 1))[:n_wolff]:     # n_wolff cluster updates (0 = Metropolis only)
                theta, size = wolff_update(theta, k, beta)
            return (theta, key), jnp.concatenate([measure(theta), jnp.stack([acc, size])])
        carry, _ = lax.scan(mc_step, (theta, key), None, length=n_equil)    # equilibration, measurements discarded
        def block(carry, _):
            carry, series = lax.scan(mc_step, carry, None, length=gap)
            return carry, (series, carry[0])
        carry, (series, snaps) = lax.scan(block, carry, None, length=n_store)
        return series.reshape(n_store * gap, len(OBS)), snaps, carry[0]
    return jax.vmap(one_chain)(theta0, keys, betas)

def run_chains(L, T_list, n_chains, n_equil, n_meas, n_store, n_wolff=0, n_or=0, delta=1.0, seed=0, start="ordered"):
    # n_chains independent chains for every temperature in T_list.
    # Returns series (nT, n_chains, n_meas, 10) and snapshots (nT, n_chains, n_store, L, L), as numpy arrays.
    T_list = np.asarray(T_list, np.float32)
    C = len(T_list) * n_chains
    betas = jnp.asarray(np.repeat(1.0 / T_list, n_chains))
    k_init, k_run = jax.random.split(jax.random.PRNGKey(seed))
    if start == "ordered":
        theta0 = jnp.zeros((C, L, L))
    else:
        theta0 = jax.random.uniform(k_init, (C, L, L), maxval=TWO_PI)
    series, snaps, _ = simulate(theta0, jax.random.split(k_run, C), betas, delta, n_equil, n_meas, n_store, n_wolff, n_or)
    series = np.asarray(series).reshape(len(T_list), n_chains, n_meas, len(OBS))
    snaps = np.asarray(snaps).reshape(len(T_list), n_chains, n_store, L, L)
    return series, snaps

2.5 Low- and high-temperature limits

Before trusting the sampler we test it. There is no exact solution of the 2D XY model, but two limits are exact enough to test the code, and the three update schemes must agree with each other.

At low temperature, expanding \(\cos x\approx1-x^2/2\) (Section 3) gives a quadratic energy with \(N-1\) non-zero modes, each carrying \(T/2\) by equipartition, so \(\;e\approx-2J+\tfrac{T}{2}\,\tfrac{N-1}{N}\), with corrections of order \(T^2\). At high temperature the bonds become nearly independent, and for independent bonds \(\langle\cos(\theta_i-\theta_j)\rangle=\dfrac{\int_0^{2\pi}\cos x\,e^{\beta J\cos x}dx}{\int_0^{2\pi}e^{\beta J\cos x}dx}=\dfrac{I_1(\beta J)}{I_0(\beta J)}\), with \(I_n\) the modified Bessel functions. This is exact on a lattice without closed loops (for instance an open chain); on the square lattice closed loops of bonds give corrections that become small at high \(T\). So \(e\approx-2J\,I_1(\beta J)/I_0(\beta J)\). Finally, Metropolis alone, Metropolis + over-relaxation and Metropolis + Wolff are all exact algorithms, so at \(T=0.9\) they must give the same averages within errors.

def jackknife(f, chain_means):
    # chain_means: (n_chains, k) per-chain averages;  f maps a (k,) vector of averages to an estimate.
    # Returns (estimate, jackknife error) using leave-one-chain-out averages.
    n = chain_means.shape[0]
    full = f(chain_means.mean(0))
    loo = (chain_means.sum(0)[None, :] - chain_means) / (n - 1)
    vals = np.array([f(v) for v in loo])
    return full, np.sqrt((n - 1) / n * ((vals - vals.mean(0)) ** 2).sum(0))

2.6 Autocorrelation times and cost

The three schemes agree within the errors in \(e\), \(\Upsilon\) and \(\langle\boldsymbol m^2\rangle\), so they differ only in efficiency, and efficiency is what we measure next. Consecutive Monte Carlo configurations are correlated. For an observable \(A\) with time series \(A_t\), the normalised autocorrelation function and the integrated autocorrelation time are \[ \begin{aligned} \rho(t)&=\frac{\langle A_sA_{s+t}\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2}{\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2},\\ \tau_{\rm int}&=\frac12+\sum_{t=1}^{W}\rho(t), \end{aligned} \] where the window \(W\) is the first \(t\) with \(t\ge6\,\tau_{\rm int}(t)\) (a standard self-consistent cut-off that avoids summing pure noise). A run of \(n\) steps contains about \(n/(2\tau_{\rm int})\) independent measurements. We measure \(\tau_{\rm int}\) of \(|\boldsymbol m|^2\), the slowest common observable, at \(T=0.9\approx T_{\rm BKT}\) for \(L=16\) and \(L=32\), and compare it with the CPU time of one step. The figure of merit is the CPU time per independent sample, \(2\tau_{\rm int}\times\)(time per step).

Code 
def tau_int(series):
    # series: (n_chains, n_t).  Autocorrelation averaged over chains, then the self-consistent window.
    a = series - series.mean()
    n_t = a.shape[1]
    f = np.fft.rfft(a, 2 * n_t, axis=1)
    acf = np.fft.irfft(f * np.conj(f), axis=1)[:, :n_t].mean(0)
    rho = acf / acf[0]
    tau = 0.5
    for t in range(1, n_t):
        tau += rho[t]
        if t >= 6 * tau:
            break
    return tau, rho

3. Spin waves and the Mermin–Wagner theorem

With the data in hand we turn to theory, starting at low temperature. At low \(T\) neighbouring spins are almost parallel, so the cosine can be expanded. The result is a Gaussian (quadratic) theory that can be solved exactly, and it already shows the central fact of two dimensions: the angle fluctuations grow logarithmically with the system size, and the magnetisation goes to zero as a power of \(L\).

3.1 Gaussian approximation

Expand each bond to second order, \(\cos x=1-\tfrac{x^2}{2}+O(x^4)\), and treat \(\theta_i\) as a real variable (ignoring its \(2\pi\) periodicity, i.e. ignoring vortices, Section 4): \[ H\approx E_0+\frac J2\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}(\theta_i-\theta_j)^2 . \] On the torus, use Fourier modes \(\theta_{\boldsymbol r}=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_{\boldsymbol k}\tilde\theta_{\boldsymbol k}\,e^{i\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol r}\) with \(\boldsymbol k=\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x,n_y)\), \(n_{x,y}=0,\dots,L-1\). A difference along \(x\) becomes multiplication by \(e^{ik_x}-1\), and \(|e^{ik_x}-1|^2=2-2\cos k_x\), so \[ \begin{aligned} H&\approx E_0+\frac J2\sum_{\boldsymbol k}\lambda_{\boldsymbol k}\,|\tilde\theta_{\boldsymbol k}|^2,\\ \lambda_{\boldsymbol k}&=4-2\cos k_x-2\cos k_y\;\xrightarrow{\;k\to0\;}\;k^2 . \end{aligned} \] The modes decouple: each is a Gaussian variable, and equipartition gives \[ \langle|\tilde\theta_{\boldsymbol k}|^2\rangle=\frac{T}{J\lambda_{\boldsymbol k}}\qquad(\boldsymbol k\neq0). \] The mode \(\boldsymbol k=0\) is the global rotation; it costs no energy and does not matter for \(|\boldsymbol m|\).

SymPy confirms the three ingredients used here: the expansion of the cosine, the small-\(k\) form of \(\lambda_{\boldsymbol k}\), and the identity \(|e^{ik}-1|^2=2-2\cos k\).

3.2 Angle fluctuations

Let \(\phi_i=\theta_i-\bar\theta\) be the angle measured from the mean direction (\(\bar\theta\) is the \(\boldsymbol k=0\) part). Then \[ \begin{aligned} \langle\phi_i^2\rangle&=\frac1N\sum_{\boldsymbol k\neq0}\langle|\tilde\theta_{\boldsymbol k}|^2\rangle=\frac TJ\,G_L,\\ G_L&=\frac1N\sum_{\boldsymbol k\ne0}\frac1{\lambda_{\boldsymbol k}} . \end{aligned} \] For large \(L\) the sum becomes an integral, \(\frac1N\sum_{\boldsymbol k}\to\int\frac{d^2k}{(2\pi)^2}\), cut off at small \(k\) by the system size, \(k_{\min}\approx2\pi/L\), and at large \(k\) by the lattice, \(k_{\max}\approx\pi/a\). In two dimensions \(d^2k=2\pi k\,dk\), and \[ \begin{aligned} G_L&\approx\int_{2\pi/L}^{\pi}\frac{2\pi k\,dk}{(2\pi)^2\,k^2}=\frac1{2\pi}\ln\frac{L}{2a}\\ \Longrightarrow\quad\langle\phi^2\rangle&\simeq\frac{T}{2\pi J}\ln L+\text{const}. \end{aligned} \] The fluctuations diverge with the system size, however small \(T\) is. In three dimensions \(d^3k=4\pi k^2dk\) cancels the \(1/k^2\), the integral converges, and \(\langle\phi^2\rangle\) stays finite (Exercise 2).

    return (1 / lam).sum() / L**d

3.3 Finite-size magnetisation

The slope converges to \(1/(2\pi)\) to five digits, so the logarithmic growth is confirmed. Its consequence for the magnetisation follows quickly.

For a Gaussian variable \(\phi\) with variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\langle\cos\phi\rangle=e^{-\sigma^2/2}\) (symbolic check below). To leading order \(|\boldsymbol m|\approx\frac1N\sum_i\cos\phi_i\), so \[ \begin{aligned} \langle|\boldsymbol m|\rangle&\approx\exp\!\Big(-\frac{T}{2J}\,G_L\Big)\\ &\propto\;L^{-T/(4\pi J)}\;\xrightarrow{\;L\to\infty\;}\;0 . \end{aligned} \] This is the spin-wave version of the

Mermin–Wagner theorem (Mermin and Wagner, 1966): at any \(T>0\), a one- or two-dimensional isotropic Heisenberg model with finite-range interactions has no spontaneous (anti)ferromagnetic order.

The rigorous proof uses the Bogoliubov inequality; the physical content is the logarithmic divergence of \(\int d^2k/k^2\) that we just computed. The decay is a power law with a tiny exponent (\(T/(4\pi J)=0.024\) at \(T=0.3\)): on any lattice that fits into a computer, or a laboratory sample, \(\langle|\boldsymbol m|\rangle\) is large at low \(T\). It is a finite-size effect nevertheless.

3.4 Spin-wave correlation function

The same calculation gives the spin–spin correlation function at distance \(r\), \[ \begin{aligned} G(r)&=\langle\cos(\theta_0-\theta_{\boldsymbol r})\rangle=\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\langle(\theta_0-\theta_{\boldsymbol r})^2\rangle\Big),\\ \langle(\theta_0-\theta_{\boldsymbol r})^2\rangle&=\frac TJ\,D(\boldsymbol r),\\ D(\boldsymbol r)&=\frac1N\sum_{\boldsymbol k}\frac{2-2\cos(\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol r)}{\lambda_{\boldsymbol k}} . \end{aligned} \] For \(1\ll r\ll L\), \(D(r)\approx\frac1\pi\ln r+\text{const}\) (the lattice sum below shows this), hence \[ G(r)\propto r^{-\eta},\qquad \eta=\frac{T}{2\pi J}. \] A power law, not an exponential, and not a constant: this is “quasi-long-range order”. The exponent \(\eta\) depends continuously on \(T\): the whole low-temperature phase is a line of critical points.

4. Vortices

The slopes of the lattice sum approach \(1/\pi\approx0.318\), which completes the spin-wave picture. So far, however, the Gaussian theory has treated \(\theta\) as a real number. But \(\theta\) is an angle: going around a closed loop, the angle may return to its starting value plus a multiple of \(2\pi\). Such configurations cannot be removed by any small deformation; they are topological defects, and the Gaussian theory misses them entirely. They are what drives the BKT transition.

4.1 Winding number

For a closed loop \(\mathcal C\) around a point, the winding number (vorticity) is \[ w=\frac1{2\pi}\oint_{\mathcal C}\nabla\theta\cdot d\boldsymbol l\;\in\;\mathbb Z . \] A vortex is \(\theta(\boldsymbol r)=\arctan(y/x)\) (the polar angle, atan2(y, x)), with \(w=+1\); an antivortex \(\theta=-\arctan(y/x)\) has \(w=-1\). On the lattice the smallest loop is a plaquette. With the bond angle differences mapped to \([-\pi,\pi)\) (the shortest rotation between two neighbours), \[ \begin{aligned} w_p&=\frac1{2\pi}\Big([\theta_2-\theta_1]+[\theta_3-\theta_2]+[\theta_4-\theta_3]+[\theta_1-\theta_4]\Big),\\ [x]&=x \text{ mapped to }[-\pi,\pi), \end{aligned} \] for the corners \(1\to2\to3\to4\) traversed counter-clockwise. Without the brackets the sum would be exactly \(0\); with them it is \(2\pi\) times an integer. Each bracket lies in \([-\pi,\pi)\), so the sum lies in \([-4\pi,4\pi)\) and \(w_p\in\{-2,-1,0,1\}\); the value \(-2\) requires all four brackets to equal \(-\pi\) exactly, which has probability zero, so in practice \(w_p\in\{-1,0,1\}\).

The sketch below draws the two elementary defects on a \(6\times6\) patch, with the core at the centre of a plaquette. Following the spins once counter-clockwise around the dashed contour \(\mathcal C\), the arrow turns once counter-clockwise around a vortex (\(w=+1\)) and once clockwise around an antivortex (\(w=-1\)). No smooth deformation of the angles can change these integers; a vortex disappears only by meeting an antivortex.

4.2 Vortex energy

Far from its core a vortex is a smooth configuration, so the Gaussian (continuum) energy applies, \(E-E_0=\frac J2\int d^2r\,|\nabla\theta|^2\). With \(|\nabla\theta|^2=1/r^2\) (SymPy above, \(n=1\)) and the integral running from the core radius \(a\) (the lattice spacing) to the system size \(R\approx L\): \[ E_v=\frac J2\int_a^{R}\frac{1}{r^2}\,2\pi r\,dr+E_{\rm core}=\pi J\ln\frac{R}{a}+E_{\rm core}. \] A single vortex costs an energy that diverges logarithmically with the system size. A vortex–antivortex pair at distance \(d\) is different: far away (\(r\gg d\)) the two windings cancel, \(\nabla\theta\) decays as \(d/r^2\), the integral converges, and \[ E_{\rm pair}(d)\approx2\pi J\ln\frac da+2E_{\rm core}, \] independent of \(L\). Pairs are cheap, single vortices are not.

def open_energy(theta):
    # energy of an L x L patch with open boundaries: only bonds inside the patch
    return -J * (jnp.cos(theta[:, 1:] - theta[:, :-1]).sum() + jnp.cos(theta[1:, :] - theta[:-1, :]).sum())

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("n_steps",))
def relax_interior(theta, n_steps, lr=0.2):
    # gradient descent on the interior spins; boundary spins stay fixed
    mask = jnp.zeros_like(theta).at[1:-1, 1:-1].set(1.0)
    g = jax.grad(open_energy)
    return lax.fori_loop(0, n_steps, lambda i, th: th - lr * mask * g(th), theta)

4.3 Kosterlitz–Thouless free-energy argument

The energy alone would forbid single vortices, but the entropy also grows with the system size. The vortex core can sit on any of the \(\approx(L/a)^2\) plaquettes, so \(S_v=\ln(L/a)^2=2\ln(L/a)\), and the free energy of one free vortex is (Kosterlitz and Thouless, 1973) \[ F_v=E_v-TS_v=(\pi J-2T)\ln\frac La+\dots \] For \(T<\pi J/2\) the coefficient is positive, \(F_v\to+\infty\) as \(L\to\infty\): free vortices are suppressed and only bound pairs (finite energy) appear. For \(T>\pi J/2\), \(F_v\to-\infty\): free vortices proliferate. The estimate of the transition temperature is \[ T_{\rm KT}^{\rm (estimate)}=\frac{\pi J}{2}\approx1.571\,J . \]

The estimate is too high by about 76% (the ratio \(1.759\) printed above). The reason is physical: below \(T_{\rm BKT}\) the system is full of thermally excited bound pairs, which act like a polarisable medium (dipoles in a dielectric) and screen the interaction, so that a free vortex costs \(\pi\Upsilon\ln(L/a)\) with a reduced, renormalised stiffness \(\Upsilon<J\) instead of \(\pi J\ln(L/a)\). Replacing \(J\) by \(\Upsilon\) in the argument gives the self-consistency condition \(\Upsilon(T_{\rm BKT})=2T_{\rm BKT}/\pi\) — exactly the Nelson–Kosterlitz universal jump of Section 5, derived properly with the renormalisation group by Kosterlitz (1974) and Nelson and Kosterlitz (1977).

5. Helicity modulus (spin stiffness)

Neither \(\langle|\boldsymbol m|\rangle\) (zero for \(L\to\infty\)) nor the vortex density (smooth) distinguishes the two phases. The quantity that does is the response to a twist. In the low-temperature phase the spins resist being twisted, as a solid resists shear or a superfluid resists a phase gradient (superfluid density); in the high-temperature phase free vortices relax any twist and the resistance vanishes.

5.1 Definition and estimator

Impose a total twist \(\Delta\) of the angle across the system in the \(x\) direction, spread evenly over the \(L\) columns: replace every horizontal bond angle \(\theta_{r+\hat x}-\theta_r\) by \(\theta_{r+\hat x}-\theta_r-\alpha\) with \(\alpha=\Delta/L\). The helicity modulus \(\Upsilon\) is defined by the increase of the free energy, \[ \begin{aligned} F(\Delta)-F(0)&=\frac{\Upsilon}{2}\,\Delta^2+O(\Delta^4)\quad\text{(in two dimensions)},\quad\text{i.e.}\\ \Upsilon&=\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial\Delta^2}\Big|_0=\frac1{L^2}\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial\alpha^2}\Big|_0 . \end{aligned} \]

The sketch below shows the twist at low temperature. A total twist \(\Delta\) means that the right neighbour of the last column is the first column turned by \(\Delta\) (orange arrows). The spins respond by turning gradually, by \(\alpha=\Delta/L\) from one column to the next, so each horizontal bond costs about \(\frac J2\alpha^2\). At \(T=0\) the \(L^2\) horizontal bonds give \(F(\Delta)-F(0)=\frac J2\Delta^2\), that is \(\Upsilon=J\). At high temperature free vortices can unwind the twist, and then \(\Upsilon=0\).

5.2 Low-temperature stiffness

In the Gaussian theory each bond angle has \(\langle(\Delta^x)^2\rangle=T/(2J)\) (equipartition: \(N\) modes \(\times\,T/2\) shared by \(2N\) bonds, each with energy \(\frac J2(\Delta)^2\)), so \(\langle\cos\Delta^x\rangle\approx1-\frac T{4J}\). The current term vanishes at this order: with periodic boundaries the sum of \(\Delta^x\) along each row is exactly zero, so \(S_x=\sum\sin\Delta^x\approx\sum\Delta^x=0\). Therefore \[ \Upsilon\approx J-\frac T4+O(T^2). \] This is the anharmonic stiffness used in Section 3.3. Below we compare with the Monte Carlo data at the lowest temperatures, and run two extra temperatures, \(T=0.1\) and \(0.3\).

5.3 Universal jump

Nelson and Kosterlitz (1977) showed with the renormalisation group that in the thermodynamic limit the stiffness jumps from a universal value to zero: \[ \lim_{T\to T_{\rm BKT}^-}\Upsilon(T)=\frac{2}{\pi}\,T_{\rm BKT},\qquad \Upsilon(T)=0\ \text{ for }T>T_{\rm BKT}. \] This is the self-consistent version of the free-energy argument of Section 4.3 (a free vortex costs \(\pi\Upsilon\ln L\) and gains \(2T\ln L\)). For superfluid films \(\Upsilon\) is proportional to the superfluid density, hence the title of their paper.

On a finite lattice \(\Upsilon_L(T)\) is smooth and positive at all \(T\); it decreases with \(L\) above \(T_{\rm BKT}\) and the drop sharpens. This suggests the crossing method: define \(T^*(L)\) as the temperature where \(\Upsilon_L(T)\) crosses the line \(2T/\pi\); as \(L\to\infty\), \(T^*(L)\to T_{\rm BKT}\).

The approach to the limit is slow. Above \(T_{\rm BKT}\) the correlation length diverges as (Kosterlitz 1974) \(\xi\propto\exp\!\big(b/\sqrt{T-T_{\rm BKT}}\big)\), with a non-universal constant \(b\). The finite lattice “looks critical” as long as \(\xi\gtrsim L\); setting \(\xi=L\) gives \[ T^*(L)-T_{\rm BKT}\simeq\frac{b^2}{(\ln L)^2}, \] a very slow, logarithmic approach. We fit this form to our three sizes; with only \(L=16,32,48\) the extrapolation is rough.

6. Correlation function

The spin-wave theory (Section 3.4) predicts \(G(r)\propto r^{-\eta(T)}\) with \(\eta=T/(2\pi J)\) at low \(T\). Vortices modify this in two ways: below \(T_{\rm BKT}\) bound pairs renormalise \(J\to\Upsilon\), so \(\eta(T)=T/(2\pi\Upsilon(T))\); at \(T_{\rm BKT}\) the universal jump gives \[ \eta(T_{\rm BKT})=\frac{T_{\rm BKT}}{2\pi\cdot2T_{\rm BKT}/\pi}=\frac14 \] (Kosterlitz 1974). Above \(T_{\rm BKT}\) free vortices randomise the angle beyond a distance \(\xi\), and \(G(r)\propto e^{-r/\xi}\).

To measure the correlation function we use, for each snapshot, \(G(\boldsymbol r)=\frac1N\sum_i\cos(\theta_i-\theta_{i+\boldsymbol r})\), the real part of the circular autocorrelation of \(z_i=e^{i\theta_i}\), computed with fast Fourier transforms (FFT): \(G=\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{IFFT}(|\mathrm{FFT}(z)|^2)/N\). We average over snapshots and over the two lattice directions, \(r=0,\dots,L/2\).

Two tests follow. Below \(T_{\rm BKT}\) we test renormalised spin waves: on the same \(48\times48\) torus the Gaussian theory with \(J\) replaced by the measured stiffness \(\Upsilon\) predicts, with no free parameter, \[ \begin{aligned} G_{\rm SW}(r)&=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{T}{2\Upsilon}\,D_L(r)\Big),\\ D_L(r)&=\frac1N\sum_{\boldsymbol k\neq0}\frac{2-2\cos k_xr}{\lambda_{\boldsymbol k}}, \end{aligned} \] which contains the periodic images automatically. A direct power-law fit \(G\propto r^{-\eta}\) is unreliable on a lattice this small: the image at distance \(L-r\) and the short-distance corrections bend the curve on the log–log plot over the whole range \(r\le L/2\). Above \(T_{\rm BKT}\) we test exponential decay and fit \(G(r)=A\big[e^{-r/\xi}+e^{-(L-r)/\xi}\big]\) (with the periodic image) for \(r\ge2\).

def correlation(theta):
    # theta: (n, L, L) -> G(r), r = 0..L/2, averaged over samples and the x and y directions
    L = theta.shape[-1]
    z = np.exp(1j * theta.astype(np.float64))

    G2d = np.fft.ifft2(np.abs(Fz) ** 2).real.mean(0) / (L * L)          # (L, L), G2d[dy, dx]

def D_torus(L):

7. Specific heat

At an ordinary continuous transition (2D Ising) the specific heat diverges at \(T_c\). At the BKT transition the free energy is infinitely differentiable. The singular part of the free energy per site scales like \(\xi^{-2}\) (one \(k_BT\) per correlated area \(\xi^2\)), and with \(\xi\propto e^{b/\sqrt{t}}\), \(t=T-T_{\rm BKT}\), \[ f_{\rm sing}\propto e^{-2b/\sqrt t}, \] a function all of whose derivatives vanish as \(t\to0^+\) (an essential singularity). Every thermodynamic derivative, including \(c=-T\,\partial^2f/\partial T^2\), is continuous at \(T_{\rm BKT}\): the transition is “of infinite order”. The Monte Carlo data below show where the specific heat has its maximum.

    # winding number of every plaquette (lower-left corner (x, y)), corners traversed counter-clockwise

The phase diagram of the 2D XY model has a single control parameter, the temperature, and the figure below draws it as a temperature axis together with the quantity that distinguishes the two phases, the helicity modulus. Its exact low-temperature behaviour \(\Upsilon\approx J-T/4\) (Section 5.2) and its universal value \(2T_{\rm BKT}/\pi\) at the transition (Section 5.3, dashed line) are drawn exactly; the curve between them is schematic. Above \(T_{\rm BKT}=1/1.1199=0.8929\,J\) [Hasenbusch 2005], \(\Upsilon=0\).

The BKT transition is of a kind different from the Ising transitions of Labs 010–012 and 029. At a first-order transition a first derivative of the free energy jumps; at a continuous transition the first derivatives are continuous but the correlation length diverges as a power law, \(\xi\sim|T-T_c|^{-\nu}\), and the specific heat or other second derivatives diverge. At the BKT transition no symmetry is broken on either side, the correlation length diverges faster than any power, \(\xi\propto\exp\big(b/\sqrt{T-T_{\rm BKT}}\big)\), and the singular part of the free energy, \(f_{\rm sing}\propto e^{-2b/\sqrt t}\) (Section 7), has derivatives of all orders that vanish at \(T_{\rm BKT}\): every thermodynamic derivative is continuous. Such a transition is called a transition of infinite order. It is visible in the jump of the helicity modulus from \(2T_{\rm BKT}/\pi\) to zero and in the change from power-law to exponential decay of \(G(r)\). The dashed orange marker at \(T\approx1.06\) is the maximum of the specific heat for \(L=48\) (Section 7): a smooth maximum above \(T_{\rm BKT}\), not a transition.

Vortices are the relevant objects, but their density is not an order parameter either. The transition is the unbinding of pairs: below \(T_{\rm BKT}\) every vortex has an antivortex partner nearby; above, free vortices exist. This is a statement about the spatial arrangement of defects, not about their number.

These facts have direct consequences for the anomaly-detection study later in Lecture 5. An algorithm fed with raw angles (or with \((\cos\theta_i,\sin\theta_i)\)) will most easily pick up the magnetisation, the dominant variation between low- and high-temperature snapshots of a finite lattice. Whatever “transition” it finds from that signal is a finite-size crossover that drifts with \(L\) (left panel above), not \(T_{\rm BKT}\). The global rotation symmetry adds a second difficulty: two snapshots differing by \(\theta_i\to\theta_i+\alpha\) are physically identical, and a representation that is not invariant (raw angles) wastes capacity on this. A physics-informed input is the vortex map \(w_p\in\{-1,0,1\}\) per plaquette, which is rotation invariant and consists directly of the topological objects. Even then, a detector must be sensitive to how vortices are arranged (paired or free), since their density is smooth through \(T_{\rm BKT}\). Finally, any temperature an algorithm singles out should be compared with the reference values of this notebook: the stiffness crossings \(T^*(L)\), the specific-heat maximum (above \(T_{\rm BKT}\)), and the literature value \(T_{\rm BKT}=0.8929\).

(Hint: \(\partial F/\partial\alpha=\langle\partial H/\partial\alpha\rangle\).)

Solution

A twist by \(\alpha\) on every horizontal bond is the same as the substitution \(\theta_{x,y}\to\theta_{x,y}-\alpha x\) inside the bulk, but it cannot be removed on the torus because \(\alpha L\) is not a multiple of \(2\pi\). We write a twisted Metropolis sweep (only the horizontal neighbours carry the extra phase: the right neighbour is seen at angle \(\theta_{r+\hat x}-\alpha\), the left one at \(\theta_{r-\hat x}+\alpha\)) and use Metropolis only.

Code 
def twisted_field(theta, alpha):
    c = lambda a: jnp.cos(a); s = lambda a: jnp.sin(a)
    right, left = jnp.roll(theta, -1, -1) - alpha, jnp.roll(theta, 1, -1) + alpha
    up, down = jnp.roll(theta, -1, -2), jnp.roll(theta, 1, -2)
    return (c(right) + c(left) + c(up) + c(down), s(right) + s(left) + s(up) + s(down))

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("n_equil", "n_meas"))
def twisted_run(theta0, keys, beta, alpha, n_equil, n_meas):
    colour = checkerboard(theta0.shape[-1])
    def sweep(carry, _):
        theta, key = carry
        for c in (0, 1):
            key, k1, k2 = jax.random.split(key, 3)
            new = theta + (2.0 * jax.random.uniform(k1, theta.shape) - 1.0)
            hx, hy = twisted_field(theta, alpha)
            dE = -J * (hx * (jnp.cos(new) - jnp.cos(theta)) + hy * (jnp.sin(new) - jnp.sin(theta)))
            acc = (jax.random.uniform(k2, theta.shape) < jnp.exp(-beta * dE)) & (colour == c)
            theta = jnp.where(acc, jnp.mod(new, TWO_PI), theta)
        dx = jnp.roll(theta, -1, -1) - theta - alpha
        return (theta, key), -J * jnp.sin(dx).sum()                        # dH/dalpha
    def chain(theta, key):
        carry, _ = lax.scan(sweep, (theta, key), None, length=n_equil)
        _, I = lax.scan(sweep, carry, None, length=n_meas)
        return I.mean()
    return jax.vmap(chain)(theta0, keys)

Lx, Tx, ax_ = 16, 0.7, 0.03
t0 = time.time()
