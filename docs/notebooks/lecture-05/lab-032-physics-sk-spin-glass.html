Lab 032 — The Sherrington–Kirkpatrick spin glass

A ferromagnet below its critical temperature is easy to recognise: one look at a snapshot shows large domains of aligned spins, and the magnetisation \(m=\frac1N\sum_i s_i\) is non-zero. A spin glass also freezes at low temperature, every spin picks a preferred direction and keeps it for a very long time, but the frozen directions look random. A snapshot of a frozen spin glass is indistinguishable by eye from a snapshot of a hot, disordered paramagnet, and the magnetisation is zero in both. The order is real, but it is hidden.

This notebook is about the physics of the simplest model with this property, the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) model [Sherrington & Kirkpatrick 1975]: \(N\) Ising spins, every pair coupled by a random coupling of random sign. No deep learning is used here. The notebook is the physics foundation for the anomaly-detection study of spin-glass data later in Lecture 5, and it produces the Monte Carlo data set used there.

We start in Section 1 with the reasons to study such a model: disorder and frustration in magnetic alloys, hard optimisation problems, and energy landscapes in machine learning. Section 2 defines the model, explains the \(1/N\) scaling of the couplings and the gauge symmetry, and introduces the two kinds of averages, thermal and over the disorder; the annealed free energy is computed exactly there and compared with exact enumeration. Section 3 introduces frustration: a triangle with a negative product of couplings cannot satisfy all its bonds, and in the SK model half of all triangles are frustrated. Section 4 builds the language needed to describe order without a pattern: the Edwards–Anderson order parameter, the overlap between two replicas, its distribution \(P(q)\), the high-temperature spin-glass susceptibility, and the main results of the replica theory, which we state with their meaning and check numerically rather than derive. With these tools, Section 5 tests the transition at \(T_c=J\) with parallel-tempering Monte Carlo in JAX for \(N=32,64,128\), checked by an exact equilibration test: it measures \(P(q)\) and the Binder cumulant, whose crossings lie somewhat below \(T_c\) at these sizes and move towards it as \(N\) grows. Section 6 explores the energy landscape: ground-state energies from exact enumeration and from Monte Carlo compared with Parisi’s value, the number of metastable states, and slow dynamics. Section 7 contrasts the SK model with the Mattis model, whose random signs carry no frustration, so that it is a ferromagnet in disguise and never forms a glass. Section 8 shows that a single snapshot carries no visible order while the overlap field \(q_i=s_i^as_i^b\) of two replicas does; this is the bridge to the machine-learning notebook. A summary, exercises with solutions and references close the notebook.

Throughout, Boltzmann’s constant is \(k_B=1\) and \(\beta=1/T\). The coupling scale \(J\) is kept in the formulas and set to \(J=1\) in the code, so temperatures and energies are in units of \(J\). Square brackets \([\,\cdot\,]\) denote the average over the random couplings, angular brackets \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) the thermal average at fixed couplings.

1. Disorder and frustration

1.1 Glassy magnets

The name spin glass was coined for metallic alloys of a non-magnetic metal with a few percent of randomly substituted magnetic atoms, such as iron in gold [Cannella & Mydosh 1972] or manganese in copper. Experiments found a low-temperature phase in which the magnetic moments freeze in non-periodic directions, with a very slow, strongly history-dependent response to external perturbations [Binder & Young 1986; Billoire 2007].

The moments interact through the conduction electrons. A standard model of this interaction is the RKKY (Ruderman–Kittel–Kasuya–Yosida) coupling \[ J(r)\propto\frac{\cos(2k_F r)}{r^3}, \] with \(k_F\) the Fermi wave number. Its sign oscillates with the distance \(r\). Combined with random positions of the magnetic atoms, any such coupling produces couplings of random sign: some pairs prefer to be parallel (ferromagnetic, \(J>0\)), others antiparallel (antiferromagnetic, \(J<0\)). The positions are fixed when the alloy is made, so the couplings are random but frozen, or quenched. A short computation shows the random signs: \(n\) moments are placed uniformly at random in a cube, and we histogram the factor that carries the sign, \(\cos(2k_Fr_{ij})\), for the strongest couplings (pairs at distances \(1<r_{ij}<3\) in units of the lattice constant; \(k_F=3\), any value of order a few gives the same picture).

    return np.cos(2 * kF * r) / r**3

Both signs occur in comparable numbers (43% ferromagnetic and 57% antiferromagnetic pairs in this example). The SK model keeps exactly this ingredient, random signs, and drops the geometry: every spin is coupled to every other spin, with a Gaussian random coupling.

1.2 Hard optimisation problems

Finding the lowest-energy configuration of \(E(\boldsymbol s)=-\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}s_is_j\) for given couplings is a combinatorial optimisation problem over \(2^N\) configurations. With the couplings chosen appropriately, many classic hard problems of computer science (graph partitioning, maximum cut, satisfiability, all of Karp’s 21 NP-complete problems, where NP stands for nondeterministic polynomial time) can be expressed in exactly this form [Lucas 2014]; the computational complexity of finding spin-glass ground states was analysed early by Barahona [Barahona 1982]. Conversely, spin-glass physics produced general-purpose algorithms: simulated annealing [Kirkpatrick, Gelatt & Vecchi 1983] was proposed by one of the two authors of the SK model, and the parallel-tempering method used in Section 5 was designed for spin glasses [Hukushima & Nemoto 1996].

1.3 Energy landscapes in machine learning

A network of binary units with symmetric weights \(W_{ij}\) and energy \(E(\boldsymbol s)=-\frac12\sum_{ij}W_{ij}s_is_j\) is the Hopfield network [Hopfield 1982]: the same Hamiltonian, with couplings constructed from data instead of drawn at random. The loss function of a neural network is likewise a function on a very high-dimensional space with many local minima. The SK model is the solvable prototype of such a rugged landscape: exponentially many minima, barriers between them, and slow dynamics. The vocabulary developed for it (metastable states, overlaps between minima, replicas) is used throughout the theory of learning [Mézard & Montanari 2009; Charbonneau et al. 2023].

In this notebook the spins have no positions at all: the SK model has no lattice, so there is no spatial pattern to look at. We will see that the low-temperature phase is nevertheless sharply defined, and that the right tool to see it is a comparison of two independent copies of the system with the same couplings.

2. The Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model

2.1 The Hamiltonian

The model consists of \(N\) Ising spins \(s_i=\pm1\), \(i=1,\dots,N\), and every pair \((i,j)\) is coupled: \[ \begin{aligned} H(\boldsymbol s) &= -\sum_{i<j} J_{ij}\, s_i s_j = -\frac12\,\boldsymbol s^{T} J\, \boldsymbol s ,\\ J_{ij}&=J_{ji}\sim\mathcal N\!\left(0,\frac{J^2}{N}\right)\ \text{independent for } i<j,\qquad J_{ii}=0 . \end{aligned} \] Here \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) is the Gaussian distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). The mean is zero: ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic couplings are equally likely. A fixed matrix \(J\) is called a disorder realisation or sample.

The figure below draws one sample with \(N=8\) spins placed on a circle; the positions carry no meaning, since the model has no lattice. Every pair is connected: a solid blue line is a ferromagnetic coupling \(J_{ij}>0\), a dashed green line an antiferromagnetic coupling \(J_{ij}<0\), and the line width is proportional to \(|J_{ij}|\). The spins follow the convention of all sketches in this course (navy disc \(s_i=+1\), white disc with an orange rim \(s_i=-1\)).

Two quantities are used throughout. The local field on spin \(i\) is \(h_i=\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}s_j\), so that \(H=-\frac12\sum_i s_ih_i\). A flip \(s_i\to-s_i\) changes the sign of only the \(N-1\) terms containing \(s_i\), so the energy change of one flip is \(\Delta E_i = 2s_ih_i\). A configuration is therefore stable against every single flip if and only if \(s_ih_i>0\) for all \(i\), that is, if every spin is aligned with its local field.

2.2 Scaling of the couplings

The variance is chosen as \(J^2/N\), not \(J^2\), and the reason is the size of the local field. Take any fixed configuration \(\boldsymbol s\). Then \(J_{ij}s_j\) has the same Gaussian distribution as \(J_{ij}\) (the distribution is symmetric under \(J\to-J\)), so the local field is a sum of \(N-1\) independent Gaussians: \[ \operatorname{Var}(h_i) = (N-1)\,\sigma^2,\qquad \sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(J_{ij}). \] With \(\sigma^2=J^2/N\) we get \(\operatorname{Var}(h_i)=J^2(N-1)/N\to J^2\): the field on a spin stays of order one as \(N\) grows, temperatures of order \(J\) compete with it, and the transition temperature has a finite limit. Without the scaling, \(\sigma^2=J^2\), the field would grow like \(\sqrt N\,J\), and so would every characteristic temperature; there would be no thermodynamic limit. The contrast with the ferromagnet is instructive. A ferromagnet with all-to-all couplings needs \(J_{ij}=J_0/N\) (the Curie–Weiss model), because there the \(N\) terms add coherently, \(h_i=J_0m\). Random signs add incoherently, like a random walk, so the coupling is scaled by \(1/\sqrt N\) in magnitude, i.e. \(1/N\) in variance.

The energy of a configuration is \(H=-\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}s_is_j\), a sum of \(N(N-1)/2\) independent terms. For a random configuration it has mean \(0\) and variance \(\frac{N(N-1)}2\,\sigma^2 = \frac{(N-1)J^2}{2}\): a typical configuration has energy of order \(\sqrt N\). The low-energy configurations, which dominate at low temperature, have energies of order \(-N\) (Section 6); the energy is extensive.

We check these statements symbolically with SymPy (the Gaussian moments by direct integration, then the sums by independence) and then numerically.

2.3 Global flip and gauge symmetry

The Hamiltonian has two symmetries. The first is the global flip: \(H(-\boldsymbol s)=H(\boldsymbol s)\) for every sample, hence \(\langle s_i\rangle=0\) exactly at any finite \(N\). The second is a gauge transformation. Choose any signs \(\tau_i=\pm1\) and transform \[ s_i\to\tau_is_i,\qquad J_{ij}\to\tau_i\tau_jJ_{ij}. \] Each term \(J_{ij}s_is_j\to\tau_i^2\tau_j^2J_{ij}s_is_j=J_{ij}s_is_j\) is unchanged, so the energy of every configuration is unchanged. Because the Gaussian distribution of \(J_{ij}\) is symmetric, the transformed couplings \(\tau_i\tau_jJ_{ij}\) moreover have the same distribution as the original ones.

The gauge symmetry has a strong consequence. Every quantity averaged over the disorder must be invariant under the transformation. The magnetisation of a sample, \(\frac1N\sum_i\langle s_i\rangle\), is not invariant (\(s_i\to\tau_is_i\) flips arbitrary spins), so it carries no information about the ordering after averaging over samples. The invariant combinations are products in which every spin index appears twice, such as \(\langle s_is_j\rangle^2\) or, for two copies \(a,b\) of the system, \(s_i^as_i^b\). These are the building blocks of the spin-glass order parameter (Section 4). The cell below verifies the invariance symbolically for \(N=4\) and numerically for one sample of \(N=200\) spins, where the energy is unchanged and the magnetisation is not.

2.4 Quenched disorder

A spin glass has two sources of randomness, on two very different time scales. The first is thermal noise: at fixed couplings \(J\) the spins fluctuate, and thermal averages are Boltzmann averages at fixed \(J\), \[ \begin{aligned} \langle A\rangle_J&=\frac{1}{Z_J}\sum_{\boldsymbol s}A(\boldsymbol s)\,e^{-\beta H_J(\boldsymbol s)},\\ Z_J&=\sum_{\boldsymbol s}e^{-\beta H_J(\boldsymbol s)} . \end{aligned} \] The second is the disorder itself. The couplings are random but frozen (quenched): in the alloy the atoms do not move on the time scale of the spin dynamics. The average over realisations, \([X]=\int\!\prod_{i<j}dJ_{ij}\,P(J_{ij})\;X(J)\), is therefore taken after the thermal average, sample by sample.

The physically relevant free energy is the quenched average \[ f=-\frac{T}{N}\,[\ln Z_J], \] not the annealed one, \(f_{\rm ann}=-\frac{T}{N}\ln[Z_J]\), which would describe couplings that fluctuate and equilibrate together with the spins. The reason the quenched average is the meaningful one is self-averaging: as \(N\to\infty\), the free energy per spin of a single large sample, \(-\frac{T}{N}\ln Z_J\), converges to its disorder average. One large sample is representative. \(Z_J\) itself is not self-averaging: it is exponential in \(N\) and its average is dominated by rare samples.

The logarithm is concave, so Jensen’s inequality gives a strict ordering: \([\ln Z_J]\le\ln[Z_J]\), i.e. \(f\ge f_{\rm ann}\).

The annealed free energy, unlike the quenched one, can be computed exactly. The average of \(Z\) is easy, because the couplings are independent and appear in the exponent: \[ \begin{aligned} [Z_J] &= \sum_{\boldsymbol s}\prod_{i<j}\big[e^{\beta J_{ij}s_is_j}\big],\\ \big[e^{\beta J_{ij}s_is_j}\big]&=e^{\beta^2\sigma^2/2}\quad(\text{Gaussian integral; independent of } s_is_j=\pm1). \end{aligned} \] Every configuration contributes the same factor, so \([Z_J]=2^N\exp\!\big(\frac{N(N-1)}2\frac{\beta^2J^2}{2N}\big)\) and, for \(N\to\infty\), \[ \begin{aligned} -\beta f_{\rm ann}&=\ln2+\frac{\beta^2J^2}{4},\\ u_{\rm ann}&=-\frac{\beta J^2}2,\\ s_{\rm ann}&=\ln2-\frac{\beta^2J^2}{4}. \end{aligned} \] The annealed entropy becomes negative below \(T^*=J/(2\sqrt{\ln2})\). The entropy of a system with a finite number of discrete states is \(-\sum p\ln p\ge0\), so the annealed result must fail at low temperature. Aizenman, Lebowitz and Ruelle proved that for \(T>J\) the quenched free energy equals the annealed one in the limit \(N\to\infty\) [Aizenman, Lebowitz & Ruelle 1987]: the high-temperature phase is simple, and something happens at \(T=J\). We derive the annealed results with SymPy and then compare them with exact enumeration of small systems.

def half_configs(N):
    """All 2^(N-1) configurations with s_N = +1, shape (2^(N-1), N), float32."""
    k = jnp.arange(2 ** (N - 1), dtype=jnp.int32)
    bits = (k[:, None] >> jnp.arange(N - 1)) & 1
    return jnp.concatenate([2.0 * bits - 1.0, jnp.ones((k.size, 1))], axis=1)

@jax.jit
def enumerate_sample(J, betas, betas_chi):
    """Exact thermodynamics of one sample J (N, N).
    Returns ln Z (n_T,), U (n_T,), E0, number of single-flip-stable configurations, chi_SG (n_chi,)."""
    N = J.shape[0]
    S = half_configs(N)                                        # (M, N)
    Hf = S @ J                                                 # (M, N) local fields
    E = -0.5 * jnp.sum(S * Hf, axis=1)                         # (M,) energies
    n_stable = 2 * jnp.sum(jnp.all(S * Hf > 0, axis=1))        # x2 for the mirror images
    logw = -betas[:, None] * E[None, :]                        # (n_T, M)
    lse = jax.scipy.special.logsumexp(logw, axis=1)
    p = jnp.exp(logw - lse[:, None])                           # Boltzmann probabilities on the half space
    logZ = jnp.log(2.0) + lse
    U = p @ E
    # spin-glass susceptibility chi_SG = (1/N) sum_ij <s_i s_j>^2 at the temperatures betas_chi
    pc = jnp.exp(-betas_chi[:, None] * E[None, :] - jax.scipy.special.logsumexp(-betas_chi[:, None] * E[None, :], axis=1)[:, None])
    C = jnp.einsum("bm,mi,mj->bij", pc, S, S)                  # (n_chi, N, N) correlation matrices
    chi = jnp.sum(C**2, axis=(1, 2)) / N
    return logZ, U, jnp.min(E), n_stable, chi

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("n_chunk",))
def ground_state_chunked(J, n_chunk):
    """E0 and the number of single-flip-stable configurations, enumerating in n_chunk chunks (for N = 22, 24)."""
    N = J.shape[0]
    M = 2 ** (N - 1); m = M // n_chunk
    def body(carry, c):
        k = c * m + jnp.arange(m, dtype=jnp.int32)
        bits = (k[:, None] >> jnp.arange(N - 1)) & 1
        S = jnp.concatenate([2.0 * bits - 1.0, jnp.ones((m, 1))], axis=1)
        Hf = S @ J
        E = -0.5 * jnp.sum(S * Hf, axis=1)
        return (jnp.minimum(carry[0], E.min()), carry[1] + jnp.sum(jnp.all(S * Hf > 0, axis=1))), None
    (E0, n), _ = lax.scan(body, (jnp.inf, 0), jnp.arange(n_chunk))
    return E0, 2 * n

3.2 Loop products

The triangle suggests a general criterion, and it can be found without enumerating anything. Multiply the three bond terms around the triangle: \[ (J_{12}s_1s_2)(J_{23}s_2s_3)(J_{31}s_3s_1)=J_{12}J_{23}J_{31}\,s_1^2s_2^2s_3^2=J_{12}J_{23}J_{31}. \] The product of the bond terms does not depend on the spins. A satisfied bond has a positive term, so if all three bonds were satisfied the product would be positive. Hence: if \(J_{12}J_{23}J_{31}<0\), at least one bond is broken in every configuration (in fact an odd number). The argument works for any closed loop of bonds, since every spin on a loop appears in exactly two bond terms. The product around a loop is also gauge invariant: \(\tau_i\tau_jJ_{ij}\) multiplies the product by \(\prod\tau^2=1\). Frustration is a property of the couplings that no relabelling of spins can remove; the signs of individual couplings are not.

For couplings of arbitrary magnitudes \(a,b,c>0\) and signs with a negative product, the ground-state energy is \(-(a+b+c)+2\min(a,b,c)\): the weakest bond is the one that breaks. SymPy confirms both statements.

3.3 Frustrated triangles

In the SK model every triple \((i,j,k)\) forms a triangle. The product of three independent couplings with symmetric distributions is negative with probability \(1/2\) (an odd number of negative factors), so half of the \(\binom N3\) triangles are frustrated. For a large \(N\) we count them without a triple loop. Let \(\sigma_{ij}=\operatorname{sign}J_{ij}\) (with \(\sigma_{ii}=0\)). Then \[ \operatorname{Tr}\sigma^3=\sum_{i,j,k}\sigma_{ij}\sigma_{jk}\sigma_{ki} \] runs over ordered triples of distinct indices (the zero diagonal removes the others), and each triangle appears \(3!=6\) times. Hence \[ \begin{aligned} n_{\rm unfrustrated}-n_{\rm frustrated}&=\tfrac16\operatorname{Tr}\sigma^3,\\ n_{\rm unfrustrated}+n_{\rm frustrated}&=\binom N3 . \end{aligned} \] We compare three coupling matrices: the SK couplings, the ferromagnet \(J_{ij}=J/N\), and the Mattis couplings \(J_{ij}=J\xi_i\xi_j/N\) with random \(\xi_i=\pm1\) (Section 7), which have random signs too. For \(N=30\) the trace formula is first compared with an explicit loop over triangles.

Code 
def frustrated_fraction(J):
    """Fraction of frustrated triangles from Tr(sign(J)^3), J of shape (N, N) with zero diagonal."""
    N = J.shape[0]
    sg = np.sign(np.asarray(J, dtype=np.float64))
    diff = np.trace(sg @ sg @ sg) / 6                         # n_unfrustrated - n_frustrated
    total = N * (N - 1) * (N - 2) / 6
    return (total - diff) / 2 / total

def frustrated_fraction_loop(J):
    """The same by an explicit loop over all triples (for checking)."""
    J = np.asarray(J); N = J.shape[0]
    f = [J[i, j] * J[j, k] * J[k, i] < 0 for i, j, k in itertools.combinations(range(N), 3)]
    return np.mean(f)

def mattis_couplings(xi, J=1.0):
    """Mattis couplings J xi_i xi_j / N, zero diagonal; xi of shape (N,)."""
    N = xi.shape[0]
    return J * (np.outer(xi, xi) - np.eye(N)) / N

4. Order without a pattern

4.1 Frozen spins with zero magnetisation

In a ferromagnet below \(T_c\) the thermal averages \(\langle s_i\rangle\) are all equal, and the magnetisation \(m=\frac1N\sum_i\langle s_i\rangle\) measures the order. In a spin glass below its freezing temperature each spin also has a non-zero average \(\langle s_i\rangle\) inside the valley the system is trapped in, but the signs of the \(\langle s_i\rangle\) follow the random couplings, not a common direction. Their sum is a sum of \(N\) terms of random sign, so \(m\) is of order \(1/\sqrt N\) and vanishes for large \(N\). (After averaging over the disorder, \([\langle s_i\rangle]=0\) exactly by the gauge symmetry of Section 2.3.) The SK model has no lattice either, so a snapshot cannot show domains or stripes: the labels \(i\) carry no geometry, and any permutation of the labels (with the couplings permuted accordingly) describes the same system. A snapshot of the frozen phase looks like noise.

4.2 The Edwards–Anderson order parameter

Edwards and Anderson proposed to measure the freezing itself, regardless of the directions [Edwards & Anderson 1975]: \[ q_{\rm EA}=\frac1N\sum_i\langle s_i\rangle^2 . \] It is zero in the paramagnet (\(\langle s_i\rangle=0\)) and positive when the spins are frozen; it is invariant under the gauge transformation. There is a practical difficulty. In a finite system \(\langle s_i\rangle=0\) exactly (global flip symmetry), and \(\langle s_i\rangle\) restricted to “the valley we are in” is not something a simulation or an experiment can define directly.

4.3 Two replicas and the overlap

The way out is to simulate two independent copies \(a\) and \(b\) of the system, called replicas, with the same couplings \(J\) and independent thermal noise. Their joint distribution is the product \(p_J(\boldsymbol s^a)\,p_J(\boldsymbol s^b)\). The overlap \[ q_{ab}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^Ns_i^as_i^b\ \in[-1,1] \] is \(+1\) for identical configurations, \(-1\) for mirror images, and close to \(0\) for unrelated ones; the fraction of spins on which the two replicas differ is \((1-q_{ab})/2\).

Because the replicas are independent at fixed \(J\), \[ \begin{aligned} \langle q_{ab}^2\rangle&=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i,j}\langle s_i^as_j^a\rangle\langle s_i^bs_j^b\rangle\\ &=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i,j}\langle s_is_j\rangle^2 . \end{aligned} \] The global flip makes \(\langle q_{ab}\rangle=0\) at finite \(N\), but \(\langle q_{ab}^2\rangle\) is not affected: it measures the frozen correlations. If the system sits in one of two mirror valleys with averages \(\pm\langle s_i\rangle\), then \(q_{ab}=\pm q_{\rm EA}\) and \(\langle q^2_{ab}\rangle=q_{\rm EA}^2\).

The full information is in the overlap distribution of a sample and its disorder average, \[ P_J(q)=\big\langle\delta(q-q_{ab})\big\rangle_J,\qquad P(q)=\big[P_J(q)\big]. \] In the paramagnet \(q_{ab}\) is a sum of \(N\) weakly correlated terms \(\pm1/N\), so \(P(q)\) is a single Gaussian peak at \(q=0\) of width of order \(1/\sqrt N\). In the spin glass, by contrast, \(P(q)\) has weight at \(q\neq0\) that does not shrink as \(N\) grows (Sections 4.5 and 5).

The spin-glass susceptibility collects the frozen correlations, \[ \chi_{\rm SG}=\frac1N\sum_{i,j}\big[\langle s_is_j\rangle^2\big]=N\,\big[\langle q_{ab}^2\rangle\big], \] and in the paramagnet it is the width of \(P(q)\): \([\langle q^2\rangle]=\chi_{\rm SG}/N\).

4.4 Spin-glass susceptibility

At high temperature \(\chi_{\rm SG}\) can be computed by an expansion in powers of \(\beta\), and the result tells us where the paramagnet ends. Write each Boltzmann factor as \(e^{\beta J_{ij}s_is_j}=\cosh(\beta J_{ij})\,(1+t_{ij}s_is_j)\) with \(t_{ij}=\tanh(\beta J_{ij})\approx\beta J_{ij}\) (small, of order \(1/\sqrt N\)). Expanding the product, \(\langle s_is_j\rangle\) is a sum over paths of bonds from \(i\) to \(j\), each path contributing the product of its \(t\)’s. Now square and average over the disorder. Cross terms between two different paths contain some coupling an odd number of times, and \([J_{kl}]=0\) removes them, so only the squares of single paths survive. A path of length \(\ell\) contributes \(\prod[t^2]\approx(\beta^2J^2/N)^\ell\), and the number of paths of length \(\ell\) from \(i\) to \(j\) through distinct intermediate sites is \((N-2)(N-3)\cdots(N-\ell)\approx N^{\ell-1}\); summing over the end point \(j\) gives another factor \(N\). Therefore, to leading order in \(1/N\), \[ \begin{aligned} \chi_{\rm SG}&=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}N^{\ell}\Big(\frac{\beta^2J^2}{N}\Big)^{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty(\beta J)^{2\ell}\\ &=\frac{1}{1-\beta^2J^2}\qquad(T>J). \end{aligned} \] The series diverges at \(\beta J=1\): the paramagnet becomes unstable at \(T_c=J\). This is the value of the SK critical temperature (the replica solution below uses the same convention, \(T_c=J\), as e.g. [Crisanti & Rizzo 2002]). SymPy checks the two ingredients, the path count and the geometric sum; then we compare with exact enumeration.

4.5 Exact overlap distribution

The susceptibility is only the second moment of \(P(q)\); for a small system we can compute the whole distribution. 4.6 Replica-symmetric solution

Sherrington and Kirkpatrick computed \([\ln Z]\) with the replica trick [Sherrington & Kirkpatrick 1975]. The identity \(\ln Z=\lim_{n\to0}(Z^n-1)/n\) replaces the hard average of a logarithm by averages of \(Z^n\), the partition function of \(n\) replicas with the same couplings, which are Gaussian integrals as in Section 2.4. Averaging couples the replicas through the overlaps \(q_{ab}\). The simplest assumption, replica symmetry (RS), is that all overlaps are equal, \(q_{ab}=q\). We do not repeat the calculation; its result has a transparent meaning.

The central result is a self-consistency equation for \(q\), which can be understood directly. In a frozen state, spin \(i\) feels the field of the other spins with \(i\) itself removed (the cavity field), \(h_i=\sum_jJ_{ij}\langle s_j\rangle\). It is a sum of \(N\) terms with random signs, hence Gaussian, with variance \(\sum_jJ_{ij}^2\langle s_j\rangle^2\approx J^2q\). The spin then has \(\langle s_i\rangle=\tanh(\beta h_i)\), and averaging \(\langle s_i\rangle^2\) over the Gaussian field closes the loop: \[ q=\int\!Dz\;\tanh^2\!\big(\beta J\sqrt q\,z\big),\qquad Dz=\frac{dz}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}, \] \[ \begin{aligned} -\beta f_{\rm RS}&=\frac{\beta^2J^2}{4}(1-q)^2+\int\!Dz\,\ln\!\big(2\cosh\beta J\sqrt q\,z\big),\\ u_{\rm RS}&=-\frac{\beta J^2}{2}\big(1-q^2\big). \end{aligned} \] At \(q=0\) this is exactly the annealed result of Section 2.4. (The same expression is the constant-\(q\) case of Parisi’s free energy given in [Crisanti & Rizzo 2002].)

Linearising the equation gives the critical temperature. For small \(q\), \(\tanh^2x\approx x^2\) and \(\int Dz\,z^2=1\), so \(q\approx\beta^2J^2q\): a non-zero solution appears exactly when \(\beta J>1\). Expanding one order further gives \(q\approx\tau\) with \(\tau=1-T/J\): the Edwards–Anderson order parameter grows linearly below \(T_c\). SymPy does the expansion.

def rs_solution(T):
    """Replica-symmetric q, f, u, s at temperature T (J = 1)."""
    b = 1 / T
    F = lambda q: q - gauss_avg(lambda z: np.tanh(b * np.sqrt(q) * z) ** 2)

    u = -b * (1 - q**2) / 2
    return q, -T * mbf, u, mbf + b * u

4.7 Parisi’s solution

Parisi found the correct solution by breaking the replica symmetry in infinitely many steps [Parisi 1979; Parisi 1980]; his free energy was later proved rigorously to be exact [Talagrand 2006]. We do not derive it, but its main results have a clear meaning, and the rest of the notebook tests them.

The order parameter becomes a function. Instead of one number \(q\), the solution is a non-decreasing function \(q(x)\), \(0\le x\le1\), whose inverse defines the overlap distribution, \(P(q)=dx/dq\) [Parisi 1983; Crisanti & Rizzo 2002]. Its physical content is that below \(T_c\) the Boltzmann distribution of a large sample splits into many “pure states” (valleys) \(\alpha\) with weights \(w_\alpha\). Two replicas fall into states \(\alpha,\beta\) independently, and \(P_J(q)=\sum_{\alpha\beta}w_\alpha w_\beta\,\delta(q-q_{\alpha\beta})\). Pairs in the same state give the self-overlap \(q_{\rm EA}\); pairs in different states give smaller overlaps. Hence \(P(q)\) has peaks at \(\pm q_{\rm EA}\) and a continuous part in between, not a single pair of delta functions as in a ferromagnet [Mézard, Parisi & Virasoro 1986]. The weights \(w_\alpha\) differ from sample to sample, so \(P_J(q)\) differs between samples even for large \(N\).

The cartoons below compare the overlap distributions met so far, for \(N\to\infty\); they are schematic shapes, not computed curves. In the paramagnet two replicas are unrelated and \(P(q)\) is a single delta function at \(q=0\). A ferromagnet, and the Mattis model of Section 7, has two mirror states and two delta peaks at \(\pm m^2\). The replica-symmetric ansatz of Section 4.6 predicts the same shape, two delta peaks at \(\pm q\). Parisi’s solution adds a continuous part between the peaks at \(\pm q_{\rm EA}\), the Edwards–Anderson order parameter of Section 4.2: replicas that fall into different valleys have overlaps smaller than \(q_{\rm EA}\). Section 5.6 measures \(P(q)\) at finite \(N\).

5.1 Parallel tempering

Testing these predictions requires equilibrium samples at low temperature, and this is where spin glasses are hard. Below \(T_c\) the free-energy landscape has many valleys separated by barriers that grow with \(N\). A Metropolis chain at a fixed low temperature (single spin flips, accepted with probability \(\min(1,e^{-\beta\Delta E})\)) stays in one valley for a very long time: the samples it produces are correct locally but do not represent the relative weights of the valleys, and the estimate of \(P(q)\), which is all about different valleys, is wrong. Section 6.3 shows this directly.

At low temperature a chain of single spin flips stays in one valley; at high temperature it crosses the barriers easily, but it does not settle in the deep valleys.

Parallel tempering (also called replica exchange or exchange Monte Carlo) [Swendsen & Wang 1986; Hukushima & Nemoto 1996; Earl & Deem 2005] runs \(K\) copies of the same sample at temperatures \(T_1<T_2<\dots<T_K\). The joint distribution of the copies is the product of their Boltzmann distributions, \[ \Pi(\boldsymbol s_1,\dots,\boldsymbol s_K)=\prod_{k=1}^K\frac{e^{-\beta_kE(\boldsymbol s_k)}}{Z(\beta_k)} . \] Two kinds of moves alternate. In a local move every copy performs one Metropolis sweep at its own temperature; each copy is updated with a move that preserves its own Boltzmann distribution, so \(\Pi\) is preserved. In an exchange move we propose, for neighbouring temperatures \(k,k+1\), to swap the two configurations, and accept with probability \[ \min\Big(1,\ \exp\big[(\beta_k-\beta_{k+1})(E_k-E_{k+1})\big]\Big), \] where \(E_k\) is the energy of the configuration currently at temperature \(T_k\). This is the Metropolis rule for the swap: the ratio \(\Pi(\text{after})/\Pi(\text{before})\) equals the exponential (SymPy below), so detailed balance holds and \(\Pi\) is preserved.

The exchanges let a configuration do a random walk in temperature. At high temperature it crosses barriers easily; when it comes back down it can land in a different valley. The swap is accepted often when the energy histograms of neighbouring temperatures overlap, which sets how dense the temperature grid must be. For the replica overlap we need two independent parallel-tempering simulations of the same sample (replicas \(a\) and \(b\)), each with its own \(K\) copies.

The sketch below shows the scheme for \(K=4\) temperatures: every copy is updated by Metropolis sweeps at its own temperature, and copies at neighbouring temperatures propose to exchange their configurations with the acceptance probability above.

5.2 Implementation

One simulation handles \(D\) disorder samples, \(2\) replicas and \(K\) temperatures at once. For one sample the \(C=2K\) chains (replica \(r\), temperature slot \(k\), column \(c=rK+k\)) are the columns of a matrix, and jax.vmap maps the per-sample code over the \(D\) samples. The arrays are:

array shape meaning
J (D, N, N) couplings of the \(D\) samples
s (D, N, 2, K) spins: sample \(d\), spin \(i\), replica \(r\in\{a,b\}\), temperature slot \(k\) (reshaped to (D, N, C) for the sweep)
h (D, N, 2, K) local fields \(h_i=\sum_jJ_{ij}s_j\) of every chain, kept up to date
beta (K,) inverse temperatures, the same for every sample and replica; beta_c = tile(beta, 2) has shape (C,)
lab (D, 2, K) label of the configuration in each temperature slot (to follow its walk in temperature)

All spins of the SK model are coupled to each other, so two spins cannot be flipped simultaneously: the energy change of one flip depends on all other spins. A sweep therefore visits the spins one after another. After a flip of spin \(i\) by \(\Delta s_i=-2s_i\) every local field changes, \(h_j\to h_j+J_{ji}\Delta s_i\), which is \(O(N)\) work per flip and \(O(N^2)\) per sweep. Done literally, spin by spin, this reads and writes the whole field array \(N\) times per sweep. We organise the same sequential sweep in blocks of \(b=16\) spins. Inside a block the spins are visited one by one, and each flip updates only the fields of the \(b\) spins of the block (a small (b, C) array, couplings J_bb of shape (b, b)). After the block, the fields of all \(N\) spins are updated at once by one matrix product, \(h\leftarrow h+J_{:,\text{block}}\,\Delta s_{\text{block}}\), with shapes (N, b) @ (b, C). Every spin still sees the exact current field when it is visited, so this is exactly the sequential Metropolis sweep, with the expensive part done as matrix products.

In the exchange step, for one sample and one replica, the \(K\) configurations are permuted: slot \(k\) receives the configuration from slot \(k+1\) and vice versa if the swap is accepted. Even pairs \((0,1),(2,3),\dots\) and odd pairs \((1,2),(3,4),\dots\) alternate between sweeps, so the attempted swaps never overlap.

from math import gcd

def sweep_sample(s, h, J, beta_c, u, block):
    """One sequential Metropolis sweep of all chains of one sample.
    s, h: (N, C) spins and local fields, one chain per column; J: (N, N); beta_c: (C,) inverse temperature of each chain;
    u: (N, C) uniform random numbers. Spins are visited in the order i = 0, ..., N-1, in blocks of `block` spins."""
    N, C = s.shape
    thr = -jnp.log(u) / (2.0 * beta_c)                             # (N, C): flip spin i iff s_i h_i < thr_i, i.e. u < exp(-2 beta s_i h_i)
    def do_block(kb, carry):
        s, h = carry
        i0 = kb * block
        s_old = lax.dynamic_slice_in_dim(s, i0, block, 0)          # (b, C) spins of the block
        hb = lax.dynamic_slice_in_dim(h, i0, block, 0)             # (b, C) their fields at the start of the block
        tb = lax.dynamic_slice_in_dim(thr, i0, block, 0)           # (b, C) acceptance thresholds
        J_cols = lax.dynamic_slice_in_dim(J, i0, block, 1)         # (N, b) couplings of every spin to the block
        J_bb = lax.dynamic_slice_in_dim(J_cols, i0, block, 0)      # (b, b) couplings inside the block
        rows = []
        for j in range(block):                                     # sequential inside the block (unrolled by jit)
            sj = s_old[j]
            accept = sj * hb[j] < tb[j]                            # Metropolis rule, Delta E = 2 s_j h_j
            d = jnp.where(accept, -2.0 * sj, 0.0)                  # (C,) change of s_j, 0 if rejected
            hb = hb + J_bb[:, j:j + 1] * d[None, :]                # fields of the block spins after this flip
            rows.append(sj + d)
        s_new = jnp.stack(rows)                                    # (b, C)
        s = lax.dynamic_update_slice_in_dim(s, s_new, i0, 0)
        h = h + J_cols @ (s_new - s_old)                           # all N fields, one matrix product per block
        return s, h
    return lax.fori_loop(0, N // block, do_block, (s, h))

def swap_moves(s, h, lab, E, beta, u, parity):
    """Exchange moves for all samples and replicas. s, h: (D, N, 2, K); lab, E: (D, 2, K); beta: (K,); u: (D, 2, K-1).
    Pairs (k, k+1) with k % 2 == parity are attempted; returns the permuted arrays and the accepted swaps (D, 2, K-1)."""
    K = beta.shape[0]
    log_ratio = (beta[:-1] - beta[1:]) * (E[..., :-1] - E[..., 1:])            # (D, 2, K-1)
    acc = (jnp.log(u) < log_ratio) & (jnp.arange(K - 1) % 2 == parity)
    pad = jnp.zeros(acc.shape[:-1] + (1,), bool)
    up = jnp.concatenate([acc, pad], -1).astype(jnp.int32)                     # slot k takes from k+1
    down = jnp.concatenate([pad, acc], -1).astype(jnp.int32)                   # slot k+1 takes from k
    perm = jnp.arange(K) + up - down                                           # (D, 2, K)
    take = lambda a: jnp.take_along_axis(a, perm[:, None], axis=3)             # permute the K axis of (D, N, 2, K)
    return take(s), take(h), jnp.take_along_axis(lab, perm, 2), jnp.take_along_axis(E, perm, 2), acc

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("n_equil", "n_save", "n_meas", "meas_every", "use_swaps"))
def parallel_tempering(J, s0, beta, key, n_equil, n_save, n_meas, meas_every, use_swaps=True):
    """Parallel tempering for D samples x 2 replicas x K temperatures; s0: (D, 2, K, N) initial spins.
    Equilibration: n_equil sweeps. Production: n_save blocks of n_meas measurements, one every meas_every sweeps;
    one configuration per slot is stored at the end of every block. Returns a dict of arrays (shapes in the comments)."""
    D, R, K, N = s0.shape
    C = R * K
    sweep_all = jax.vmap(partial(sweep_sample, block=gcd(N, 16)), in_axes=(0, 0, 0, None, 0))   # over the D samples
    beta_c = jnp.tile(beta, R)                                                  # (C,) column c = r K + k
    s_init = jnp.transpose(s0, (0, 3, 1, 2))                                   # (D, N, 2, K)
    h_init = jnp.einsum("dij,djrk->dirk", J, s_init)
    carry0 = dict(s=s_init, h=h_init, lab=jnp.broadcast_to(jnp.arange(K), (D, R, K)),
                  emin=jnp.full((D, R), jnp.inf), smin=s0[:, :, 0], acc=jnp.zeros(K - 1), t=0, key=key)

    def one_sweep(c, _):
        key, k1, k2 = jax.random.split(c["key"], 3)
        s, h = sweep_all(c["s"].reshape(D, N, C), c["h"].reshape(D, N, C), J, beta_c, jax.random.uniform(k1, (D, N, C)))
        s, h = s.reshape(D, N, R, K), h.reshape(D, N, R, K)
        E = -0.5 * jnp.sum(s * h, axis=1)                                      # (D, 2, K) energies
        lab, acc = c["lab"], jnp.zeros((D, R, K - 1), bool)
        if use_swaps:
            s, h, lab, E, acc = swap_moves(s, h, lab, E, beta, jax.random.uniform(k2, (D, R, K - 1)), c["t"] % 2)
        k_low = jnp.argmin(E, -1)                                              # (D, 2) lowest-energy slot
        s_low = jnp.take_along_axis(s, k_low[:, None, :, None], axis=3)[..., 0]   # (D, N, 2)
        better = E.min(-1) < c["emin"]
        q = jnp.mean(s[:, :, 0] * s[:, :, 1], axis=1)                          # (D, K) overlaps of the two replicas
        new = dict(s=s, h=h, lab=lab, emin=jnp.where(better, E.min(-1), c["emin"]),
                   smin=jnp.where(better[..., None], jnp.swapaxes(s_low, 1, 2), c["smin"]),
                   acc=c["acc"] + acc.sum((0, 1)), t=c["t"] + 1, key=key)
        trace = (E.mean((0, 1)) / N, (q**2).mean(0), lab[0, 0].astype(jnp.int8))   # [E]/N (K,), [q^2] (K,), labels (K,)
        return new, trace

    def measure(c, _):
        c, tr = lax.scan(one_sweep, c, None, length=meas_every)
        q_int = jnp.sum(c["s"][:, :, 0] * c["s"][:, :, 1], axis=1).astype(jnp.int16)   # (D, K): N q
        return c, (tr, q_int)

    def block(c, _):
        c, (tr, q_int) = lax.scan(measure, c, None, length=n_meas)
        return c, (tr, q_int, jnp.transpose(c["s"], (0, 2, 3, 1)).astype(jnp.int8))   # snapshot (D, 2, K, N)

    c, tr_eq = lax.scan(one_sweep, carry0, None, length=n_equil)
    c, (tr_pr, q_int, conf) = lax.scan(block, c, None, length=n_save)
    flat = lambda a: a.reshape((-1,) + a.shape[3:])                             # (n_save, n_meas, meas_every, K) -> (sweeps, K)
    traces = [jnp.concatenate([tr_eq[i], flat(tr_pr[i])]) for i in range(3)]
    return dict(e_trace=traces[0], q2_trace=traces[1], label_trace=traces[2],

5.4 An exact energy–overlap identity

For \(N=128\) no exact answer is available, so equilibration has to be checked in another way. For Gaussian couplings there is an exact identity between the energy and the overlap [Katzgraber, Palassini & Young 2001], and it rests on two ingredients. The first is Gaussian integration by parts (Stein’s lemma): for \(x\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) and a smooth function \(g\), \(\ [x\,g(x)]=\sigma^2\,[g'(x)]\). It follows from \(\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}=-\frac{x}{\sigma^2}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}\) and one integration by parts. The second is the derivative of a correlation with respect to its own coupling. Since \(J_{ij}\) enters \(Z\) through \(e^{\beta J_{ij}s_is_j}\), \(\partial\langle s_is_j\rangle/\partial J_{ij}=\beta\big(\langle(s_is_j)^2\rangle-\langle s_is_j\rangle^2\big)=\beta\big(1-\langle s_is_j\rangle^2\big)\).

Apply both to the disorder-averaged energy, \([U]=-\sum_{i<j}[J_{ij}\langle s_is_j\rangle]=-\sum_{i<j}\sigma^2\beta\big(1-[\langle s_is_j\rangle^2]\big)\), and use \(\sum_{i<j}\langle s_is_j\rangle^2=\frac12\big(N^2\langle q^2\rangle-N\big)\) (Section 4.3). With \(\sigma^2=J^2/N\) everything combines into \[ \boxed{\;\frac{[U]}{N}=-\frac{\beta J^2}{2}\Big(1-\big[\langle q^2\rangle\big]\Big)\;} \] exactly, for every \(N\) and every temperature. In a simulation that starts from random configurations the two sides approach the equilibrium value from opposite directions: the energy decreases from \(0\), while the right-hand side starts near \(-\beta J^2/2\) (no overlap yet) and increases. Equality of the two sides (within errors) is a necessary condition for equilibrium. At high temperature \([\langle q^2\rangle]\to0\) and the identity reduces to the annealed \(u=-\beta J^2/2\).

5.5 The Monte Carlo data set

The production simulation uses \(K=25\) temperatures \(T=0.40,0.45,\dots,1.60\) (in units of \(J\)), symmetric around \(T_c=1\) in the sense that the grid reaches \(0.6\,J\) above and below. The lowest temperature is limited by the equilibration time, which grows quickly as \(T\to0\).

For each size \(N=32,64,128\) we simulate \(D\) disorder samples, two replicas and \(K\) temperatures: n_equil equilibration sweeps, then a production run in which the overlap is measured every meas_every sweeps and one configuration per temperature and replica is stored every n_meas * meas_every sweeps. 5.6 The overlap distribution

We can now measure the central object of the theory. From the measured overlaps we build \(P_J(q)\) for every sample (a histogram over the \(N+1\) possible values \(q=-1,-1+2/N,\dots,1\)) and average over samples. We plot the density \(P(q)\), i.e. the probability of each value divided by the spacing \(2/N\), so that curves for different \(N\) can be compared. The global flip symmetry makes every \(P_J(q)\) exactly symmetric, \(P_J(q)=P_J(-q)\); a finite run visits the mirror valleys only a limited number of times, so we symmetrise the histogram of each sample, which uses this exact symmetry to reduce the noise.

Code 
def overlap_hist(d):
    """P_J(q) for every sample and temperature: array (D, K, N+1) of probabilities of q = -1, -1+2/N, ..., 1,
    symmetrised, P_J(q) -> [P_J(q) + P_J(-q)]/2 (an exact symmetry; it halves the statistical noise of a finite run)."""
    N = int(d["N"])
    idx = (d["q_int"].astype(np.int64) + N) // 2                               # 0 .. N
    D, K, n = idx.shape
    P = np.zeros((D, K, N + 1))
    np.add.at(P, (np.arange(D)[:, None, None], np.arange(K)[None, :, None], idx), 1.0)
    return 0.5 * (P + P[..., ::-1]) / n

5.7 Susceptibility and Binder cumulant

The overlap distribution also gives the two standard quantities for locating the transition. The spin-glass susceptibility \(\chi_{\rm SG}=N[\langle q^2\rangle]\) should approach \(1/(1-\beta^2J^2)\) above \(T_c\) (Section 4.4); below \(T_c\), where \([\langle q^2\rangle]\) stays finite, it grows proportionally to \(N\).

A sharper tool is the Binder cumulant, the dimensionless ratio \[ g(T,N)=\frac12\left(3-\frac{[\langle q^4\rangle]}{[\langle q^2\rangle]^2}\right), \] which is \(0\) for a Gaussian \(P(q)\) (because \(\langle q^4\rangle=3\langle q^2\rangle^2\)) and \(1\) for two delta peaks at \(\pm q_0\). Near a continuous transition, finite-size scaling says that \(g\) depends on \(T\) and \(N\) only through one combination. For the SK model this combination is \((T-T_c)N^{1/3}\) (mean-field exponents with the upper critical dimension \(d=6\) and \(\nu=1/2\), so \(N^{1/(\nu d)}=N^{1/3}\)) [Billoire 2007]. Consequently \(g(T_c,N)\) does not depend on \(N\): the curves for different \(N\) cross at \(T_c\).

We compute both quantities from the per-sample moments \(\langle q^2\rangle\), \(\langle q^4\rangle\), and estimate errors with a bootstrap over the disorder samples (200 resamplings of the \(D\) samples with replacement).

def overlap_moments(d):
    """Per-sample thermal moments <q^2>, <q^4>: arrays (D, K)."""
    q = d["q_int"].astype(np.float64) / int(d["N"])
    return (q**2).mean(-1), (q**4).mean(-1)

def binder(q2, q4):
    return 0.5 * (3 - q4.mean(0) / q2.mean(0) ** 2)

def crossing(T, g1, g2, window=(0.6, 1.4)):
    """Temperature in the window where g1 - g2 changes sign (linear interpolation); NaN if it does not."""
    sel = (T >= window[0]) & (T <= window[1])
    T, dlt = T[sel], (g1 - g2)[sel]
    i = np.where(np.sign(dlt[:-1]) != np.sign(dlt[1:]))[0]
    if len(i) == 0:
        return np.nan
    i = i[0]
    return T[i] - dlt[i] * (T[i + 1] - T[i]) / (dlt[i + 1] - dlt[i])

6. The energy landscape

6.1 Ground-state energy

Finding the ground state of one SK sample is a hard optimisation problem (Section 1.2). For \(N\le24\) exact enumeration gives it (Section 2.4). For \(N=32,64,128\) we use the parallel-tempering runs: the lowest energy visited by each replica during the run (E_min), improved by a zero-temperature descent, which flips, one at a time, every spin anti-aligned with its local field (\(s_ih_i<0\), so \(\Delta E_i<0\)) until no such spin is left. The descent is also applied to all snapshots stored at the lowest temperature. The result is an upper bound on the true ground-state energy of each sample. A useful indicator of its quality is that the two replicas search independently, so if both find the same lowest energy, it is very likely the ground state.

For the extrapolation to \(N\to\infty\) we use the form \([E_0]/N=e_\infty+a\,N^{-2/3}\); the exponent \(2/3\) of the finite-size correction is supported by extensive ground-state computations for the SK model [Boettcher 2005].

def descent_columns(s, J, block):
    """Zero-temperature descent of C chains of one sample, s: (N, C), J: (N, N). The sweep of Section 5.2 at beta -> infinity
    (beta = infinity, u = 1/2, so the threshold is 0): a spin flips if and only if s_i h_i < 0. Sweeps are repeated until no spin flips."""
    big = jnp.full(s.shape[1], jnp.inf)
    half = jnp.full(s.shape, 0.5)
    def body(c):
        s, h, _ = c
        s2, h2 = sweep_sample(s, h, J, big, half, block)
        return s2, h2, jnp.any(s2 != s)
    s, _, _ = lax.while_loop(lambda c: c[2], body, (s, J @ s, True))
    return s

    """Weighted least squares of e(N) = e_inf + a N^(-2/3) over the sizes selected by mask: (e_inf, a), error of e_inf, chi^2."""
    A = np.stack([np.ones(mask.sum()), x_fit[mask]], 1) / e_err[mask, None]

7. The Mattis model

7.1 Mapping to the Curie–Weiss model

Section 3.3 showed that random signs alone do not produce frustration. Mattis proposed couplings that look as random as the SK couplings but are not frustrated [Mattis 1976]: \[ J_{ij}=\frac{J}{N}\,\xi_i\xi_j,\qquad \xi_i=\pm1\ \text{random and fixed}. \] Half of the couplings are negative. But the gauge transformation of Section 2.3 with \(\tau_i=\xi_i\), i.e. \(\sigma_i=\xi_is_i\), gives \[ \begin{aligned} H&=-\frac JN\sum_{i<j}\xi_i\xi_js_is_j=-\frac JN\sum_{i<j}\sigma_i\sigma_j\\ &=-\frac{J}{2N}\Big[\Big(\sum_i\sigma_i\Big)^2-N\Big], \end{aligned} \] the Curie–Weiss ferromagnet in the variables \(\sigma_i\). All of its thermodynamics follows: a transition at \(T_c=J\), below which the Mattis magnetisation \(m_\xi=\frac1N\sum_i\xi_is_i\) solves \(m_\xi=\tanh(\beta Jm_\xi)\). The ordinary magnetisation \(m=\frac1N\sum_is_i\) stays of order \(1/\sqrt N\): a snapshot looks just as random as an SK snapshot. The order is in the hidden pattern \(\boldsymbol\xi\). (This is the Hopfield network with one stored pattern.)

We check four consequences: no frustrated loops (Section 3.3), only two metastable states, a \(P(q)\) with two sharp peaks at \(\pm m_\xi^2\) (two replicas both aligned with \(\pm\boldsymbol\xi\)) and no weight in between, and a Binder cumulant that goes to \(1\) below \(T_c\).

The Mattis model shows how a ferromagnetic bias competes with frustration. The classical phase diagram of Sherrington and Kirkpatrick [1975] and Kirkpatrick and Sherrington [1978] makes this competition quantitative: the couplings are drawn with a non-zero mean, \(J_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(J_0/N,\,J^2/N)\), and the phases are mapped in the plane of the mean coupling \(J_0/J\) and the temperature \(T/J\), drawn below. The model studied in this notebook is the vertical axis \(J_0=0\).

At high temperature the system is a paramagnet, with \(m=0\) and \(q_{\rm EA}=0\). Lowering the temperature, the paramagnet becomes unstable in one of two ways. For \(J_0<J\) the spin-glass susceptibility \(\chi_{\rm SG}=1/(1-\beta^2J^2)\) of Section 4.4 diverges first, at \(T=J\), and below this line the spins freeze with \(m=0\) and \(q_{\rm EA}>0\): the spin glass. For \(J_0>J\) the ferromagnetic response diverges first, at \(T=J_0\), the Curie–Weiss transition of Section 7.1 with \(J\) replaced by \(J_0\), and below it \(m>0\): the ferromagnet. Both lines follow from the stability of the paramagnet, which the replica-symmetric solution describes exactly, and they are drawn exactly. The boundary between the spin glass and the ferromagnet below \(T=J\) requires the replica-symmetry-breaking solution of Parisi and is beyond this course; it is drawn only as a schematic dotted line.

Both transitions out of the paramagnet are continuous. At a first-order transition a first derivative of the free energy would jump; here the order parameters grow continuously from zero, \(q_{\rm EA}\approx1-T/J\) just below \(T_c=J\) (Section 4.6) and \(m\propto(1-T/J_0)^{1/2}\) below \(T=J_0\) (the same mean-field self-consistency equation as in Lab 012, Section 9), while the susceptibilities diverge as \(1/(T-T_c)\). These are mean-field exponents, exact for a model in which every spin interacts with all others.

8. Replica pairs

8.1 Pooled single snapshots

The gauge symmetry makes a sharp statement about what a single snapshot can reveal. Let \(p_J(\boldsymbol s)\) be the Boltzmann distribution of sample \(J\), and pool snapshots from many samples, as a data set of the kind produced in Section 5 does. The pooled distribution is \([p_J(\boldsymbol s)]\). For any signs \(\tau\), \[ \big[p_J(\tau\boldsymbol s)\big]=\big[p_{\tau J\tau}(\boldsymbol s)\big]=\big[p_J(\boldsymbol s)\big], \] because \(p_J(\tau\boldsymbol s)=p_{\tau J\tau}(\boldsymbol s)\) (Section 2.3) and \(\tau J\tau\) has the same distribution as \(J\). Every configuration can be reached from any other by some \(\tau\), so \[ \big[p_J(\boldsymbol s)\big]=2^{-N}\qquad\text{at every temperature.} \] Pooled over disorder, a single snapshot is a uniformly random string of signs, in the paramagnet and in the spin glass alike. No statistic of single snapshots (magnetisation, correlations between spins, anything) can tell the two phases apart without knowing \(J\).

For pairs of replicas the same argument with \(\tau=\boldsymbol s^a\) maps \((\boldsymbol s^a,\boldsymbol s^b)\to(\boldsymbol 1,\boldsymbol q)\), where \(\boldsymbol q=(s_1^as_1^b,\dots,s_N^as_N^b)\) is the overlap field. The pooled pair distribution depends only on \(\boldsymbol q\), and because the SK ensemble is also symmetric under permutations of the spin labels, only on the number of \(+1\)’s in \(\boldsymbol q\), i.e. on the overlap \(q\). The overlap field \(q_i=s_i^as_i^b\) is gauge invariant, and it is where the information about the phase lives. We check both statements by exact enumeration.

Code 
@jax.jit
def boltzmann_all(J, beta, S):
    """Boltzmann probabilities of all configurations S (2^N, N) for one sample."""
    E = -0.5 * jnp.sum(S * (S @ J), axis=1)
    return jax.nn.softmax(-beta * E)

9. Summary

The SK model, \(H=-\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}s_is_j\) with Gaussian couplings of variance \(J^2/N\), is the simplest spin glass; the factor \(1/N\) keeps the local field of order one (Section 2.2). Its physics involves two averages, thermal averages at fixed couplings followed by the average of \(\ln Z\) over the quenched disorder. The annealed free energy \(\ln2+\beta^2J^2/4\) is exact above \(T=J\) and fails below, where it predicts a negative entropy, as exact enumeration confirms (Section 2.4).

What makes the model glassy is frustration: a loop with a negative product of couplings cannot satisfy all its bonds, half of the SK triangles are frustrated, and frustration is gauge invariant (Section 3). The resulting order has no pattern. The magnetisation vanishes, and the order parameter is the overlap of two replicas with the same couplings, whose distribution \(P(q)\) is a narrow peak at \(0\) above \(T_c\) and broad below, with sample-to-sample fluctuations (Sections 4 and 5).

The transition lies at \(T_c=J\): the spin-glass susceptibility \(1/(1-\beta^2J^2)\) diverges there (Section 4.4), and the Binder cumulants cross at \(0.87\)–\(0.92\), below \(T_c\) at \(N\le128\) but moving towards it with \(N\), while the finite-size-scaling collapse with \(T_c=J\) holds within the error bars (Section 5.7). Parallel tempering, implemented in JAX with vmap over temperatures, replicas and samples, passes the exact energy–overlap identity of Section 5.4, a necessary condition for equilibrium, for \(N\le128\) down to \(T=0.4J\). Below \(T_c\) the energy landscape has exponentially many metastable states, relaxation without exchange moves is slow, and the ground-state energy extrapolates towards Parisi’s \(e_0=-0.7632\), well above the replica-symmetric \(-0.798\) (Section 6).

The Mattis model shows that random signs without frustration give only a hidden ferromagnet, with a sharp \(P(q)\), a Binder cumulant approaching \(1\) and two metastable states (Section 7). Finally, pooled over samples, single snapshots are uniformly random at every temperature, while pairs of replicas depend only on their overlap: the information about the phase is in pairs of replicas (Section 8).
