Lab 032 — Physics of the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick spin glass

A ferromagnet below its critical temperature is easy to recognise: one look at a snapshot shows large domains of aligned spins, and the magnetisation \(m=\frac1N\sum_i s_i\) is non-zero. A spin glass also freezes at low temperature, every spin picks a preferred direction and keeps it for a very long time, but the frozen directions look random. A snapshot of a frozen spin glass is indistinguishable by eye from a snapshot of a hot, disordered paramagnet, and the magnetisation is zero in both. The order is real, but it is hidden.

This notebook is about the physics of the simplest model with this property, the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) model [Sherrington & Kirkpatrick 1975]: \(N\) Ising spins, every pair coupled by a random coupling of random sign. No deep learning is used here. The notebook is the physics foundation for the anomaly-detection study of spin-glass data later in Lecture 5, and it produces the Monte Carlo data set used there.

Plan. 1. Motivation: disorder and frustration in magnetic alloys, hard optimisation problems, and energy landscapes in machine learning. 2. The model: the Hamiltonian, the \(1/N\) scaling of the couplings, the gauge symmetry, and the two kinds of averages (thermal and over the disorder), with the annealed free energy computed exactly and compared with exact enumeration. 3. Frustration: a triangle with a negative product of couplings cannot satisfy all its bonds; in the SK model half of all triangles are frustrated. 4. Order without a pattern: the Edwards–Anderson order parameter, the overlap between two replicas, its distribution \(P(q)\), the high-temperature spin-glass susceptibility, and the main results of the replica theory (stated with their meaning and checked numerically, not derived). 5. The transition at \(T_c=J\): parallel-tempering Monte Carlo in JAX for \(N=32,64,128\), an exact equilibration test, \(P(q)\), and the Binder cumulant. 6. The energy landscape: ground-state energies from exact enumeration and from Monte Carlo against Parisi’s value, the number of metastable states, and slow dynamics. 7. The Mattis model: random signs without frustration, a ferromagnet in disguise, no glass. 8. The information is in pairs of replicas: a single snapshot carries no visible order; the overlap field \(q_i=s_i^as_i^b\) carries it. This is the bridge to the machine-learning notebook. 9. Summary, exercises with solutions, references.

Conventions. Boltzmann’s constant \(k_B=1\); \(\beta=1/T\). The coupling scale \(J\) is kept in the formulas and set to \(J=1\) in the code, so temperatures and energies are in units of \(J\). Square brackets \([\,\cdot\,]\) denote the average over the random couplings, angular brackets \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) the thermal average at fixed couplings.

1. Disorder and frustration: from magnetic alloys to hard optimisation problems

1.1 Glassy magnets

The name spin glass was coined for metallic alloys of a non-magnetic metal with a few percent of randomly substituted magnetic atoms, such as iron in gold [Cannella & Mydosh 1972] or manganese in copper. Experiments found a low-temperature phase in which the magnetic moments freeze in non-periodic directions, with a very slow, strongly history-dependent response to external perturbations [Binder & Young 1986; Billoire 2007].

The moments interact through the conduction electrons. A standard model of this interaction is the RKKY (Ruderman–Kittel–Kasuya–Yosida) coupling \[ J(r)\propto\frac{\cos(2k_F r)}{r^3}, \] with \(k_F\) the Fermi wave number. Its sign oscillates with the distance \(r\). Combined with random positions of the magnetic atoms, any such coupling produces couplings of random sign: some pairs prefer to be parallel (ferromagnetic, \(J>0\)), others antiparallel (antiferromagnetic, \(J<0\)). The positions are fixed when the alloy is made, so the couplings are random but frozen, or quenched. We check the random signs with a few lines of code: \(n\) moments are placed uniformly at random in a cube, and we histogram the factor that carries the sign, \(\cos(2k_Fr_{ij})\), for the strongest couplings (pairs at distances \(1<r_{ij}<3\) in units of the lattice constant; \(k_F=3\), any value of order a few gives the same picture).

def rkky(r, kF=3.0):

    return np.cos(2 * kF * r) / r**3

Both signs occur in comparable numbers. The SK model keeps exactly this ingredient, random signs, and drops the geometry: every spin is coupled to every other spin, with a Gaussian random coupling.

1.2 Hard optimisation problems

Finding the lowest-energy configuration of \(E(\boldsymbol s)=-\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}s_is_j\) for given couplings is a combinatorial optimisation problem over \(2^N\) configurations. With the couplings chosen appropriately, many classic hard problems of computer science (graph partitioning, maximum cut, satisfiability, all of Karp’s 21 NP-complete problems, where NP stands for nondeterministic polynomial time) can be written in exactly this form [Lucas 2014]; the computational complexity of finding spin-glass ground states was analysed early by Barahona [Barahona 1982]. Conversely, spin-glass physics produced general-purpose algorithms: simulated annealing [Kirkpatrick, Gelatt & Vecchi 1983] was proposed by one of the two authors of the SK model, and the parallel-tempering method used in Section 5 was designed for spin glasses [Hukushima & Nemoto 1996].

1.3 Energy landscapes in machine learning

A network of binary units with symmetric weights \(W_{ij}\) and energy \(E(\boldsymbol s)=-\frac12\sum_{ij}W_{ij}s_is_j\) is the Hopfield network [Hopfield 1982]: the same Hamiltonian, with couplings constructed from data instead of drawn at random. The loss function of a neural network is likewise a function on a very high-dimensional space with many local minima. The SK model is the solvable prototype of such a rugged landscape: exponentially many minima, barriers between them, and slow dynamics. The vocabulary developed for it (metastable states, overlaps between minima, replicas) is used throughout the theory of learning [Mézard & Montanari 2009; Charbonneau et al. 2023].

What to expect. In this notebook the spins have no positions at all: the SK model has no lattice, so there is no spatial pattern to look at. We will see that the low-temperature phase is nevertheless sharply defined, and that the right tool to see it is a comparison of two independent copies of the system with the same couplings.

2. The Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model

2.1 The Hamiltonian

\(N\) Ising spins \(s_i=\pm1\), \(i=1,\dots,N\), and every pair \((i,j)\) is coupled: \[ H(\boldsymbol s) = -\sum_{i<j} J_{ij}\, s_i s_j = -\frac12\,\boldsymbol s^{T} J\, \boldsymbol s , \qquad J_{ij}=J_{ji}\sim\mathcal N\!\left(0,\frac{J^2}{N}\right)\ \text{independent for } i<j,\qquad J_{ii}=0 . \] \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) is the Gaussian distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). The mean is zero: ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic couplings are equally likely. A fixed matrix \(J\) is called a disorder realisation or sample.

Two quantities are used throughout: * the local field on spin \(i\), \(h_i=\sum_{j\neq i}J_{ij}s_j\), so that \(H=-\frac12\sum_i s_ih_i\); * the energy change of one flip \(s_i\to-s_i\): only the \(N-1\) terms containing \(s_i\) change sign, so \(\Delta E_i = 2s_ih_i\). A configuration is stable against every single flip if and only if \(s_ih_i>0\) for all \(i\): every spin is aligned with its local field.

2.2 The \(1/N\) scaling of the couplings

The variance is chosen as \(J^2/N\), not \(J^2\), for the following reason. Take any fixed configuration \(\boldsymbol s\). Then \(J_{ij}s_j\) has the same Gaussian distribution as \(J_{ij}\) (the distribution is symmetric under \(J\to-J\)), so the local field is a sum of \(N-1\) independent Gaussians: \[ \operatorname{Var}(h_i) = (N-1)\,\sigma^2,\qquad \sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}(J_{ij}). \] * With \(\sigma^2=J^2/N\): \(\operatorname{Var}(h_i)=J^2(N-1)/N\to J^2\). The field on a spin stays of order one as \(N\) grows, so temperatures of order \(J\) compete with it, and the transition temperature has a finite limit. * With \(\sigma^2=J^2\) (no scaling): the field grows like \(\sqrt N\,J\), and so would every characteristic temperature; there would be no thermodynamic limit. * A ferromagnet with all-to-all couplings needs \(J_{ij}=J_0/N\) (the Curie–Weiss model), because there the \(N\) terms add coherently: \(h_i=J_0m\). Random signs add incoherently, like a random walk, so the coupling is scaled by \(1/\sqrt N\) in magnitude, i.e. \(1/N\) in variance.

The energy of a configuration is \(H=-\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}s_is_j\), a sum of \(N(N-1)/2\) independent terms. For a random configuration it has mean \(0\) and variance \(\frac{N(N-1)}2\,\sigma^2 = \frac{(N-1)J^2}{2}\): a typical configuration has energy of order \(\sqrt N\). The low-energy configurations, which dominate at low temperature, have energies of order \(-N\) (Section 6); the energy is extensive.

We check these statements symbolically with SymPy (the Gaussian moments by direct integration, then the sums by independence) and then numerically.

Code 
def sk_couplings(key, n_dis, N, J=1.0):
    """n_dis independent SK coupling matrices, shape (n_dis, N, N): symmetric, zero diagonal, J_ij ~ N(0, J^2/N)."""
    A = jax.random.normal(key, (n_dis, N, N)) * (J / jnp.sqrt(N))
    upper = jnp.triu(A, 1)                                   # keep i < j only
    return upper + jnp.swapaxes(upper, 1, 2)                 # J_ji = J_ij, J_ii = 0

2.3 Symmetries: the global flip and the gauge transformation

  • Global flip. \(H(-\boldsymbol s)=H(\boldsymbol s)\) for every sample. Hence, at any finite \(N\), \(\langle s_i\rangle=0\) exactly.
  • Gauge transformation. Choose any signs \(\tau_i=\pm1\) and transform \[ s_i\to\tau_is_i,\qquad J_{ij}\to\tau_i\tau_jJ_{ij}. \] Each term \(J_{ij}s_is_j\to\tau_i^2\tau_j^2J_{ij}s_is_j=J_{ij}s_is_j\) is unchanged, so the energy of every configuration is unchanged. Because the Gaussian distribution of \(J_{ij}\) is symmetric, the transformed couplings \(\tau_i\tau_jJ_{ij}\) have the same distribution as the original ones.

The gauge symmetry has a strong consequence. Every quantity averaged over the disorder must be invariant under the transformation. The magnetisation of a sample, \(\frac1N\sum_i\langle s_i\rangle\), is not invariant (\(s_i\to\tau_is_i\) flips arbitrary spins), so it carries no information about the ordering after averaging over samples. The invariant combinations are products in which every spin index appears twice, such as \(\langle s_is_j\rangle^2\) or, for two copies \(a,b\) of the system, \(s_i^as_i^b\). These are the building blocks of the spin-glass order parameter (Section 4).

2.4 Quenched disorder: two different averages

A spin glass has two sources of randomness, on two very different time scales.

  • Thermal noise. At fixed couplings \(J\) the spins fluctuate. Thermal averages are Boltzmann averages at fixed \(J\): \[ \langle A\rangle_J=\frac{1}{Z_J}\sum_{\boldsymbol s}A(\boldsymbol s)\,e^{-\beta H_J(\boldsymbol s)},\qquad Z_J=\sum_{\boldsymbol s}e^{-\beta H_J(\boldsymbol s)} . \]
  • Disorder. The couplings are random but frozen (quenched): in the alloy the atoms do not move on the time scale of the spin dynamics. The average over realisations, \([X]=\int\!\prod_{i<j}dJ_{ij}\,P(J_{ij})\;X(J)\), is taken after the thermal average, sample by sample.

The physically relevant free energy is therefore the quenched average \[ f=-\frac{T}{N}\,[\ln Z_J], \] not the annealed one, \(f_{\rm ann}=-\frac{T}{N}\ln[Z_J]\), which would describe couplings that fluctuate and equilibrate together with the spins. The reason the quenched average is the meaningful one is self-averaging: as \(N\to\infty\), the free energy per spin of a single large sample, \(-\frac{T}{N}\ln Z_J\), converges to its disorder average. One large sample is representative. \(Z_J\) itself is not self-averaging: it is exponential in \(N\) and its average is dominated by rare samples.

The logarithm is concave, so Jensen’s inequality gives a strict ordering: \([\ln Z_J]\le\ln[Z_J]\), i.e. \(f\ge f_{\rm ann}\).

The annealed free energy, exactly. The average of \(Z\) is easy, because the couplings are independent and appear in the exponent: \[ [Z_J] = \sum_{\boldsymbol s}\prod_{i<j}\big[e^{\beta J_{ij}s_is_j}\big],\qquad \big[e^{\beta J_{ij}s_is_j}\big]=e^{\beta^2\sigma^2/2}\quad(\text{Gaussian integral; independent of } s_is_j=\pm1). \] Every configuration contributes the same factor, so \([Z_J]=2^N\exp\!\big(\frac{N(N-1)}2\frac{\beta^2J^2}{2N}\big)\) and, for \(N\to\infty\), \[ -\beta f_{\rm ann}=\ln2+\frac{\beta^2J^2}{4},\qquad u_{\rm ann}=-\frac{\beta J^2}2,\qquad s_{\rm ann}=\ln2-\frac{\beta^2J^2}{4}. \] The annealed entropy becomes negative below \(T^*=J/(2\sqrt{\ln2})\). The entropy of a system with a finite number of discrete states is \(-\sum p\ln p\ge0\), so the annealed result must fail at low temperature. Aizenman, Lebowitz and Ruelle proved that for \(T>J\) the quenched free energy equals the annealed one in the limit \(N\to\infty\) [Aizenman, Lebowitz & Ruelle 1987]: the high-temperature phase is simple, and something happens at \(T=J\). We derive the annealed results with SymPy and then compare them with exact enumeration of small systems.

def half_configs(N):
    """All 2^(N-1) configurations with s_N = +1, shape (2^(N-1), N), float32."""
    k = jnp.arange(2 ** (N - 1), dtype=jnp.int32)
    bits = (k[:, None] >> jnp.arange(N - 1)) & 1
    return jnp.concatenate([2.0 * bits - 1.0, jnp.ones((k.size, 1))], axis=1)

@jax.jit
def enumerate_sample(J, betas, betas_chi):
    """Exact thermodynamics of one sample J (N, N).
    Returns ln Z (n_T,), U (n_T,), E0, number of single-flip-stable configurations, chi_SG (n_chi,)."""
    N = J.shape[0]
    S = half_configs(N)                                        # (M, N)
    Hf = S @ J                                                 # (M, N) local fields
    E = -0.5 * jnp.sum(S * Hf, axis=1)                         # (M,) energies
    n_stable = 2 * jnp.sum(jnp.all(S * Hf > 0, axis=1))        # x2 for the mirror images
    logw = -betas[:, None] * E[None, :]                        # (n_T, M)
    lse = jax.scipy.special.logsumexp(logw, axis=1)
    p = jnp.exp(logw - lse[:, None])                           # Boltzmann probabilities on the half space
    logZ = jnp.log(2.0) + lse
    U = p @ E
    # spin-glass susceptibility chi_SG = (1/N) sum_ij <s_i s_j>^2 at the temperatures betas_chi
    pc = jnp.exp(-betas_chi[:, None] * E[None, :] - jax.scipy.special.logsumexp(-betas_chi[:, None] * E[None, :], axis=1)[:, None])
    C = jnp.einsum("bm,mi,mj->bij", pc, S, S)                  # (n_chi, N, N) correlation matrices
    chi = jnp.sum(C**2, axis=(1, 2)) / N
    return logZ, U, jnp.min(E), n_stable, chi

3.2 The criterion: the sign of the product of couplings

Multiply the three bond terms around the triangle: \[ (J_{12}s_1s_2)(J_{23}s_2s_3)(J_{31}s_3s_1)=J_{12}J_{23}J_{31}\,s_1^2s_2^2s_3^2=J_{12}J_{23}J_{31}. \] The product of the bond terms does not depend on the spins. A satisfied bond has a positive term, so if all three bonds were satisfied the product would be positive. Hence: if \(J_{12}J_{23}J_{31}<0\), at least one bond is broken in every configuration (in fact an odd number). The argument works for any closed loop of bonds, since every spin on a loop appears in exactly two bond terms. The product around a loop is also gauge invariant: \(\tau_i\tau_jJ_{ij}\) multiplies the product by \(\prod\tau^2=1\). Frustration is a property of the couplings that no relabelling of spins can remove; the signs of individual couplings are not.

For couplings of arbitrary magnitudes \(a,b,c>0\) and signs with a negative product, the ground-state energy is \(-(a+b+c)+2\min(a,b,c)\): the weakest bond is the one that breaks. SymPy confirms both statements.

3.3 Half of all triangles of the SK model are frustrated

In the SK model every triple \((i,j,k)\) forms a triangle. The product of three independent couplings with symmetric distributions is negative with probability \(1/2\) (an odd number of negative factors), so half of the \(\binom N3\) triangles are frustrated. For a large \(N\) we count them without a triple loop. Let \(\sigma_{ij}=\operatorname{sign}J_{ij}\) (with \(\sigma_{ii}=0\)). Then \[ \operatorname{Tr}\sigma^3=\sum_{i,j,k}\sigma_{ij}\sigma_{jk}\sigma_{ki} \] runs over ordered triples of distinct indices (the zero diagonal removes the others), and each triangle appears \(3!=6\) times. Hence \[ n_{\rm unfrustrated}-n_{\rm frustrated}=\tfrac16\operatorname{Tr}\sigma^3,\qquad n_{\rm unfrustrated}+n_{\rm frustrated}=\binom N3 . \] We compare three coupling matrices: the SK couplings, the ferromagnet \(J_{ij}=J/N\), and the Mattis couplings \(J_{ij}=J\xi_i\xi_j/N\) with random \(\xi_i=\pm1\) (Section 7), which have random signs too.

Code 
def frustrated_fraction(J):
    """Fraction of frustrated triangles from Tr(sign(J)^3), J of shape (N, N) with zero diagonal."""
    N = J.shape[0]
    sg = np.sign(np.asarray(J, dtype=np.float64))
    diff = np.trace(sg @ sg @ sg) / 6                         # n_unfrustrated - n_frustrated
    total = N * (N - 1) * (N - 2) / 6
    return (total - diff) / 2 / total

def frustrated_fraction_loop(J):
    """The same by an explicit loop over all triples (for checking)."""
    J = np.asarray(J); N = J.shape[0]
    f = [J[i, j] * J[j, k] * J[k, i] < 0 for i, j, k in itertools.combinations(range(N), 3)]
    return np.mean(f)

4. Order without a pattern: the Edwards–Anderson order parameter and the replica overlap

4.1 Frozen spins with zero magnetisation

In a ferromagnet below \(T_c\) the thermal averages \(\langle s_i\rangle\) are all equal, and the magnetisation \(m=\frac1N\sum_i\langle s_i\rangle\) measures the order. In a spin glass below its freezing temperature each spin also has a non-zero average \(\langle s_i\rangle\) inside the valley the system is trapped in, but the signs of the \(\langle s_i\rangle\) follow the random couplings, not a common direction. Their sum is a sum of \(N\) terms of random sign, so \(m\) is of order \(1/\sqrt N\) and vanishes for large \(N\). (After averaging over the disorder, \([\langle s_i\rangle]=0\) exactly by the gauge symmetry of Section 2.3.) The SK model has no lattice either, so a snapshot cannot show domains or stripes: the labels \(i\) carry no geometry, and any permutation of the labels (with the couplings permuted accordingly) describes the same system. A snapshot of the frozen phase looks like noise.

4.2 The Edwards–Anderson order parameter

Edwards and Anderson proposed to measure the freezing itself, regardless of the directions [Edwards & Anderson 1975]: \[ q_{\rm EA}=\frac1N\sum_i\langle s_i\rangle^2 . \] It is zero in the paramagnet (\(\langle s_i\rangle=0\)) and positive when the spins are frozen; it is invariant under the gauge transformation. There is a practical difficulty. In a finite system \(\langle s_i\rangle=0\) exactly (global flip symmetry), and \(\langle s_i\rangle\) restricted to “the valley we are in” is not something a simulation or an experiment can define directly.

4.3 Two replicas and the overlap

The solution is to simulate two independent copies \(a\) and \(b\) of the system, called replicas, with the same couplings \(J\) and independent thermal noise. Their joint distribution is the product \(p_J(\boldsymbol s^a)\,p_J(\boldsymbol s^b)\). The overlap \[ q_{ab}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^Ns_i^as_i^b\ \in[-1,1] \] is \(+1\) for identical configurations, \(-1\) for mirror images, and close to \(0\) for unrelated ones; the fraction of spins on which the two replicas differ is \((1-q_{ab})/2\). Because the replicas are independent at fixed \(J\), \[ \langle q_{ab}^2\rangle=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i,j}\langle s_i^as_j^a\rangle\langle s_i^bs_j^b\rangle=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i,j}\langle s_is_j\rangle^2 . \] The global flip makes \(\langle q_{ab}\rangle=0\) at finite \(N\), but \(\langle q_{ab}^2\rangle\) is not affected: it measures the frozen correlations. If the system sits in one of two mirror valleys with averages \(\pm\langle s_i\rangle\), then \(q_{ab}=\pm q_{\rm EA}\) and \(\langle q^2_{ab}\rangle=q_{\rm EA}^2\).

The full information is in the overlap distribution of a sample and its disorder average, \[ P_J(q)=\big\langle\delta(q-q_{ab})\big\rangle_J,\qquad P(q)=\big[P_J(q)\big]. \] * Paramagnet: \(q_{ab}\) is a sum of \(N\) weakly correlated terms \(\pm1/N\), so \(P(q)\) is a single Gaussian peak at \(q=0\) of width of order \(1/\sqrt N\). * Spin glass: \(P(q)\) has weight at \(q\neq0\) that does not shrink as \(N\) grows (Sections 4.5 and 5).

The spin-glass susceptibility collects the frozen correlations, \[ \chi_{\rm SG}=\frac1N\sum_{i,j}\big[\langle s_is_j\rangle^2\big]=N\,\big[\langle q_{ab}^2\rangle\big], \] and in the paramagnet it is the width of \(P(q)\): \([\langle q^2\rangle]=\chi_{\rm SG}/N\).

4.4 The spin-glass susceptibility diverges at \(T=J\)

At high temperature \(\chi_{\rm SG}\) can be computed by an expansion in powers of \(\beta\). Write each Boltzmann factor as \(e^{\beta J_{ij}s_is_j}=\cosh(\beta J_{ij})\,(1+t_{ij}s_is_j)\) with \(t_{ij}=\tanh(\beta J_{ij})\approx\beta J_{ij}\) (small, of order \(1/\sqrt N\)). Expanding the product, \(\langle s_is_j\rangle\) is a sum over paths of bonds from \(i\) to \(j\), each path contributing the product of its \(t\)’s. Now square and average over the disorder: 1. Cross terms between two different paths contain some coupling an odd number of times, and \([J_{kl}]=0\) removes them. Only the squares of single paths survive. 2. A path of length \(\ell\) contributes \(\prod[t^2]\approx(\beta^2J^2/N)^\ell\). 3. The number of paths of length \(\ell\) from \(i\) to \(j\) through distinct intermediate sites is \((N-2)(N-3)\cdots(N-\ell)\approx N^{\ell-1}\), and summing over the end point \(j\) gives another factor \(N\).

Therefore, to leading order in \(1/N\), \[ \chi_{\rm SG}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}N^{\ell}\Big(\frac{\beta^2J^2}{N}\Big)^{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty(\beta J)^{2\ell}=\frac{1}{1-\beta^2J^2}\qquad(T>J). \] The series diverges at \(\beta J=1\): the paramagnet becomes unstable at \(T_c=J\). This is the value of the SK critical temperature (the replica solution below uses the same convention, \(T_c=J\), as e.g. [Crisanti & Rizzo 2002]). SymPy checks the two ingredients, the path count and the geometric sum; then we compare with exact enumeration.

4.6 The replica-symmetric solution of Sherrington and Kirkpatrick

Sherrington and Kirkpatrick computed \([\ln Z]\) with the replica trick [Sherrington & Kirkpatrick 1975]. The identity \(\ln Z=\lim_{n\to0}(Z^n-1)/n\) replaces the hard average of a logarithm by averages of \(Z^n\), the partition function of \(n\) replicas with the same couplings, which are Gaussian integrals as in Section 2.4. Averaging couples the replicas through the overlaps \(q_{ab}\). The simplest assumption, replica symmetry (RS), is that all overlaps are equal, \(q_{ab}=q\). We do not repeat the calculation; its result has a transparent meaning.

The self-consistency equation. In a frozen state, spin \(i\) feels the field of the other spins with \(i\) itself removed (the cavity field), \(h_i=\sum_jJ_{ij}\langle s_j\rangle\). It is a sum of \(N\) terms with random signs, hence Gaussian, with variance \(\sum_jJ_{ij}^2\langle s_j\rangle^2\approx J^2q\). The spin then has \(\langle s_i\rangle=\tanh(\beta h_i)\), and averaging \(\langle s_i\rangle^2\) over the Gaussian field closes the loop: \[ q=\int\!Dz\;\tanh^2\!\big(\beta J\sqrt q\,z\big),\qquad Dz=\frac{dz}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}, \] \[ -\beta f_{\rm RS}=\frac{\beta^2J^2}{4}(1-q)^2+\int\!Dz\,\ln\!\big(2\cosh\beta J\sqrt q\,z\big),\qquad u_{\rm RS}=-\frac{\beta J^2}{2}\big(1-q^2\big). \] At \(q=0\) this is exactly the annealed result of Section 2.4. (The same expression is the constant-\(q\) case of Parisi’s free energy written in [Crisanti & Rizzo 2002].)

\(T_c=J\) from the linearised equation. For small \(q\), \(\tanh^2x\approx x^2\) and \(\int Dz\,z^2=1\), so \(q\approx\beta^2J^2q\): a non-zero solution appears exactly when \(\beta J>1\). Expanding one order further gives \(q\approx\tau\) with \(\tau=1-T/J\): the Edwards–Anderson order parameter grows linearly below \(T_c\). SymPy does the expansion.

Now the full RS solution, numerically. The Gaussian integrals are one-dimensional; we evaluate them with scipy.integrate.quad on \(z>0\) (the integrands are even), and find \(q(T)\) as the root of \(q-\int Dz\tanh^2(\beta\sqrt q z)\) with brentq. From \(q\) we get \(f_{\rm RS}\), \(u_{\rm RS}\) and the entropy \(s_{\rm RS}=\beta(u_{\rm RS}-f_{\rm RS})\).

Code 
from scipy.integrate import quad
from scipy.optimize import brentq

phi = lambda z: np.exp(-z**2 / 2) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi)
gauss_avg = lambda g: 2 * quad(lambda z: phi(z) * g(z), 0, 12, limit=400)[0]          # int Dz g(z) for an even g

def rs_solution(T):
    """Replica-symmetric q, f, u, s at temperature T (J = 1)."""
    b = 1 / T
    F = lambda q: q - gauss_avg(lambda z: np.tanh(b * np.sqrt(q) * z) ** 2)
    q = brentq(F, 1e-10, 1.0) if T < 1 else 0.0
    log2cosh = lambda x: np.abs(x) + np.log1p(np.exp(-2 * np.abs(x)))               # ln(2 cosh x) without overflow
    mbf = b**2 * (1 - q) ** 2 / 4 + gauss_avg(lambda z: log2cosh(b * np.sqrt(q) * z))
    u = -b * (1 - q**2) / 2
    return q, -T * mbf, u, mbf + b * u

4.7 Parisi’s solution: the results we use

Parisi found the correct solution by breaking the replica symmetry in infinitely many steps [Parisi 1979; Parisi 1980]; his free energy was later proved rigorously to be exact [Talagrand 2006]. We do not derive it. Its main results, and their meaning:

  1. The order parameter is a function. Instead of one number \(q\), the solution is a non-decreasing function \(q(x)\), \(0\le x\le1\). Its inverse defines the overlap distribution, \(P(q)=dx/dq\) [Parisi 1983; Crisanti & Rizzo 2002].
  2. Many states. Below \(T_c\) the Boltzmann distribution of a large sample splits into many “pure states” (valleys) \(\alpha\) with weights \(w_\alpha\). Two replicas fall into states \(\alpha,\beta\) independently, and \(P_J(q)=\sum_{\alpha\beta}w_\alpha w_\beta\,\delta(q-q_{\alpha\beta})\). Pairs in the same state give the self-overlap \(q_{\rm EA}\); pairs in different states give smaller overlaps. Hence \(P(q)\) has peaks at \(\pm q_{\rm EA}\) and a continuous part in between, not a single pair of delta functions as in a ferromagnet [Mézard, Parisi & Virasoro 1986].
  3. Sample dependence. The weights \(w_\alpha\) differ from sample to sample, so \(P_J(q)\) differs between samples even for large \(N\).
  4. The critical temperature is \(T_c=J\), the same as in the RS calculation and in the high-temperature expansion.
  5. The ground-state energy per spin for \(N\to\infty\) is \(e_0=-0.76321\pm0.00003\) [Crisanti & Rizzo 2002], in agreement with Parisi’s estimate \(-0.7633\pm0.0001\) [Parisi 1980], above the RS value \(-\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx-0.798\).

5. The transition at \(T_c=J\): parallel-tempering Monte Carlo

5.1 Barriers, and the idea of parallel tempering

Below \(T_c\) the free-energy landscape has many valleys separated by barriers that grow with \(N\). A Metropolis chain at a fixed low temperature (single spin flips, accepted with probability \(\min(1,e^{-\beta\Delta E})\)) stays in one valley for a very long time: the samples it produces are correct locally but do not represent the relative weights of the valleys, and the estimate of \(P(q)\), which is all about different valleys, is wrong. Section 6.4 shows this directly.

Parallel tempering (also called replica exchange or exchange Monte Carlo) [Swendsen & Wang 1986; Hukushima & Nemoto 1996; Earl & Deem 2005] runs \(K\) copies of the same sample at temperatures \(T_1<T_2<\dots<T_K\). The joint distribution of the copies is the product of their Boltzmann distributions, \[ \Pi(\boldsymbol s_1,\dots,\boldsymbol s_K)=\prod_{k=1}^K\frac{e^{-\beta_kE(\boldsymbol s_k)}}{Z(\beta_k)} . \] Two moves alternate: 1. Local moves: one Metropolis sweep of every copy at its own temperature. Each copy is updated with a move that preserves its own Boltzmann distribution, so \(\Pi\) is preserved. 2. Exchange moves: for neighbouring temperatures \(k,k+1\), propose to swap the two configurations. Accept with probability \[ \min\Big(1,\ \exp\big[(\beta_k-\beta_{k+1})(E_k-E_{k+1})\big]\Big), \] where \(E_k\) is the energy of the configuration currently at temperature \(T_k\). This is the Metropolis rule for the swap: the ratio \(\Pi(\text{after})/\Pi(\text{before})\) equals the exponential (SymPy below), so detailed balance holds and \(\Pi\) is preserved.

The mechanism. A configuration does a random walk in temperature. At high temperature it crosses barriers easily; when it comes back down it can land in a different valley. The swap is accepted often when the energy histograms of neighbouring temperatures overlap, which sets how dense the temperature grid must be. For the replica overlap we need two independent parallel-tempering simulations of the same sample (replicas \(a\) and \(b\)), each with its own \(K\) copies.

5.2 Implementation in JAX

Shapes. One simulation handles \(D\) disorder samples, \(2\) replicas and \(K\) temperatures at once. For one sample the \(C=2K\) chains (replica \(r\), temperature slot \(k\), column \(c=rK+k\)) are the columns of a matrix, and jax.vmap maps the per-sample code over the \(D\) samples:

array shape meaning
J (D, N, N) couplings of the \(D\) samples
s (D, N, 2, K) spins: sample \(d\), spin \(i\), replica \(r\in\{a,b\}\), temperature slot \(k\) (reshaped to (D, N, C) for the sweep)
h (D, N, 2, K) local fields \(h_i=\sum_jJ_{ij}s_j\) of every chain, kept up to date
beta (K,) inverse temperatures, the same for every sample and replica; beta_c = tile(beta, 2) has shape (C,)
lab (D, 2, K) label of the configuration in each temperature slot (to follow its walk in temperature)

The sweep. All spins of the SK model are coupled to each other, so two spins cannot be flipped simultaneously: the energy change of one flip depends on all other spins. A sweep visits the spins one after another. After a flip of spin \(i\) by \(\Delta s_i=-2s_i\) every local field changes, \(h_j\to h_j+J_{ji}\Delta s_i\), which is \(O(N)\) work per flip and \(O(N^2)\) per sweep. Done literally, spin by spin, this reads and writes the whole field array \(N\) times per sweep. We organise the same sequential sweep in blocks of \(b=16\) spins: 1. inside a block, the spins are visited one by one; each flip updates only the fields of the \(b\) spins of the block (a small (b, C) array, couplings J_bb of shape (b, b)); 2. after the block, the fields of all \(N\) spins are updated at once by one matrix product, \(h\leftarrow h+J_{:,\text{block}}\,\Delta s_{\text{block}}\), shapes (N, b) @ (b, C).

Every spin still sees the exact current field when it is visited, so this is exactly the sequential Metropolis sweep, with the expensive part done as matrix products.

The exchange. For one sample and one replica the \(K\) configurations are permuted: slot \(k\) receives the configuration from slot \(k+1\) and vice versa if the swap is accepted. Even pairs \((0,1),(2,3),\dots\) and odd pairs \((1,2),(3,4),\dots\) alternate between sweeps, so the attempted swaps never overlap.

from math import gcd

def sweep_sample(s, h, J, beta_c, u, block):
    """One sequential Metropolis sweep of all chains of one sample.
    s, h: (N, C) spins and local fields, one chain per column; J: (N, N); beta_c: (C,) inverse temperature of each chain;
    u: (N, C) uniform random numbers. Spins are visited in the order i = 0, ..., N-1, in blocks of `block` spins."""
    N, C = s.shape
    thr = -jnp.log(u) / (2.0 * beta_c)                             # (N, C): flip spin i iff s_i h_i < thr_i, i.e. u < exp(-2 beta s_i h_i)
    def do_block(kb, carry):
        s, h = carry
        i0 = kb * block
        s_old = lax.dynamic_slice_in_dim(s, i0, block, 0)          # (b, C) spins of the block
        hb = lax.dynamic_slice_in_dim(h, i0, block, 0)             # (b, C) their fields at the start of the block
        tb = lax.dynamic_slice_in_dim(thr, i0, block, 0)           # (b, C) acceptance thresholds
        J_cols = lax.dynamic_slice_in_dim(J, i0, block, 1)         # (N, b) couplings of every spin to the block
        J_bb = lax.dynamic_slice_in_dim(J_cols, i0, block, 0)      # (b, b) couplings inside the block
        rows = []
        for j in range(block):                                     # sequential inside the block (unrolled by jit)
            sj = s_old[j]
            accept = sj * hb[j] < tb[j]                            # Metropolis rule, Delta E = 2 s_j h_j
            d = jnp.where(accept, -2.0 * sj, 0.0)                  # (C,) change of s_j, 0 if rejected
            hb = hb + J_bb[:, j:j + 1] * d[None, :]                # fields of the block spins after this flip
            rows.append(sj + d)
        s_new = jnp.stack(rows)                                    # (b, C)
        s = lax.dynamic_update_slice_in_dim(s, s_new, i0, 0)
        h = h + J_cols @ (s_new - s_old)                           # all N fields, one matrix product per block
        return s, h
    return lax.fori_loop(0, N // block, do_block, (s, h))

def swap_moves(s, h, lab, E, beta, u, parity):
    """Exchange moves for all samples and replicas. s, h: (D, N, 2, K); lab, E: (D, 2, K); beta: (K,); u: (D, 2, K-1).
    Pairs (k, k+1) with k % 2 == parity are attempted; returns the permuted arrays and the accepted swaps (D, 2, K-1)."""
    K = beta.shape[0]
    log_ratio = (beta[:-1] - beta[1:]) * (E[..., :-1] - E[..., 1:])            # (D, 2, K-1)
    acc = (jnp.log(u) < log_ratio) & (jnp.arange(K - 1) % 2 == parity)
    pad = jnp.zeros(acc.shape[:-1] + (1,), bool)
    up = jnp.concatenate([acc, pad], -1).astype(jnp.int32)                     # slot k takes from k+1
    down = jnp.concatenate([pad, acc], -1).astype(jnp.int32)                   # slot k+1 takes from k
    perm = jnp.arange(K) + up - down                                           # (D, 2, K)
    take = lambda a: jnp.take_along_axis(a, perm[:, None], axis=3)             # permute the K axis of (D, N, 2, K)
    return take(s), take(h), jnp.take_along_axis(lab, perm, 2), jnp.take_along_axis(E, perm, 2), acc

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("n_equil", "n_save", "n_meas", "meas_every", "use_swaps"))
def parallel_tempering(J, s0, beta, key, n_equil, n_save, n_meas, meas_every, use_swaps=True):
    """Parallel tempering for D samples x 2 replicas x K temperatures; s0: (D, 2, K, N) initial spins.
    Equilibration: n_equil sweeps. Production: n_save blocks of n_meas measurements, one every meas_every sweeps;
    one configuration per slot is stored at the end of every block. Returns a dict of arrays (shapes in the comments)."""
    D, R, K, N = s0.shape
    C = R * K
    sweep_all = jax.vmap(partial(sweep_sample, block=gcd(N, 16)), in_axes=(0, 0, 0, None, 0))   # over the D samples
    beta_c = jnp.tile(beta, R)                                                  # (C,) column c = r K + k
    s_init = jnp.transpose(s0, (0, 3, 1, 2))                                   # (D, N, 2, K)
    h_init = jnp.einsum("dij,djrk->dirk", J, s_init)
    carry0 = dict(s=s_init, h=h_init, lab=jnp.broadcast_to(jnp.arange(K), (D, R, K)),
                  emin=jnp.full((D, R), jnp.inf), smin=s0[:, :, 0], acc=jnp.zeros(K - 1), t=0, key=key)

    def one_sweep(c, _):
        key, k1, k2 = jax.random.split(c["key"], 3)
        s, h = sweep_all(c["s"].reshape(D, N, C), c["h"].reshape(D, N, C), J, beta_c, jax.random.uniform(k1, (D, N, C)))
        s, h = s.reshape(D, N, R, K), h.reshape(D, N, R, K)
        E = -0.5 * jnp.sum(s * h, axis=1)                                      # (D, 2, K) energies
        lab, acc = c["lab"], jnp.zeros((D, R, K - 1), bool)
        if use_swaps:
            s, h, lab, E, acc = swap_moves(s, h, lab, E, beta, jax.random.uniform(k2, (D, R, K - 1)), c["t"] % 2)
        k_low = jnp.argmin(E, -1)                                              # (D, 2) lowest-energy slot
        s_low = jnp.take_along_axis(s, k_low[:, None, :, None], axis=3)[..., 0]   # (D, N, 2)
        better = E.min(-1) < c["emin"]
        q = jnp.mean(s[:, :, 0] * s[:, :, 1], axis=1)                          # (D, K) overlaps of the two replicas
        new = dict(s=s, h=h, lab=lab, emin=jnp.where(better, E.min(-1), c["emin"]),
                   smin=jnp.where(better[..., None], jnp.swapaxes(s_low, 1, 2), c["smin"]),
                   acc=c["acc"] + acc.sum((0, 1)), t=c["t"] + 1, key=key)
        trace = (E.mean((0, 1)) / N, (q**2).mean(0), lab[0, 0].astype(jnp.int8))   # [E]/N (K,), [q^2] (K,), labels (K,)
        return new, trace

    def measure(c, _):
        c, tr = lax.scan(one_sweep, c, None, length=meas_every)
        q_int = jnp.sum(c["s"][:, :, 0] * c["s"][:, :, 1], axis=1).astype(jnp.int16)   # (D, K): N q
        return c, (tr, q_int)

    def block(c, _):
        c, (tr, q_int) = lax.scan(measure, c, None, length=n_meas)
        return c, (tr, q_int, jnp.transpose(c["s"], (0, 2, 3, 1)).astype(jnp.int8))   # snapshot (D, 2, K, N)

    c, tr_eq = lax.scan(one_sweep, carry0, None, length=n_equil)
    c, (tr_pr, q_int, conf) = lax.scan(block, c, None, length=n_save)
    flat = lambda a: a.reshape((-1,) + a.shape[3:])                             # (n_save, n_meas, meas_every, K) -> (sweeps, K)
    traces = [jnp.concatenate([tr_eq[i], flat(tr_pr[i])]) for i in range(3)]
    return dict(e_trace=traces[0], q2_trace=traces[1], label_trace=traces[2],
                q_int=jnp.transpose(q_int.reshape(-1, D, K), (1, 2, 0)),        # (D, K, n_save * n_meas)
                configs=jnp.transpose(conf, (1, 2, 3, 0, 4)),                   # (D, 2, K, n_save, N)
                E_min=c["emin"], s_min=c["smin"].astype(jnp.int8), acc_count=c["acc"], n_sweeps=c["t"])

5.4 An exact equilibration test for Gaussian couplings

How do we know that a simulation of \(N=128\) spins, where no exact answer is available, has reached equilibrium? For Gaussian couplings there is an exact identity between the energy and the overlap [Katzgraber, Palassini & Young 2001]. Its two ingredients:

  • Gaussian integration by parts (Stein’s lemma): for \(x\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) and a smooth function \(g\), \(\ [x\,g(x)]=\sigma^2\,[g'(x)]\). It follows from \(\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}=-\frac{x}{\sigma^2}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}\) and one integration by parts.
  • Derivative of a correlation with respect to its own coupling: \(J_{ij}\) enters \(Z\) through \(e^{\beta J_{ij}s_is_j}\), so \(\partial\langle s_is_j\rangle/\partial J_{ij}=\beta\big(\langle(s_is_j)^2\rangle-\langle s_is_j\rangle^2\big)=\beta\big(1-\langle s_is_j\rangle^2\big)\).

Apply both to the disorder-averaged energy, \([U]=-\sum_{i<j}[J_{ij}\langle s_is_j\rangle]=-\sum_{i<j}\sigma^2\beta\big(1-[\langle s_is_j\rangle^2]\big)\), and use \(\sum_{i<j}\langle s_is_j\rangle^2=\frac12\big(N^2\langle q^2\rangle-N\big)\) (Section 4.3). With \(\sigma^2=J^2/N\) everything combines into \[ \boxed{\;\frac{[U]}{N}=-\frac{\beta J^2}{2}\Big(1-\big[\langle q^2\rangle\big]\Big)\;} \] exactly, for every \(N\) and every temperature. In a simulation that starts from random configurations the two sides approach the equilibrium value from opposite directions: the energy decreases from \(0\), while the right-hand side starts near \(-\beta J^2/2\) (no overlap yet) and increases. Equality of the two sides (within errors) is a necessary condition for equilibrium. At high temperature \([\langle q^2\rangle]\to0\) and the identity reduces to the annealed \(u=-\beta J^2/2\).

5.6 The overlap distribution

From the measured overlaps we build \(P_J(q)\) for every sample (a histogram over the \(N+1\) possible values \(q=-1,-1+2/N,\dots,1\)) and average over samples. We plot the density \(P(q)\), i.e. the probability of each value divided by the spacing \(2/N\), so that curves for different \(N\) can be compared. The global flip symmetry makes every \(P_J(q)\) exactly symmetric, \(P_J(q)=P_J(-q)\); a finite run visits the mirror valleys only a limited number of times, so we symmetrise the histogram of each sample, which uses this exact symmetry to reduce the noise.

Code 
def overlap_hist(d):
    """P_J(q) for every sample and temperature: array (D, K, N+1) of probabilities of q = -1, -1+2/N, ..., 1,
    symmetrised, P_J(q) -> [P_J(q) + P_J(-q)]/2 (an exact symmetry; it halves the statistical noise of a finite run)."""
    N = int(d["N"])
    idx = (d["q_int"].astype(np.int64) + N) // 2                               # 0 .. N
    D, K, n = idx.shape
    P = np.zeros((D, K, N + 1))
    np.add.at(P, (np.arange(D)[:, None, None], np.arange(K)[None, :, None], idx), 1.0)
    return 0.5 * (P + P[..., ::-1]) / n

5.7 The spin-glass susceptibility and the Binder cumulant

Spin-glass susceptibility. \(\chi_{\rm SG}=N[\langle q^2\rangle]\). Above \(T_c\) it should approach \(1/(1-\beta^2J^2)\) (Section 4.4); below \(T_c\), where \([\langle q^2\rangle]\) stays finite, it grows proportionally to \(N\).

Binder cumulant. The dimensionless ratio \[ g(T,N)=\frac12\left(3-\frac{[\langle q^4\rangle]}{[\langle q^2\rangle]^2}\right) \] is \(0\) for a Gaussian \(P(q)\) (because \(\langle q^4\rangle=3\langle q^2\rangle^2\)) and \(1\) for two delta peaks at \(\pm q_0\). Near a continuous transition, finite-size scaling says that \(g\) depends on \(T\) and \(N\) only through one combination. For the SK model this combination is \((T-T_c)N^{1/3}\) (mean-field exponents with the upper critical dimension \(d=6\) and \(\nu=1/2\), so \(N^{1/(\nu d)}=N^{1/3}\)) [Billoire 2007]. Consequently \(g(T_c,N)\) does not depend on \(N\): the curves for different \(N\) cross at \(T_c\).

We compute both quantities from the per-sample moments \(\langle q^2\rangle\), \(\langle q^4\rangle\), and estimate errors with a bootstrap over the disorder samples (200 resamplings of the \(D\) samples with replacement).

def overlap_moments(d):
    """Per-sample thermal moments <q^2>, <q^4>: arrays (D, K)."""
    q = d["q_int"].astype(np.float64) / int(d["N"])
    return (q**2).mean(-1), (q**4).mean(-1)

def binder(q2, q4):
    return 0.5 * (3 - q4.mean(0) / q2.mean(0) ** 2)

def crossing(T, g1, g2, window=(0.6, 1.4)):
    """Temperature in the window where g1 - g2 changes sign (linear interpolation); NaN if it does not."""
    sel = (T >= window[0]) & (T <= window[1])
    T, dlt = T[sel], (g1 - g2)[sel]
    i = np.where(np.sign(dlt[:-1]) != np.sign(dlt[1:]))[0]
    if len(i) == 0:
        return np.nan
    i = i[0]
    return T[i] - dlt[i] * (T[i + 1] - T[i]) / (dlt[i + 1] - dlt[i])

6. The energy landscape

6.1 The ground-state energy per spin

Finding the ground state of one SK sample is a hard optimisation problem (Section 1.2). For \(N\le24\) exact enumeration gives it (Section 2.4). For \(N=32,64,128\) we use the parallel-tempering runs: the lowest energy visited by each replica during the run (E_min), improved by a zero-temperature descent: flip, one at a time, every spin anti-aligned with its local field (\(s_ih_i<0\), so \(\Delta E_i<0\)) until no such spin is left. The descent is also applied to all snapshots stored at the lowest temperature. The result is an upper bound on the true ground-state energy of each sample. A useful indicator of its quality: the two replicas search independently, so if both find the same lowest energy, it is very likely the ground state.

For the extrapolation to \(N\to\infty\) we use the form \([E_0]/N=e_\infty+a\,N^{-2/3}\); the exponent \(2/3\) of the finite-size correction is supported by extensive ground-state computations for the SK model [Boettcher 2005].

def descent_columns(s, J, block):
    """Zero-temperature descent of C chains of one sample, s: (N, C), J: (N, N). The sweep of Section 5.2 at beta -> infinity
    (beta = infinity, u = 1/2, so the threshold is 0): a spin flips if and only if s_i h_i < 0. Sweeps are repeated until no spin flips."""
    big = jnp.full(s.shape[1], jnp.inf)
    half = jnp.full(s.shape, 0.5)
    def body(c):
        s, h, _ = c
        s2, h2 = sweep_sample(s, h, J, big, half, block)
        return s2, h2, jnp.any(s2 != s)
    s, _, _ = lax.while_loop(lambda c: c[2], body, (s, J @ s, True))
    return s

7. The Mattis model: random signs without a glass

7.1 A ferromagnet in disguise

Mattis proposed couplings that look as random as the SK couplings but are not frustrated [Mattis 1976]: \[ J_{ij}=\frac{J}{N}\,\xi_i\xi_j,\qquad \xi_i=\pm1\ \text{random and fixed}. \] Half of the couplings are negative. But the gauge transformation of Section 2.3 with \(\tau_i=\xi_i\), i.e. \(\sigma_i=\xi_is_i\), gives \[ H=-\frac JN\sum_{i<j}\xi_i\xi_js_is_j=-\frac JN\sum_{i<j}\sigma_i\sigma_j=-\frac{J}{2N}\Big[\Big(\sum_i\sigma_i\Big)^2-N\Big], \] the Curie–Weiss ferromagnet in the variables \(\sigma_i\). All of its thermodynamics follows: a transition at \(T_c=J\), below which the Mattis magnetisation \(m_\xi=\frac1N\sum_i\xi_is_i\) solves \(m_\xi=\tanh(\beta Jm_\xi)\). The ordinary magnetisation \(m=\frac1N\sum_is_i\) stays of order \(1/\sqrt N\): a snapshot looks just as random as an SK snapshot. The order is in the hidden pattern \(\boldsymbol\xi\). (This is the Hopfield network with one stored pattern.)

Consequences we check: no frustrated loops (Section 3.3), only two metastable states, a \(P(q)\) with two sharp peaks at \(\pm m_\xi^2\) (two replicas both aligned with \(\pm\boldsymbol\xi\)) and no weight in between, and a Binder cumulant that goes to \(1\) below \(T_c\).

8. The information is in pairs of replicas

8.1 A single snapshot, pooled over samples, is uniformly random

The gauge symmetry makes a sharp statement about what a single snapshot can reveal. Let \(p_J(\boldsymbol s)\) be the Boltzmann distribution of sample \(J\), and pool snapshots from many samples, as a data set of the kind produced in Section 5 does. The pooled distribution is \([p_J(\boldsymbol s)]\). For any signs \(\tau\), \[ \big[p_J(\tau\boldsymbol s)\big]=\big[p_{\tau J\tau}(\boldsymbol s)\big]=\big[p_J(\boldsymbol s)\big], \] because \(p_J(\tau\boldsymbol s)=p_{\tau J\tau}(\boldsymbol s)\) (Section 2.3) and \(\tau J\tau\) has the same distribution as \(J\). Every configuration can be reached from any other by some \(\tau\), so \[ \big[p_J(\boldsymbol s)\big]=2^{-N}\qquad\text{at every temperature.} \] Pooled over disorder, a single snapshot is a uniformly random string of signs, in the paramagnet and in the spin glass alike. No statistic of single snapshots (magnetisation, correlations between spins, anything) can tell the two phases apart without knowing \(J\).

For pairs of replicas the same argument with \(\tau=\boldsymbol s^a\) maps \((\boldsymbol s^a,\boldsymbol s^b)\to(\boldsymbol 1,\boldsymbol q)\), where \(\boldsymbol q=(s_1^as_1^b,\dots,s_N^as_N^b)\) is the overlap field. The pooled pair distribution depends only on \(\boldsymbol q\), and because the SK ensemble is also symmetric under permutations of the spin labels, only on the number of \(+1\)’s in \(\boldsymbol q\), i.e. on the overlap \(q\). The overlap field \(q_i=s_i^as_i^b\) is gauge invariant, and it is where the information about the phase lives. We check both statements by exact enumeration.

9. Summary

  • The model. \(H=-\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}s_is_j\) with Gaussian couplings of variance \(J^2/N\); the \(1/N\) keeps the local field of order one (SymPy and numerics, Section 2.2).
  • Two averages. Thermal averages at fixed couplings, then the average over quenched disorder of \(\ln Z\). The annealed free energy \(\ln2+\beta^2J^2/4\) is exact above \(T=J\) and fails below (negative entropy), as exact enumeration confirms (Section 2.4).
  • Frustration. A loop with a negative product of couplings cannot satisfy all its bonds; half of the SK triangles are frustrated. Frustration is gauge invariant (Section 3).
  • Order without a pattern. The magnetisation vanishes; the order parameter is the overlap of two replicas with the same couplings. Its distribution \(P(q)\) is a narrow peak at \(0\) above \(T_c\) and broad below, with sample-to-sample fluctuations (Sections 4 and 5).
  • The transition at \(T_c=J\). The spin-glass susceptibility \(1/(1-\beta^2J^2)\) diverges at \(T=J\) (Section 4.4); the Binder cumulants cross near \(T_c\) (Section 5.7). Parallel tempering, implemented in JAX with vmap over temperatures, replicas and samples, equilibrates \(N=128\) down to \(T=0.4J\), verified by an exact identity (Section 5.4).
  • The landscape. Exponentially many metastable states, slow relaxation without exchange moves, and a ground-state energy that extrapolates towards Parisi’s \(e_0=-0.7632\), well above the replica-symmetric \(-0.798\) (Section 6).
  • Mattis. Random signs without frustration give a hidden ferromagnet: sharp \(P(q)\), Binder cumulant \(\to1\), two metastable states (Section 7).
  • The information is in pairs of replicas. Pooled over samples, single snapshots are uniformly random at every temperature; pairs of replicas depend only on their overlap (Section 8).
