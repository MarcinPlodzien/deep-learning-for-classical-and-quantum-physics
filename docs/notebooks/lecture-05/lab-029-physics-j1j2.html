Lab 029 — The J1–J2 Ising model

Labs 018–020 use the \(J_1\)–\(J_2\) Ising model as a test bed: Lab 018 generates Monte Carlo snapshots, and Labs 019 and 020 ask unsupervised algorithms to find its phases without labels. To judge what an algorithm finds we must know the answer, and this notebook supplies it. It contains no machine learning; it collects the physics of the model, derived step by step and checked numerically.

The model consists of Ising spins \(s_i=\pm1\) on a square lattice, with a ferromagnetic coupling \(J_1\) between nearest neighbours and an antiferromagnetic coupling \(J_2\) between diagonal neighbours. The two couplings cannot be satisfied at the same time — the model is frustrated — and their ratio \(g=J_2/J_1\) decides which order wins.

1. Statistical mechanics and frustration

Many students of this course come from computer science or mathematics, so we begin by recalling the five objects of statistical mechanics that the notebook uses — energy, Boltzmann weight, partition function, free energy and order parameter — and then introduce the one new idea of the model, frustration. Lab 012 develops the same material at length for the ordinary Ising model.

A configuration is a vector \(\mathbf s=(s_1,\dots,s_N)\in\{-1,+1\}^N\) of \(N\) spins, and a Hamiltonian \(H(\mathbf s)\) assigns it an energy. For pair interactions \[ H(\mathbf s) = -\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}\,s_is_j , \] a bond with \(J_{ij}>0\) is ferromagnetic (energy \(-J_{ij}\) if \(s_i=s_j\), \(+J_{ij}\) otherwise) and a bond with \(J_{ij}<0\) is antiferromagnetic. We call a bond satisfied when it has its lower energy.

In thermal equilibrium at temperature \(T\) a configuration occurs with the Boltzmann probability \[ p(\mathbf s) = \frac{e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)}}{Z},\qquad \beta=\frac1T,\qquad Z(T) = \sum_{\mathbf s}e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)} . \] Here the normalisation \(Z\) is the partition function. At low \(T\) the low-energy configurations dominate; at high \(T\) all \(2^N\) configurations become equally likely.

All thermal averages follow from the free energy per spin \(f = -\frac{T}{N}\ln Z\): the energy per spin is \(u = \partial(\beta f)/\partial\beta = \langle H\rangle/N\), the specific heat per spin is \(c = \partial u/\partial T = (\langle H^2\rangle-\langle H\rangle^2)/(NT^2)\), and the entropy per spin is \(s=(u-f)/T\). A phase transition is a temperature at which \(f\), in the limit \(N\to\infty\), is not an analytic function of \(T\).

An ordered phase is recognised by an order parameter, a quantity that is non-zero in it and zero in the disordered (paramagnetic) phase; for a ferromagnet this is the magnetisation \(m = \frac1N\sum_is_i\). Since the Hamiltonian is invariant under \(\mathbf s\to-\mathbf s\), \(\langle m\rangle=0\) on any finite lattice, and one therefore uses \(\langle|m|\rangle\) or \(\langle m^2\rangle\). Choosing the order parameter requires knowing what pattern the spins form, which for our model is the subject of Sections 2–3.

1.1 Frustration on a triangle of bonds

On the square lattice with only ferromagnetic bonds all bonds can be satisfied at once, by setting all spins up. A diagonal antiferromagnetic bond changes this: it closes a triangle of two nearest-neighbour bonds and one diagonal bond, and the three bonds of this triangle cannot all be satisfied. Take sites \(1,2,3\) with \(1\)–\(2\) and \(2\)–\(3\) nearest neighbours and \(1\)–\(3\) diagonal neighbours: \[ H_\triangle = -J_1(s_1s_2+s_2s_3) + J_2\,s_1s_3 . \] Satisfying both \(J_1\) bonds requires \(s_1=s_2=s_3\); then \(s_1s_3=+1\) and the \(J_2\) bond is broken. Satisfying the \(J_2\) bond requires \(s_1=-s_3\), and then \(s_2\) agrees with only one of them.

The sketch below shows three of the eight states of the triangle, with the \(J_1\) bonds \(1\)–\(2\) and \(2\)–\(3\) between nearest neighbours and the \(J_2\) bond \(1\)–\(3\) on the diagonal. A solid line is a satisfied bond, a dashed purple line with a cross a broken one; a navy disc is \(s=+1\) and a white disc with an orange rim \(s=-1\), the convention of all sketches in this course. Whatever we do, one bond stays broken: aligning all three spins breaks the diagonal, and turning one end spin to satisfy the diagonal breaks a nearest-neighbour bond.

The same reasoning gives a general criterion. Going around a closed loop of bonds, multiply the signs of the couplings (ferromagnetic \(+\), antiferromagnetic \(-\)); if the product is negative, at least one bond on the loop is broken in every configuration. To see this, note that the product of the bond variables \(s_is_j\) around a loop is \(\prod_i s_i^2=+1\); a satisfied bond has \(s_is_j=\operatorname{sign}J_{ij}\), so if all bonds were satisfied the product of signs would be \(+1\). Our triangle has signs \((+,+,-)\), so it is frustrated.

To make this concrete, the next cell lists the states of the triangle with SymPy, writes their energies as symbolic functions of \(J_1,J_2\), and counts the broken bonds. Look for the minimum number of broken bonds over all eight states: if the criterion is right, it cannot be zero.

def H_triangle(s1, s2, s3):
    # energy of the triangle: two nearest-neighbour bonds (1-2, 2-3) and one diagonal bond (1-3)
    return -J1s * (s1 * s2 + s2 * s3) + J2s * s1 * s3

The output confirms the criterion: every state of the triangle breaks at least one bond, and two kinds of state compete: \((+,+,+)\) with energy \(-2J_1+J_2\) (the diagonal bond broken) and \((+,+,-)\), \((+,-,-)\) with energy \(-J_2\) (one nearest-neighbour bond broken). For an isolated triangle the first wins when \(-2J_1+J_2<-J_2\), i.e. \(J_2<J_1\). On the lattice every nearest-neighbour bond is shared by several triangles, so the balance point moves; Section 3 shows that it lies at \(J_2=J_1/2\). Frustration does not by itself say which state wins. It says that some bonds must be broken, and the question becomes which compromise has the lowest energy and how many compromises of equal energy exist.

2. Hamiltonian, symmetries and order parameters

Before computing anything we need the symmetries of the model. They decide which ordered states are degenerate, which order parameter measures them and — through the symmetry group of the order parameter — which universality class a continuous transition can belong to. We establish them once here and use them in every later section.

The spins \(s_{x,y}=\pm1\) live on an \(L\times L\) square lattice with periodic boundaries (a torus), \(x,y\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}\), \(N=L^2\), and the Hamiltonian is \[ \begin{aligned} H(\mathbf s) &= -J_1\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}s_is_j + J_2\sum_{\langle\langle i,j\rangle\rangle}s_is_j\\ &= -J_1\,B_1(\mathbf s) + J_2\,B_2(\mathbf s),\qquad g=\frac{J_2}{J_1}\ge0 , \end{aligned} \] which is the convention of Lab 018. Here \(\langle i,j\rangle\) runs over the \(2N\) nearest-neighbour bonds (horizontal \((x,y)\)–\((x+1,y)\) and vertical \((x,y)\)–\((x,y+1)\)), \(\langle\langle i,j\rangle\rangle\) over the \(2N\) diagonal bonds (\((x,y)\)–\((x+1,y+1)\) and \((x,y)\)–\((x+1,y-1)\)), and \(B_1=\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j\), \(B_2=\sum_{\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle}s_is_j\) are integers in \([-2N,2N]\). With \(J_1=1\) the energy is \(H=-B_1+gB_2\), so computing \(B_1,B_2\) once gives the energy for every \(g\). We also split \(B_1=B_1^{x}+B_1^{y}\) into horizontal and vertical bonds.

The figure below shows the lattice and its two kinds of bonds. Every site \(i\) has four nearest neighbours, coupled by \(J_1\) (solid blue), and four diagonal neighbours, coupled by \(J_2\) (dashed green). The ferromagnetic \(J_1\) favours parallel nearest neighbours, the antiferromagnetic \(J_2\) favours antiparallel diagonal neighbours, and no configuration satisfies both; the ratio \(g=J_2/J_1\) decides which compromise has the lowest energy.

A symmetry is a map of configurations that leaves \(H\) unchanged, and the model has two kinds of them. The global spin flip \(\mathbf s\to-\mathbf s\) leaves every product \(s_is_j\) unchanged; this is the \(\mathbb Z_2\) symmetry of every Ising model. The lattice symmetries — translations by one site, rotation by \(90^\circ\) and reflections — map nearest-neighbour bonds onto nearest-neighbour bonds and diagonal bonds onto diagonal bonds.

Two further transformations change the signs of a subset of spins. They are not symmetries of \(H\) but gauge transformations, which map it onto another Hamiltonian, and both will be useful later. The checkerboard gauge \(s_{x,y}\to(-1)^{x+y}s_{x,y}\) changes the sign of every nearest-neighbour product, because nearest neighbours have opposite parity of \(x+y\), while diagonal neighbours have equal parity and their products are unchanged: \(B_1\to-B_1\), \(B_2\to B_2\). Consequently the model with antiferromagnetic \(J_1\) has exactly the same thermodynamics as ours, with Néel order mapped onto ferromagnetic order. Several papers in Section 10 use \(J_1<0\) (nearest-neighbour antiferromagnet) or write \(J_1<0\) for the ferromagnet; their phase diagrams apply here without change. The column gauge \(s_{x,y}\to(-1)^{x}s_{x,y}\) acts differently: horizontal and diagonal bonds join different \(x\) parities and change sign, vertical bonds do not, so \(B_1^x\to-B_1^x\), \(B_1^y\to B_1^y\), \(B_2\to-B_2\). Section 5 uses it for the limit \(g\to\infty\).

def bond_sums(s):
    # s: (..., L, L) array of +-1; axis -2 is x, axis -1 is y; periodic boundaries.
    # Returns (B1x, B1y, B2): sums of s_i s_j over horizontal, vertical and diagonal bonds (each bond counted once), integer arrays of shape (...).
    s = np.asarray(s, dtype=np.int64)
    ax = (-2, -1)
    b1x = (s * np.roll(s, -1, -2)).sum(ax)                                    # (x,y)-(x+1,y)
    b1y = (s * np.roll(s, -1, -1)).sum(ax)                                    # (x,y)-(x,y+1)
    b2 = (s * np.roll(s, (-1, -1), ax)).sum(ax) + (s * np.roll(s, (-1, 1), ax)).sum(ax)   # (x,y)-(x+1,y+1) and (x,y)-(x+1,y-1)
    return b1x, b1y, b2

def energy(s, g, J1=1.0):
    # H = -J1 B1 + J2 B2 with J2 = g J1; shape (...)
    b1x, b1y, b2 = bond_sums(s)
    return -J1 * (b1x + b1y) + g * J1 * b2

2.1 Stripe order parameter and its symmetry group

Periodic patterns of spins are described by their Fourier components \[ \begin{aligned} m_{\boldsymbol k}(\mathbf s) &= \frac1N\sum_{\boldsymbol r}s_{\boldsymbol r}\,e^{i\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol r},\\ \boldsymbol r&=(x,y),\quad \boldsymbol k\in\Big\{\tfrac{2\pi}{L}(n_x,n_y)\Big\}. \end{aligned} \] Section 3 derives three candidate orders as ground states; the table lists their patterns, wave vectors and order parameters.

order pattern \(s_{x,y}\) wave vector order parameter
ferromagnet \(\pm1\) \((0,0)\) \(m = m_{(0,0)}\)
stripes \(\pm(-1)^x\) (vertical) or \(\pm(-1)^y\) (horizontal) \((\pi,0)\), \((0,\pi)\) \(m_{\rm str}=\sqrt{m_x^2+m_y^2}\), \(\ m_x\equiv m_{(\pi,0)},\ m_y\equiv m_{(0,\pi)}\)
Néel \(\pm(-1)^{x+y}\) \((\pi,\pi)\) \(m_{(\pi,\pi)}\)

For \(\boldsymbol k\) with components \(0\) or \(\pi\), \(e^{i\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol r}=\pm1\) and \(m_{\boldsymbol k}\) is real.

The stripe phase has four ground states, two orientations times the global flip. In the plane \((m_x,m_y)\) they sit at \((\pm1,0)\) (vertical stripes) and \((0,\pm1)\) (horizontal stripes), whereas the ferromagnet has only two states, \(m=\pm1\), on a line.

The lattice symmetries act on the stripe order parameter in a simple way. A translation \(x\to x+1\) changes the sign of \((-1)^{x}\), so \((m_x,m_y)\to(-m_x,m_y)\), and a translation in \(y\) gives \((m_x,-m_y)\). A rotation by \(90^\circ\), \((x,y)\to(-y,x)\), turns the factor \((-1)^x\) into \((-1)^y\), so \((m_x,m_y)\to(m_y,m_x)\). The global flip sends \((m_x,m_y)\to(-m_x,-m_y)\). A sign change of one component together with the exchange of the two components generate the eight symmetries of a square, the dihedral group \(D_4\), while the ferromagnetic order parameter sees only \(\mathbb Z_2\) (\(m\to-m\)).

This difference matters. An order parameter with two components and the symmetry of a square is exactly that of the Ashkin–Teller model [Ashkin & Teller 1943], two Ising spins \(\sigma_i,\tau_i\) per site coupled by \(-K_2(\sigma_i\sigma_j+\tau_i\tau_j)-K_4\,\sigma_i\sigma_j\tau_i\tau_j\). The four stripe states correspond to the four values of \((\sigma,\tau)\); one possible identification is \(\sigma=\operatorname{sign}(m_x+m_y)\), \(\tau=\operatorname{sign}(m_x-m_y)\). This is why the stripe transition, unlike the ferromagnetic one, can belong to the Ashkin–Teller class, whose critical exponents vary continuously along a line [Jin, Sen, Sandvik 2012]. Section 5 shows the same structure from the \(g\to\infty\) limit, where \(\sigma\) and \(\tau\) become the magnetisations of two decoupled sublattices.

The first line also confirms that the four states have the same energy.

3. Ground states

We now turn to the lowest temperatures. At \(T=0\) the Boltzmann weight selects the configurations of minimum energy. They tell us which orders can appear at low temperature, and their number, the degeneracy, tells us how strongly thermal fluctuations will compete with them. For this model the answer changes qualitatively at one value of \(g\).

3.1 Bond counting

We start by comparing the energies of the three simple periodic patterns. Every spin has 4 nearest neighbours and 4 diagonal neighbours; each bond is shared by two spins, so there are 2 nearest-neighbour bonds and 2 diagonal bonds per spin, so for a periodic pattern the energy per spin is the average over one unit cell of \(-J_1(s_{x,y}s_{x+1,y}+s_{x,y}s_{x,y+1})+J_2(s_{x,y}s_{x+1,y+1}+s_{x,y}s_{x+1,y-1})\).

For the ferromagnet (all \(+1\)) both nearest-neighbour products and both diagonal products are \(+1\), so both diagonal bonds are broken and \(E/N=-2J_1+2J_2\). For the stripes \((-1)^x\) the horizontal product is \(-1\) and the vertical one \(+1\), which cancel, while both diagonal products are \(-1\): \(E/N=-2J_2\). For the Néel state \((-1)^{x+y}\) both nearest-neighbour products are \(-1\) and both diagonal products \(+1\): \(E/N=+2J_1+2J_2\).

The figure below draws the four patterns on a \(4\times4\) patch with every bond marked: thick lines are \(J_1\) bonds, thin lines \(J_2\) bonds, solid when satisfied and dashed purple when broken. The Néel state, drawn for contrast, breaks every bond and is never a ground state for \(J_1>0\); it is the ground state of the antiferromagnetic nearest-neighbour model, which the checkerboard gauge of Section 2 maps onto our ferromagnet.

3.2 Plaquette bound

Section 3.1 compared three guesses; a proof must exclude every other configuration. We obtain one by writing \(H\) as a sum over the \(N\) elementary squares (plaquettes) and bounding each term from below.

Label the corners of the plaquette with lower-left corner \((x,y)\) as \(a=(x,y)\), \(b=(x+1,y)\), \(c=(x+1,y+1)\), \(d=(x,y+1)\). Every nearest-neighbour bond is an edge of exactly two plaquettes, and every diagonal bond is a diagonal of exactly one plaquette. Therefore \[ H = \sum_{P}h_P,\qquad h_P = -\frac{J_1}{2}\big(s_as_b+s_bs_c+s_cs_d+s_ds_a\big) + J_2\big(s_as_c+s_bs_d\big). \] It follows that \(H\ge N\min h_P\), where the minimum runs over the 16 states of one plaquette. If a configuration reaches the minimum on every plaquette simultaneously, it is a ground state and the bound is the ground-state energy.

The cell lists \(h_P\) for all 16 plaquette states with SymPy (with \(J_1=1\), \(J_2=g\)), groups them by energy, finds the lower envelope, and checks numerically that the plaquette energies indeed add up to \(H\).

3.4 Zero-temperature transfer matrix

The degeneracy \(n_0=30\) at \(g=1/2\) is the first sign of something special, and to understand it we need larger lattices. Enumeration stops at about \(5\times5\) (\(2^{25}\) states). A transfer matrix performs the sum over configurations row by row, at a cost exponential only in the width, and at \(T\to0\) it turns into a shortest-path calculation that also counts the shortest paths.

Group the spins of an \(L\times M\) torus into rows \(\sigma_y=(s_{0,y},\dots,s_{L-1,y})\), super-spins with \(2^L\) states each. The energy splits into terms within a row and terms between neighbouring rows: \[ \begin{aligned} H &= \sum_{y=0}^{M-1}W(\sigma_y,\sigma_{y+1}),\\ W(\sigma,\sigma') &= -J_1\sum_x s_xs'_x + J_2\sum_x s_x\big(s'_{x+1}+s'_{x-1}\big)\\ &\quad - \frac{J_1}{2}\sum_x\big(s_xs_{x+1}+s'_xs'_{x+1}\big), \end{aligned} \] where the bonds inside a row are split half–half between the two neighbouring factors. Then \(Z = \operatorname{Tr}\mathsf V^M\) with \(\mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'}=e^{-\beta W(\sigma,\sigma')}\) (Lab 012, Section 3).

In the limit \(\beta\to\infty\) the ordinary algebra of Boltzmann weights turns into the (min, +) algebra: \(\ e^{-\beta a}+e^{-\beta b}\simeq n\,e^{-\beta\min(a,b)}\), with \(n\) the number of terms attaining the minimum, and \(e^{-\beta a}e^{-\beta b}=e^{-\beta(a+b)}\). The matrix product \(\sum_{\sigma'}\mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'}\mathsf V_{\sigma'\sigma''}\) therefore becomes \[ \begin{aligned} (W\otimes W)_{\sigma\sigma''} &= \min_{\sigma'}\big[W_{\sigma\sigma'}+W_{\sigma'\sigma''}\big],\\ n_{\sigma\sigma''} &= \sum_{\sigma'\,\text{attaining the min}} n_{\sigma\sigma'}n_{\sigma'\sigma''} , \end{aligned} \] and \(Z\simeq n_0e^{-\beta E_0}\) with \(E_0=\min_\sigma(W^{\otimes M})_{\sigma\sigma}\) and \(n_0\) the sum of the counts on the diagonal entries attaining \(E_0\). This is a shortest-path problem (Bellman–Ford style) with path counting. The function minplus_torus below implements it with arrays of shape \((2^L,2^L)\). We first make sure that it reproduces the enumeration on the \(4\times4\) torus, and then go to tori up to \(8\times8\).

Code 
def row_terms(L):
    # for all 2^L rows of width L (periodic): intra[sigma] = sum_x s_x s_{x+1};  nn[sigma, sigma'] = sum_x s_x s'_x;
    # dd[sigma, sigma'] = sum_x s_x (s'_{x+1} + s'_{x-1}).  Shapes (2^L,), (2^L, 2^L), (2^L, 2^L).
    R = all_configs(L).astype(np.float64)
    intra = (R * np.roll(R, -1, 1)).sum(1)
    nn = R @ R.T
    dd = R @ np.roll(R, -1, 1).T + R @ np.roll(R, 1, 1).T
    return intra, nn, dd

def W_matrix(L, J1, J2):
    # row-pair energy W(sigma, sigma') of the text; shape (2^L, 2^L)
    intra, nn, dd = row_terms(L)
    return -J1 * nn + J2 * dd - 0.5 * J1 * (intra[:, None] + intra[None, :])

def minplus_mult(Ea, Ca, Eb, Cb, tol=1e-9, block=64):
    # (min,+) product with path counting; E: energies, C: counts, all (n, n)
    n = Ea.shape[0]
    E = np.empty((n, n)); C = np.empty((n, n))
    for i0 in range(0, n, block):                                     # blocks of rows keep the (block, n, n) temporary small
        tot = Ea[i0:i0 + block, :, None] + Eb[None, :, :]
        m = tot.min(1)
        hit = tot <= m[:, None, :] + tol
        E[i0:i0 + block] = m
        C[i0:i0 + block] = (hit * Ca[i0:i0 + block, :, None] * Cb[None, :, :]).sum(1)
    return E, C

def minplus_torus(L, M, g):
    # ground-state energy per spin and degeneracy of the L x M torus (J1 = 1, J2 = g)
    W = W_matrix(L, 1.0, g); C1 = np.ones_like(W)
    E, C = W.copy(), C1.copy()
    for _ in range(M - 1):
        E, C = minplus_mult(E, C, W, C1)
    d = np.diag(E); E0 = d.min()
    return E0 / (L * M), int(round(np.diag(C)[d <= E0 + 1e-9].sum()))

This growth can be derived exactly. At \(g=1/2\) a configuration is a ground state if and only if every plaquette is one of the six minimal ones (Section 3.2): the two uniform plaquettes and the four with one edge up and the opposite edge down. Describe a configuration by its bond variables \(h_{x,y}=s_{x,y}s_{x+1,y}\) (horizontal) and \(v_{x,y}=s_{x,y}s_{x,y+1}\) (vertical). Two properties characterise the six minimal plaquettes:

  1. The product of the four corner spins is \(+1\). For the plaquette at \((x,y)\) this says \(s_{x,y}s_{x+1,y}=s_{x,y+1}s_{x+1,y+1}\), i.e. \(h_{x,y}=h_{x,y+1}\): the horizontal bond variables do not depend on \(y\), \(h_{x,y}\equiv h_x\). Likewise \(v_{x,y}\equiv v_y\) does not depend on \(x\).
  2. It is not a Néel plaquette. A Néel plaquette is the only one with product \(+1\) that has a broken horizontal and a broken vertical edge. So \(h_x=-1\) and \(v_y=-1\) never occur together.

By property 2, either all \(h_x=+1\) — every row is uniform and the \(M\) row signs are free: \(2^M\) configurations — or all \(v_y=+1\) — every column is uniform: \(2^L\) configurations. The two sets share the two uniform states. Hence, on the \(L\times M\) torus, \[ \boxed{\,n_0\big(g=\tfrac12\big) = 2^{L}+2^{M}-2\,}. \] The degeneracy grows exponentially with the linear size, not with the number of spins: the entropy per spin \(\ln n_0/N\approx\ln 2/L\to0\). The ground state at \(g=1/2\) is therefore a “sub-extensively” degenerate manifold of independently flippable rows (or columns).

The same count follows from a transfer matrix, which gives an independent check. In the row language, two rows \(\sigma,\sigma'\) may follow each other if all \(L\) plaquettes between them are minimal; we define the \(0/1\) matrix \(A_{\sigma\sigma'}\) accordingly, so that \(n_0=\operatorname{Tr}A^M\). The derivation predicts the structure of \(A\): a uniform row may be followed by either uniform row (a \(2\times2\) block of ones, eigenvalues \(2\) and \(0\)), and a non-uniform row only by itself (an identity block of size \(2^L-2\)). So \(\operatorname{Tr}A^M = 2^M+(2^L-2)\), and the largest eigenvalue of \(A\) is \(2\) for every width \(L\). The cell builds \(A\) from the plaquette energies, checks its structure through its largest eigenvalue, and counts \(\operatorname{Tr}A^M\) for \(L,M\le10\); for \(L\le6\) it also checks against the full (min, +) calculation.

4. Domain walls

The ground states tell us what orders, but not up to which temperature the order survives. An ordered phase survives at \(T>0\) only if large regions of the “wrong” ordered state are rare. Such a region is bounded by a domain wall, and the competition between the energy of the wall and the number of ways to draw it decides whether order survives (Lab 012, Section 3.3). We therefore compute the energy per unit length of straight walls as a function of \(g\). There are four kinds of straight wall along a lattice axis (a vertical line between columns \(x_0\) and \(x_0+1\), or a horizontal one):

  1. Ferromagnet, \(+|-\). Across the wall, per unit length: one horizontal nearest-neighbour bond turns from satisfied to broken (\(+2J_1\)), and the two diagonal bonds crossing the wall turn from broken (\(s_is_j=+1\)) to satisfied (\(-2J_2\) each): \(\ \sigma_{\rm FM} = 2J_1-4J_2 = 2J_1(1-2g)\).
  2. Stripes, wall parallel to the stripes (a phase slip, \(\dots{+}{-}{+}\,|\,{+}{-}{+}\dots\)): the horizontal bond across the wall turns from broken to satisfied (\(-2J_1\)), the two diagonals from satisfied to broken (\(+4J_2\)): \(\ \sigma_{\parallel} = 4J_2-2J_1 = 2J_1(2g-1)\).
  3. Stripes, wall perpendicular to the stripes (all spins flipped on one side): the vertical bond turns from satisfied to broken (\(+2J_1\)), the two diagonals from satisfied to broken (\(+4J_2\)): \(\ \sigma_{\perp} = 2J_1+4J_2\).
  4. Stripes, a \(90^\circ\) wall between vertical and horizontal stripes. Here a bond-by-bond count near the wall is misleading, because a finite domain of horizontal stripes has a different share of satisfied horizontal and vertical bonds than a domain of vertical stripes. Count the totals instead. Nearest-neighbour bonds: in both stripe orientations half of them are satisfied, \(B_1=0\), and the horizontal bonds crossing the wall have products that alternate along it and sum to zero, so \(\Delta B_1=0\). Diagonal bonds: the two per unit length that cross the wall change from \(s_is_j=-1\) to products that alternate along the wall, i.e. from energy \(-J_2\) to \(0\) on average: \(\ \sigma_{90^\circ} = 2J_2\).

The four walls are drawn below on \(8\times6\) patches; the purple line is the wall and \(\sigma\) its energy per unit length. In the ferromagnet and for the stripe wall parallel to the stripes, crossing the wall exchanges broken nearest-neighbour bonds for satisfied diagonal bonds or the reverse, which is why their cost is proportional to \(1-2g\) or \(2g-1\).

The wall energies can be turned into a temperature scale by a Peierls-type argument. Consider a closed wall of length \(\ell\) (in lattice units) enclosing a region of the wrong ordered state. Its energy is \(\sigma\ell\). The number of closed walls of length \(\ell\) through a given bond grows at most like \(\mu^\ell\) with some constant \(\mu\) (for walls drawn along lattice bonds, \(\mu\le3\), since each step can go in at most three directions without stepping back). The free energy of such walls is therefore \[ \Delta F(\ell) \gtrsim \ell\,\big(\sigma - T\ln\mu\big). \] While \(\sigma>T\ln\mu\), long walls are exponentially suppressed and the order survives: this is Peierls’ argument for the ferromagnet [Peierls 1936; rigorous form Griffiths 1964], which gives the estimate \(T_c\gtrsim 2J_1/\ln3\approx1.82\,J_1\) at \(g=0\) (Lab 012), consistent with the exact \(2.269\,J_1\). For our model the argument is a heuristic (other wall shapes, corners and the extra stripe walls would have to be counted), but its two conclusions are robust. First, the temperature scale below which walls are suppressed is proportional to the cheapest wall energy: \(\sigma_{\min}(g) = 2J_1(1-2g)\) on the ferromagnetic side; on the stripe side \(\sigma_{\min}=\min(2J_1(2g-1),\,2J_2)\), i.e. the parallel wall for \(1/2<g<1\) and the \(90^\circ\) wall for \(g>1\). Second, at \(g=1/2\) this energy vanishes. Walls along rows cost nothing — exactly the free row flips of Section 3.5 — and nothing prevents the order from being destroyed by thermal fluctuations at any \(T>0\). Near \(g=1/2\) the ordering temperature must therefore go to zero.

We plot the estimate \(T_{\rm wall}(g)=\sigma_{\min}(g)/\ln3\) together with the computed \(T_c(g)\) in Section 9; it is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a prediction of \(T_c\).

In short, straight domain walls cost \(2J_1(1-2g)\) per unit length in the ferromagnet; in the stripe phase the parallel wall costs \(2J_1(2g-1)\), the \(90^\circ\) wall \(2J_2\) and the perpendicular wall \(2J_1+4J_2\). The cheapest wall on each side vanishes linearly at \(g=1/2\) — the physical reason why the transition temperature drops to zero there.

5. Exact limits

Before computing \(T_c(g)\) numerically we fix its two ends, where the model can be solved exactly. These limits anchor the phase diagram, test every numerical method below, and the large-\(g\) limit explains the Ashkin–Teller structure of Section 2.

5.1 Onsager limit

At \(J_2=0\) the model is the square-lattice Ising ferromagnet, solved by Onsager [1944]: \(T_c\) is fixed by \(\sinh(2J_1/T_c)=1\) (Lab 012, Kramers–Wannier duality), so \(T_c = 2J_1/\ln(1+\sqrt2)\).

5.2 Decoupled sublattices at large \(g\)

Dividing by \(J_2\) gives \(H/J_2 = B_2 - B_1/g\), so for \(g\to\infty\) only the diagonal bonds remain. A diagonal bond joins \((x,y)\) to \((x\pm1,y\pm1)\), which has the same parity of \(x+y\). The lattice splits into two sublattices, \(\mathcal A\) (\(x+y\) even) and \(\mathcal B\) (\(x+y\) odd), with no bond between them. Each is itself a square lattice, rotated by \(45^\circ\) and with lattice constant \(\sqrt2\) (primitive vectors \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\)); on it the diagonal bonds are nearest-neighbour bonds with antiferromagnetic coupling \(J_2\).

The antiferromagnetic sign is removed by the column gauge \(s_{x,y}\to(-1)^xs_{x,y}\), which changes the sign of every diagonal product (Section 2): \[ \begin{aligned} Z(J_1=0,J_2) &= \sum_{\mathbf s}e^{-\beta J_2B_2(\mathbf s)} = \sum_{\mathbf s}e^{+\beta J_2B_2(\mathbf s)}\\ &= Z^{\rm Ising}_{\mathcal A}(J_2)\;Z^{\rm Ising}_{\mathcal B}(J_2), \end{aligned} \] because the gauge is a bijection of the configuration space. Each factor is a square-lattice Ising ferromagnet with coupling \(J_2\), so \[ \begin{aligned} T_c &\xrightarrow{g\to\infty} \frac{2J_2}{\ln(1+\sqrt2)} = g\cdot\frac{2J_1}{\ln(1+\sqrt2)},\\ \frac{T_c}{J_2}&\to2.269 . \end{aligned} \]

The gauge also exposes the Ashkin–Teller variables. After it, vertical stripes \((-1)^x\) become all \(+1\), and horizontal stripes \((-1)^y\) become \((-1)^{x+y}\): \(+1\) on \(\mathcal A\), \(-1\) on \(\mathcal B\). So the four stripe states are the four combinations of the two sublattice magnetisations \((\sigma_{\mathcal A},\sigma_{\mathcal B})=(\pm1,\pm1)\): two Ising order parameters, as in the Ashkin–Teller model. A finite \(J_1\) couples the two sublattices. In the gauged frame the nearest-neighbour bonds are ferromagnetic vertically and antiferromagnetic horizontally, so a spin of \(\mathcal A\) in a uniformly magnetised \(\mathcal B\) feels \(J_1(\sigma_{\mathcal B}+\sigma_{\mathcal B}-\sigma_{\mathcal B}-\sigma_{\mathcal B})=0\): \(J_1\) does not couple the magnetisations linearly — it cannot prefer either orientation, which is the fourfold degeneracy again. Kalz, Honecker and Moliner [2011] derived, by perturbation in \(J_1\) around the two decoupled critical Ising models, that the effective theory is the Ashkin–Teller model.

6. Mean-field theory

Mean-field theory is the first approximation one tries for any interacting system: it reduces the many-body problem to one spin in an effective field. It is worth deriving carefully, because its failure here is instructive: it gets the ordered phases right, the transition temperatures wrong, and misses the vanishing of \(T_c\) at \(g=1/2\) entirely.

Write \(H=-\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}s_is_j\) with \(J_{ij}=J_1\) for nearest neighbours and \(J_{ij}=-J_2\) for diagonal neighbours. Spin \(i\) interacts with its neighbours through the local field \(\sum_jJ_{ij}s_j\). Mean-field theory replaces the neighbours by their averages \(m_j=\langle s_j\rangle\), i.e. neglects the correlations between \(s_i\) and its neighbours. Since a single spin in a field \(h_i\) has \(\langle s_i\rangle = \frac{e^{\beta h_i}-e^{-\beta h_i}}{e^{\beta h_i}+e^{-\beta h_i}}=\tanh\beta h_i\), we obtain \[ m_i = \tanh\Big(\beta\sum_jJ_{ij}m_j\Big)\qquad\text{(self-consistency).} \]

To solve these equations we try modulated solutions \(m_{\boldsymbol r}=m\,e^{i\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol r}\) with \(\boldsymbol k\in\{(0,0),(\pi,0),(0,\pi),(\pi,\pi)\}\) (then \(e^{i\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol r}=\pm1\)). The local field becomes \(\sum_jJ_{ij}m_j = \mathcal J(\boldsymbol k)\,m_i\) with the Fourier transform of the couplings \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal J(\boldsymbol k) &= \sum_{\boldsymbol\delta}J_{\boldsymbol\delta}e^{i\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol\delta}\\ &= 2J_1(\cos k_x+\cos k_y) - 4J_2\cos k_x\cos k_y , \end{aligned} \] (\(\boldsymbol\delta\) runs over the 4 nearest and 4 diagonal neighbour vectors) and the self-consistency reduces to the single equation \(m=\tanh(\beta\mathcal J(\boldsymbol k)m)\).

The transition temperature follows from the expansion for small \(m\), \(\tanh z = z - z^3/3+O(z^5)\): \[ \begin{aligned} m &= \beta\mathcal J m - \tfrac13(\beta\mathcal J)^3m^3\\ \Rightarrow\quad m&=0\ \ \text{or}\ \ m^2 = \frac{3T^2(T_{\rm MF}-T)}{T_{\rm MF}^3}\approx 3\Big(1-\frac{T}{T_{\rm MF}}\Big),\\ T_{\rm MF}&=\mathcal J(\boldsymbol k). \end{aligned} \] A non-zero solution exists below \(T_{\rm MF}\), with \(m\propto(T_{\rm MF}-T)^{1/2}\) (the mean-field exponent \(\beta=1/2\); the exact 2D Ising value is \(1/8\)). For general \(m_i\), the linearised equation \(m_i=\beta\sum_jJ_{ij}m_j\) first has a non-zero solution when \(T\) equals the largest eigenvalue of the matrix \(J_{ij}\), i.e. \(\max_{\boldsymbol k}\mathcal J(\boldsymbol k)\) (the matrix is diagonalised by plane waves). Evaluating at the candidate wave vectors gives \[ \begin{aligned} T^{\rm FM}_{\rm MF}&=\mathcal J(0,0)=4(J_1-J_2),\\ T^{\rm str}_{\rm MF}&=\mathcal J(\pi,0)=4J_2,\\ \mathcal J(\pi,\pi)&=-4(J_1+J_2)<0 . \end{aligned} \] The cell derives these results with SymPy and also shows that at \(g=1/2\) the maximum of \(\mathcal J(\boldsymbol k)\) is reached on whole lines of the Brillouin zone.

The second failure is the point \(g=1/2\). Mean field predicts \(T_{\rm MF}=2J_1\) there, the lowest point of the V but still a finite temperature. The truth is \(T_c\to0\) (Sections 4, 8, 9). The white contour shows the reason: at \(g=1/2\) the maximum of \(\mathcal J(\boldsymbol k)\) is reached on the two lines \(k_x=0\) and \(k_y=0\), \(2L-1\) wave vectors on an \(L\times L\) torus (the multiplicity \(23=2\cdot12-1\) printed above). A mode \((0,k_y)\) is a pattern uniform along \(x\) with an arbitrary profile along \(y\): the row-flip degeneracy of Section 3.5. Mean field picks one mode and orders it; in reality the system fluctuates between all of them, and no order survives at \(T>0\).

The third failure concerns the nature of the transition. Mean field always gives a continuous transition with \(\beta=1/2\). The true ferromagnetic transition has the 2D Ising exponents, and the stripe transition is weakly first order or Ashkin–Teller-like (Section 10).

Mean field thus identifies the two ordered phases and the special role of \(g=1/2\) — the degenerate maximum of \(\mathcal J(\boldsymbol k)\) — but turns it into the wrong prediction: fluctuations are the essence of the problem.

7. Monte Carlo and the static structure factor

The exact methods so far either work at \(T=0\) (Section 3) or neglect fluctuations (Section 6). To see the phases at finite temperature on a large lattice we sample the Boltzmann distribution with Monte Carlo. The snapshots also give the static structure factor, the quantity measured by neutron or X-ray scattering, which identifies the ordering pattern without knowing it in advance.

7.1 Four-sublattice Metropolis sampler

We use the Metropolis algorithm of Lab 010: propose flipping spin \(i\) and accept it with probability \(\min(1,e^{-\beta\Delta E_i})\), where \[ \Delta E_i = 2s_ih_i,\qquad h_i = J_1\sum_{j\in\rm nn(i)}s_j - J_2\sum_{j\in\rm diag(i)}s_j . \] This choice satisfies detailed balance, \(p(\mathbf s)P(\mathbf s\to\mathbf s')=p(\mathbf s')P(\mathbf s'\to\mathbf s)\), so the Boltzmann distribution is stationary.

Spins that are not coupled to each other can be updated simultaneously. The chessboard decomposition used for the Ising model fails here, because diagonal neighbours have the same chessboard colour. The four sublattices \((x\bmod2,\,y\bmod2)\) work instead: nearest neighbours differ in one parity, diagonal neighbours in both, so no two spins of one sublattice interact (for \(L\) even and \(L\ge4\)). One sweep updates the four sublattices in turn, which amounts to one attempted flip per spin.

This suggests storing a configuration directly as its four sublattices, a tuple S of four arrays S[2a+b] = s[a::2, b::2], each of shape \((C, L/2, L/2)\) for \(C\) chains. The neighbours of a spin of sublattice \((a,b)\) lie on the other three sublattices: its two horizontal neighbours on \((1-a,b)\), its two vertical neighbours on \((a,1-b)\), its four diagonal neighbours on \((1-a,1-b)\). With nsum(X, a, axis) \(=X+\) roll\((X,\pm1)\) (sign \(+\) for \(a=0\), \(-\) for \(a=1\)) the local field is h = nsum(S[1-a,b], a, x) + nsum(S[a,1-b], b, y) - g * nsum(nsum(S[1-a,1-b], a, x), b, y). No work is wasted on spins that are not being updated, and all chains are processed by the same array operations.

In JAX the sampler is a pure function sweep(S, key, g, beta); lax.scan repeats sweeps inside one compiled program, and jax.jit compiles the whole simulation. The chains form the leading axis of every array (equivalent to jax.vmap over chains, written out explicitly); each chain has its own \(g\) and its own temperature schedule: T_sched has shape \((n_{\rm steps}, C)\), and at step \(k\) chain \(c\) runs n_equil sweeps of equilibration at temperature T_sched[k, c] followed by n_meas measurements separated by n_between sweeps. A decreasing schedule anneals the chains; a schedule with one row runs every chain at its own fixed temperature. Each measurement is the vector \((m,m_x,m_y,e)\), and with keep=True the configurations are stored as well. The input S0 consists of four arrays of shape \((C,L/2,L/2)\), g has shape \((C,)\) and T_sched shape \((n_{\rm steps},C)\); the output measurements have shape \((n_{\rm steps}, n_{\rm meas}, C, 4)\) and, with keep=True, the configurations shape \((n_{\rm steps}, n_{\rm meas}, C, L, L)\), stored as int8. The cell ends by checking the sublattice layout against a plain NumPy implementation.

def nsum(X, a, axis):
    # X plus its copy shifted by one site along axis; for a = 0 the partner sites are i and i-1, for a = 1 they are i and i+1
    return X + jnp.roll(X, 1 if a == 0 else -1, axis)

def sublattice_field(S, a, b, g):
    # local field h on sublattice (a, b): nearest neighbours minus g times diagonal neighbours; shape (C, L/2, L/2)
    return (nsum(S[2 * (1 - a) + b], a, 1) + nsum(S[2 * a + 1 - b], b, 2)
            - g * nsum(nsum(S[2 * (1 - a) + 1 - b], a, 1), b, 2))

def sweep(S, key, g, beta):
    # one Metropolis sweep of all chains; g, beta: (C, 1, 1)
    S = list(S)
    for a, b in ((0, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (1, 1)):
        key, sub = jax.random.split(key)
        si = S[2 * a + b]
        dE = 2.0 * si * sublattice_field(S, a, b, g)
        accept = jax.random.uniform(sub, si.shape) < jnp.exp(-beta * dE)
        S[2 * a + b] = jnp.where(accept, -si, si)
    return tuple(S), key

def measure(S, g):
    # (m, m_x, m_y, e) per chain, shape (C, 4); g: (C,); the sign of (-1)^x on sublattice (a, b) is (-1)^a
    s00, s01, s10, s11 = S
    g = jnp.reshape(g, (-1, 1, 1))
    mean = lambda X: X.mean((1, 2)) / 4
    m, mx, my = mean(s00 + s01 + s10 + s11), mean(s00 + s01 - s10 - s11), mean(s00 - s01 + s10 - s11)
    e = -0.5 * sum(mean(S[2 * a + b] * sublattice_field(S, a, b, g)) for a in (0, 1) for b in (0, 1))   # each bond twice
    return jnp.stack([m, mx, my, e], 1)

def assemble(S):
    # four sublattices (C, L/2, L/2) -> full configurations (C, L, L), s[c, 2i+a, 2j+b] = S[2a+b][c, i, j]
    X = jnp.stack(S)                                         # (4, C, h, h)
    C, h = X.shape[1], X.shape[2]
    return X.reshape(2, 2, C, h, h).transpose(2, 3, 0, 4, 1).reshape(C, 2 * h, 2 * h)

def split(s):
    # full configurations (C, L, L) -> tuple of four sublattices
    return tuple(jnp.asarray(s[:, a::2, b::2], jnp.float32) for a in (0, 1) for b in (0, 1))

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnames=("n_equil", "n_meas", "n_between", "keep"))
def simulate(S0, key, g, T_sched, n_equil, n_meas, n_between, keep=False):
    g = g[:, None, None]
    def at_step(carry, T):
        beta = (1.0 / T)[:, None, None]
        step = lambda c, _: (sweep(c[0], c[1], g, beta), None)
        carry, _ = lax.scan(step, carry, None, length=n_equil)
        def record(c, _):
            c, _ = lax.scan(step, c, None, length=n_between)
            obs = measure(c[0], g[:, 0, 0])
            return c, ((obs, assemble(c[0]).astype(jnp.int8)) if keep else (obs,))
        return lax.scan(record, carry, None, length=n_meas)
    return lax.scan(at_step, (S0, key), T_sched)[1]

def run_mc(s0, g, T_sched, n_equil, n_meas, n_between, seed, keep=False):
    # s0: (C, L, L) initial configurations; g: (C,); T_sched: (n_steps, C). Returns numpy arrays (see shapes above).
    out = simulate(split(np.asarray(s0)), jax.random.PRNGKey(seed), jnp.asarray(g, jnp.float32),
                   jnp.asarray(T_sched, jnp.float32), n_equil, n_meas, n_between, keep)
    return tuple(np.asarray(o) for o in out) if keep else np.asarray(out[0])

7.2 Static structure factor

The agreement is good at all five points, so we can trust the sampler on larger lattices. For a configuration \(\mathbf s\) define the Fourier amplitude \(\hat s(\boldsymbol k)=\sum_{\boldsymbol r}s_{\boldsymbol r}e^{-i\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol r}\) on the \(L^2\) wave vectors \(\boldsymbol k=\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x,n_y)\). The static structure factor is \[ \begin{aligned} S(\boldsymbol k) &= \frac1N\big\langle|\hat s(\boldsymbol k)|^2\big\rangle = N\big\langle|m_{\boldsymbol k}|^2\big\rangle\\ &= \sum_{\boldsymbol r}e^{-i\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol r}\,\frac1N\sum_{\boldsymbol r'}\langle s_{\boldsymbol r'}s_{\boldsymbol r'+\boldsymbol r}\rangle , \end{aligned} \] the Fourier transform of the spin–spin correlation function and the quantity a scattering experiment measures.

In a phase with long-range order at wave vector \(\boldsymbol Q\), \(\langle|m_{\boldsymbol Q}|^2\rangle\) stays finite as \(N\to\infty\), so \(S(\boldsymbol Q)\propto N\): a Bragg peak. In the paramagnet correlations decay over a finite length and \(S(\boldsymbol k)\) is a broad, \(N\)-independent function. Parseval’s theorem adds a sum rule: \(\sum_{\boldsymbol k}|\hat s(\boldsymbol k)|^2=N\sum_{\boldsymbol r}s_{\boldsymbol r}^2=N^2\), so the average of \(S(\boldsymbol k)\) over the Brillouin zone is exactly \(1\), and ordering only redistributes the weight.

We compute \(S(\boldsymbol k)\) with np.fft.fft2 from Monte Carlo snapshots (\(L=32\), 16 annealed chains \(\times\) 20 snapshots per point, all four points in one batch) in four situations: the ferromagnet, the paramagnet, the frustrated point \(g=1/2\) at low \(T\), and the stripe phase.

8. Transfer matrices of infinite strips

Monte Carlo has statistical errors and needs equilibration. For a strip of finite width \(L\) and infinite length the free energy can be computed exactly from the largest eigenvalue of the row transfer matrix — the method of Lab 012, here with diagonal bonds. The results are exact for each \(L\), and the limit \(L\to\infty\) is approached through a sequence of widths.

With the row-pair energy \(W(\sigma,\sigma')\) of Section 3.4 the transfer matrix is \[ \begin{aligned} \mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'} &= e^{-\beta W(\sigma,\sigma')}\\ &= \exp\Big\{\beta\Big[J_1\sum_xs_xs'_x - J_2\sum_xs_x\big(s'_{x+1}+s'_{x-1}\big) + \frac{J_1}{2}\sum_x\big(s_xs_{x+1}+s'_xs'_{x+1}\big)\Big]\Big\}, \end{aligned} \] a \(2^L\times2^L\) matrix. It is symmetric (\(\sum_xs_x(s'_{x+1}+s'_{x-1})=\sum_xs'_x(s_{x-1}+s_{x+1})\)) and has positive entries. For a strip of length \(M\to\infty\), \(Z=\operatorname{Tr}\mathsf V^M\simeq\lambda_0^M\), so \[ f_L(T) = -\frac{T}{L}\ln\lambda_0,\qquad \xi_L = \frac{1}{\ln(\lambda_0/|\lambda_1|)}, \] where \(\lambda_0\) is the largest eigenvalue (positive and non-degenerate by the Perron–Frobenius theorem) and \(\lambda_1\) the eigenvalue of largest modulus among the eigenvectors odd under the global spin flip — the ones that carry the spin–spin correlation along the strip, \(\langle s_0s_r\rangle\sim(|\lambda_1|/\lambda_0)^r\). The modulus matters here: horizontal stripes (rows alternately \(+\) and \(-\)) appear as a negative eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\approx-\lambda_0\).

The spin-flip symmetry halves the work. The flip \(\sigma\to-\sigma\) maps the index \(n\to2^L-1-n\) and commutes with \(\mathsf V\). With \(\mathsf V^{\pm}_{ab}=\mathsf V_{ab}\pm\mathsf V_{a,\bar b}\) for \(a,b<2^{L-1}\) (Lab 012), the even and odd sectors are two symmetric matrices of half the size.

As a first test, \(Z\) of the \(4\times4\) torus is \(\operatorname{Tr}\mathsf V^4\) and must equal the enumeration of Section 3.3 at any \((g,T)\).

    # largest algebraic ("LA") or largest-modulus ("LM") eigenvalue of a symmetric matrix
    n = A.shape[0]
    if n <= 512:
        w = eigh(A, eigvals_only=True)
        return w[-1] if which == "LA" else w[np.argmax(np.abs(w))]

def strip_eigs(L, J1, J2, T, odd=True):
    # ln(lambda_0) and ln|lambda_1| of the strip of width L (lambda_1 only if odd=True)
    lv = log_V(L, J1, J2, T)
    shift = lv.max()
    V = np.exp(lv - shift)                                 # rescaled to avoid overflow; ln lambda = ln(eig) + shift
    n = V.shape[0] // 2
    Vf = V[:n, ::-1][:, :n]                                # V[a, flip(b)] for a, b < 2^(L-1)
    l0 = np.log(top_eig(V[:n, :n] + Vf, "LA")) + shift
    l1 = np.log(abs(top_eig(V[:n, :n] - Vf, "LM"))) + shift if odd else np.nan
    return l0, l1

8.2 Transition temperature from the strips

For each \(g\) the position \(T_{\max}(g)\) of the maximum of \(c_L\) is a finite-size indicator of the transition (Lab 012: for the Ising model it approaches \(T_c\) slowly). A sharper indicator is the correlation length. Above \(T_c\), \(\xi_L\) saturates as \(L\) grows, so \(\xi_L/L\) decreases with \(L\); below \(T_c\) the ordered states become degenerate, \(|\lambda_1|\to\lambda_0\), and \(\xi_L/L\) increases with \(L\). The temperature \(T^*(L,L+2)\) where \(\xi_L/L=\xi_{L+2}/(L+2)\) therefore estimates \(T_c\), and it converges fast for the Ising model (Lab 012). We solve for the crossing with brentq in a bracket found on a coarse grid, for widths up to \(L=12\) (\(2^{12}\times2^{12}\) matrices); where no crossing exists in the bracket the table prints none.

9. Binder cumulants

The strips of Section 8 are exact but narrow (\(L\le12\)). Monte Carlo reaches larger lattices at the price of statistical errors. A finite lattice has no sharp transition (Lab 012, Section 1), so we need a quantity whose size dependence changes sign at \(T_c\); the Binder cumulant is such a quantity [Binder 1981]. For a scalar order parameter, here the ferromagnetic \(m\), it is defined as \[ U_m = 1-\frac{\langle m^4\rangle}{3\langle m^2\rangle^2}. \] In the disordered phase, \(L\gg\xi\), \(m\) is a sum of many weakly correlated contributions, hence Gaussian by the central limit theorem; then \(\langle m^4\rangle=3\langle m^2\rangle^2\) and \(U_m\to0\). In the ordered phase \(m\approx\pm m_0\), \(\langle m^4\rangle=\langle m^2\rangle^2\) and \(U_m\to2/3\). At \(T_c\) the distribution of \(m/\sqrt{\langle m^2\rangle}\) has an \(L\)-independent shape (scale invariance), so \(U_m\) takes the same value for all large \(L\): the curves \(U_m(T)\) for different \(L\) cross at \(T_c\).

The stripe order parameter has two components \((m_x,m_y)\), and the normalisation must be adapted. For a two-dimensional Gaussian vector \(\langle r^4\rangle=2\langle r^2\rangle^2\), and for a vector of fixed length \(\langle r^4\rangle=\langle r^2\rangle^2\), so \[ U_{\rm str} = 2-\frac{\langle m_{\rm str}^4\rangle}{\langle m_{\rm str}^2\rangle^2} \] goes from \(0\) (disordered) to \(1\) (ordered). SymPy verifies the Gaussian moments.

def binder(meas, stripe):
    # meas: (chains, nT, n_meas, 4) -> U(T) and its jackknife error over blocks of chains; shapes (nT,), (nT,)
    q = np.hypot(meas[..., 1], meas[..., 2]) if stripe else meas[..., 0]
    q2, q4 = (q**2).mean(2), (q**4).mean(2)                         # per chain and temperature: (chains, nT)
    U = (lambda a2, a4: 2 - a4 / a2**2) if stripe else (lambda a2, a4: 1 - a4 / (3 * a2**2))
    full = U(q2.mean(0), q4.mean(0))
    blocks = np.array_split(np.arange(q2.shape[0]), 8)
    jk = np.array([U(np.delete(q2, b, 0).mean(0), np.delete(q4, b, 0).mean(0)) for b in blocks])
    err = np.sqrt((len(blocks) - 1) * ((jk - jk.mean(0))**2).mean(0))
    return full, err

def crossing_T(T, Ua, Ub, T_ref):
    # temperature where the interpolated curves Ua(T), Ub(T) cross, the crossing closest to T_ref; None if none
    d = Ua - Ub
    idx = np.where(d[:-1] * d[1:] < 0)[0]
    if len(idx) == 0:
        return None
    Tx = [T[i] - d[i] * (T[i + 1] - T[i]) / (d[i + 1] - d[i]) for i in idx]
    return min(Tx, key=lambda t: abs(t - T_ref))

10. First-order and continuous transitions

Our calculations locate the transition line but cannot decide its nature close to \(g=1/2\): that needs lattices far larger than ours, histogram analysis and dedicated methods. The model has a long history of conflicting answers, which makes it a good benchmark — and a warning that small-lattice data can mislead. In what follows, each statement is attributed to the paper that makes it. Some papers take the nearest-neighbour coupling antiferromagnetic, others write the ferromagnetic one as \(J_1<0\); by the checkerboard gauge of Section 2 all these results apply to our model with \(g=|J_2/J_1|\) (an antiferromagnetic \(J_1\) turns our ferromagnet into a Néel state).

On the ferromagnetic side, \(g<1/2\), the order parameter is a scalar with \(\mathbb Z_2\) symmetry. Kalz, Honecker, Fuchs and Pruschke [2008] find a transition in the two-dimensional Ising universality class (for example \(\nu=0.99(1)\) at \(g=0.3\), against the Ising value \(\nu=1\)), and a transition temperature consistent with \(T_c\to0\) at \(g=1/2\).

On the stripe side, \(g>1/2\), the literature records a long debate. Early Monte Carlo work [Landau & Binder 1985, among others] was interpreted as a continuous transition with non-universal (continuously varying) exponents (as summarised by Kalz et al. [2008]). Kalz et al. [2008] then observed a double-peaked energy histogram at \(g=0.6\), the signature of a first-order transition (coexistence of the ordered and disordered phases), and concluded that the transition is weakly first order for moderate \(g>1/2\). Kalz, Honecker and Moliner [2011] found doubly peaked histograms for \(1/2<g\lesssim1\) and, by perturbation theory around the two decoupled critical Ising models at \(J_1=0\) (our Section 5.2), derived the Ashkin–Teller model as the effective theory.

Jin, Sen and Sandvik [2012] reached a different picture. They showed that for \(g\ge g^*\) the transition is continuous with Ashkin–Teller exponents that vary continuously from those of the 4-state Potts model at \(g=g^*\) to those of the Ising model for \(g\to\infty\), with \(g^*=0.67(1)\); the transition is first order for \(1/2<g<g^*\), and pseudo-first-order for \(g^*<g<1\): on finite lattices a continuous transition near the Potts point shows first-order-like features such as double-peaked histograms. Jin, Sen, Guo and Sandvik [2013] extended this with cluster mean-field theory, Monte Carlo and transfer matrices: a line of very weak first-order transitions for \(1/2<g<g^*\), and continuous transitions above \(g^*\) that map onto the critical line of the Ashkin–Teller model from its 4-state Potts point to the decoupled-Ising limit. Finally, Li and Yang [2021] used tensor networks and found that if a first-order region exists, it is smaller than in the earlier studies; their analysis suggests that the model evolves continuously from two decoupled Ising models (central charge \(c=1\)) to a point in the tricritical Ising class (\(c=0.7\)) at \(g^*\simeq0.54\).

These results connect directly with this notebook. The Ashkin–Teller structure is the \(D_4\) symmetry of the stripe order parameter (Section 2.1) and the two sublattice magnetisations of the \(g\to\infty\) limit (Section 5.2). The difficulty of the problem near \(g=1/2\) — tiny energy scales (Section 4), a degenerate manifold (Section 3.5), first-order or pseudo-first-order behaviour — is what makes the model a demanding test for the unsupervised methods of Labs 019–020.

The results of this notebook and the literature above combine into the phase diagram drawn below. A phase diagram is a map of the control parameters, here the coupling ratio \(g=J_2/J_1\) and the temperature \(T/J_1\), divided into regions with qualitatively different behaviour, the phases, and the lines between them, the phase transitions. The type of a transition is read off from the free energy per spin \(f\) and its derivatives. At a first-order transition a first derivative of \(f\), such as the energy or the order parameter, jumps, and the two phases coexist at the transition temperature; on a finite lattice this shows up as an energy histogram with two peaks, the observation of Kalz et al. [2008]. At a continuous transition the first derivatives are continuous, the order parameter goes to zero continuously, and the correlation length diverges as a power of \(|T-T_c|\) with critical exponents shared by a whole universality class.

The orange dots are the transfer-matrix crossings of Section 8.2 and the blue squares the Monte Carlo Binder crossings of Section 9; the solid lines through them are only a guide to the eye. Three statements are exact and placed exactly: \(T_c=2/\ln(1+\sqrt2)=2.269\,J_1\) at \(g=0\) (Onsager), the asymptote \(T_c\to2.269\,g\,J_1\) for \(g\to\infty\) (two decoupled Ising lattices, Section 5.2), and \(T_c\to0\) at \(g=1/2\), where domain walls cost nothing (Section 4) and where Kalz et al. [2008] find their data consistent with a vanishing transition temperature; the dashed segments connect the computed points to this limit. Below the line on the left the ferromagnet has \(m\neq0\); below the line on the right the stripe phase has \(m_{\rm str}\neq0\); above both lies the paramagnet. On the ferromagnetic side the transition is continuous and in the two-dimensional Ising universality class [Kalz et al. 2008]. On the stripe side, for \(g\) well above \(1/2\), it is continuous with exponents that vary along the line, those of the Ashkin–Teller model introduced in Section 2.1 [Jin, Sen & Sandvik 2012]. Close to \(g=1/2\) (hatched band, \(1/2<g\lesssim0.7\)) its nature is debated: first order according to Kalz et al. [2008, 2011] and Jin et al. [2012, 2013], who place the end of the first-order region at \(g^*=0.67(1)\), while Li and Yang [2021] find at most a much smaller first-order region.
