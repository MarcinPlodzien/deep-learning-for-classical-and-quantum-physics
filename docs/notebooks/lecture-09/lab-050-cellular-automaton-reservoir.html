Lab 050 — Cellular automaton reservoir

A reservoir computer drives a fixed dynamical system with the measured signal and fits only a linear readout of its state by ridge regression [H. Jaeger, GMD Report 148 (2001), pdf; M. Lukoševičius, H. Jaeger, Comput. Sci. Rev. 3, 127 (2009), doi:10.1016/j.cosrev.2009.03.005]. In an echo state network (ESN) this system is a random network of tanh units. Any driven nonlinear system that forgets its initial state and keeps a readable record of the recent input can take its place. A cellular automaton (CA) is such a system with discrete space, discrete time and a finite number of cell states.

We first define cellular automata, their rule tables and their classes of behaviour, and we look at linear rules and at the spreading of a local perturbation, the damage. Two short parts on the Game of Life and on a traffic model show that cellular automata are also physical models. We then build the CA reservoir of Yilmaz, explain its encoding, evolution and readout on one input step, work it out with numbers on a small example together with the training of the readout, show how the number of generations per input step is chosen and where the nonlinearity comes from, and forecast the five benchmark signals with the protocol and the scores used for the ESN. A scan over the rule space tests the hypothesis that the best reservoirs sit at the edge of chaos, with Langton’s parameter and with the spreading rate of the damage. A map over all 88 distinct elementary rules and the number of generation steps per input step shows which rules forecast well, the capacity curves of nine single rules on all five records show how far each kind of rule forecasts, and a shift register of quantised past values without automaton shows how much of the forecast comes from the transport of the input along the lattice. The notebook ends with the cost of the CA fit. Two exploration cells let the reader run elementary automata and a driven automaton with their own parameters.

The implementation uses JAX. Every automaton runs as one jax.lax.scan over time, and the ridge regressions are done in double precision.

1. Signals, protocol and reference models

The signals, the forecasting protocol, the scores and the reference models are the ones used for the ESN, and the code of this section repeats them so that the notebook runs on its own. It can be skimmed. The first coordinate \(x\) of the Lorenz-63 system, \(\dot x=10\,(y-x)\), \(\dot y=x\,(28-z)-y\), \(\dot z=xy-\tfrac83 z\), is integrated by the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (RK4) and sampled every \(\Delta t=0.02\). The Mackey–Glass delay equation \[\dot s(t)=\frac{0.2\, s(t-\tau_{\rm MG})}{1+s(t-\tau_{\rm MG})^{10}}-0.1\, s(t)\] is sampled once per time unit for the delays \(\tau_{\rm MG}=15\), 25 and 35, and the Santa Fe series (data set A) is the measured intensity of a far-infrared NH\(_3\) laser. Every signal has 4000 samples and is rescaled to \([0,1]\) with the minimum and maximum of its first 3000 samples.

A forecast at the origin \(k\) returns all horizons \(\tau=1,\dots,50\) at once, with the target vector \(\mathbf y_k=(s_{k+1},\dots,s_{k+50})\). Fitting uses the origins \(k=63,\dots,2449\), validation the origins \(k=2499,\dots,2949\) and testing the origins \(k=2999,\dots,3949\). For each horizon the capacity \(C(\tau)=r(\tau)^2\) is the squared Pearson coefficient between forecast and truth over the test origins, the total capacity \(C_{\rm tot}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{50}C(\tau)\le50\) summarises a curve, and the skill horizon \(\tau_{1/2}\) is the first horizon with \(C(\tau)<0.5\). Every setting is chosen by the validation \(C_{\rm tot}\). Persistence repeats the last value. The autoregressive (AR) model and the nonlinear vector autoregressive (NVAR) model with all quadratic products of the delay vector are ridge regressions on the delay vector \((s_k,\dots,s_{k-p+1})\) [D. J. Gauthier, E. Bollt, A. Griffith, W. A. S. Barbosa, Nat. Commun. 12, 5564 (2021), doi:10.1038/s41467-021-25801-2]. The ESN has \(N=300\) tanh units, drawn with seed 0, at the spectral radius, input scaling, leak rate and ridge strength selected on validation data for each signal.

# Lorenz-63: x' = 10 (y - x), y' = x (28 - z) - y, z' = x y - 8/3 z; RK4 with h=0.01, sampled every 2 steps (dt=0.02);
# initial state (1,1,1), first 50 time units discarded; we keep the x component.
def lorenz_rhs(u):
    x, y, z = u
    return jnp.array([10.0 * (y - x), x * (28.0 - z) - y, x * y - 8.0 / 3.0 * z])

def rk4_step(f, u, h):
    k1 = f(u); k2 = f(u + 0.5*h*k1); k3 = f(u + 0.5*h*k2); k4 = f(u + h*k3)
    return u + h/6.0*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnums=(0, 1, 2))
def lorenz_trajectory(n_samples, sub=2, n_discard=2500, h=0.01):
    def one_sample(u, _):
        u = jax.lax.fori_loop(0, sub, lambda i, v: rk4_step(lorenz_rhs, v, h), u)
        return u, u
    u0 = jnp.array([1.0, 1.0, 1.0])
    u, _ = jax.lax.scan(one_sample, u0, None, length=n_discard)
    _, traj = jax.lax.scan(one_sample, u, None, length=n_samples)
    return traj

# Mackey-Glass: ds/dt = 0.2 s(t-tau)/(1+s(t-tau)^10) - 0.1 s(t); RK4 with h=0.1, delayed value at half steps by
# linear interpolation, constant history 1.2 on [-tau, 0], first 3000 fine steps discarded, every 10th point kept.
def mackey_glass(tau, n_samples, h=0.1, x0=1.2, transient=3000, every=10):
    lag = int(round(tau / h))
    f = lambda x, xd: 0.2 * xd / (1.0 + xd**10) - 0.1 * x
    def step(buf, _):
        x, xd0, xd1 = buf[-1], buf[0], buf[1]
        xdh = 0.5 * (xd0 + xd1)
        k1 = f(x, xd0); k2 = f(x + 0.5*h*k1, xdh); k3 = f(x + 0.5*h*k2, xdh); k4 = f(x + h*k3, xd1)
        xn = x + h/6.0*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)
        return jnp.concatenate([buf[1:], xn[None]]), xn
    _, xs = jax.lax.scan(step, jnp.full(lag + 1, x0), None, length=transient + n_samples * every)
    x = np.concatenate([np.full(lag + 1, x0), np.asarray(xs)])
    return x[transient::every][:n_samples]

def r2_columns(pred, true):
    pc, tc = pred - pred.mean(0), true - true.mean(0)
    return (pc * tc).sum(0) ** 2 / ((pc ** 2).sum(0) * (tc ** 2).sum(0))

def score(pred, true):
    # C(tau) = r(tau)^2 over the rows, C_tot and the skill horizon tau_1/2
    C = r2_columns(np.asarray(pred, float), np.asarray(true, float))
    below = np.nonzero(C < 0.5)[0]
    return dict(C=C, Ctot=float(C.sum()), tau_half=int(below[0] + 1) if len(below) else None)

def ridge_fit(F, Y, beta):
    # centred ridge regression, F: (n, d) features, Y: (n, m) targets -> W (d, m), b (m,)
    fm, ym = F.mean(0), Y.mean(0)
    Fc, Yc = F - fm, Y - ym
    W = np.linalg.solve(Fc.T @ Fc + beta * np.eye(F.shape[1]), Fc.T @ Yc)
    return W, ym - fm @ W

def delay_features(s, ks, p):
    return s[ks[:, None] - np.arange(p)[None, :]]
def nvar_features(s, ks, p):
    d = delay_features(s, ks, p)
    iu, ju = np.triu_indices(p)
    return np.concatenate([d, d[:, iu] * d[:, ju]], axis=1)

    # median wall time of n calls and the last result

    for _ in range(n):
        t0 = time.perf_counter(); out = fn(); ts.append(time.perf_counter() - t0)

def ridge_model(s, kind):
    Yf, Yv, Yt = targets(s, K_FIT), targets(s, K_VAL), targets(s, K_TEST)
    best = (-1.0, None, None)
    for p in GRID_P[kind]:
        Ff, Fv = FEATS[kind](s, K_FIT, p), FEATS[kind](s, K_VAL, p)
        for beta in GRID_BETA:
            W, b = ridge_fit(Ff, Yf, beta)
            ctot = score(Fv @ W + b, Yv)["Ctot"]

                best = (ctot, p, beta)

    Ff = FEATS[kind](s, K_FIT, p)
    t_fit, (W, b) = timed(lambda: ridge_fit(Ff, Yf, beta))
    return dict(p=p, beta=beta, n_feat=Ff.shape[1], t_fit=t_fit, **score(FEATS[kind](s, K_TEST, p) @ W + b, Yt))

# ESN with N = 300 tanh units (seed 0); settings from the numbers file of the ESN notebook if present, else a new search
def esn_base(N, seed, density=0.1):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    W = rng.normal(size=(N, N)) * (rng.random((N, N)) < density)
    W /= np.max(np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(W)))
    return W, rng.uniform(-1.0, 1.0, size=N)

@jax.jit
def esn_states(W, w_in, u, alpha, x0):
    # r_k = (1 - alpha) r_{k-1} + alpha tanh(W r_{k-1} + w_in s_k), returns all states (T, N)
    def step(x, uk):
        x = (1.0 - alpha) * x + alpha * jnp.tanh(W @ x + w_in * uk)
        return x, x
    _, X = jax.lax.scan(step, x0, u)
    return X

2. Cellular automata

A cellular automaton consists of a lattice, a set of cell states, a neighbourhood and a local rule.

  1. A lattice of cells \(j=0,1,\dots,L-1\), here a ring of \(L\) cells with periodic boundary conditions, so that cell \(L-1\) is the left neighbour of cell 0.
  2. A finite set of cell states, here the integers modulo \(q\), \(\mathbb Z_q=\{0,1,\dots,q-1\}\), with \(q=2\) for binary cells.
  3. A neighbourhood of radius \(a\), the \(n=2a+1\) cells \(j-a,\dots,j+a\).
  4. A local rule \(f:\mathbb Z_q^n\to\mathbb Z_q\) that returns the new state of a cell from the states of its neighbourhood.

The state of cell \(j\) at iteration \(i\) is \(c_j^{(i)}\). The global map \(F\) applies \(f\) to all cells at once, \[c_j^{(i)}=f\big(c_{j-a}^{(i-1)},\dots,c_{j+a}^{(i-1)}\big)\quad\text{for all }j,\qquad \mathbf c^{(i)}=F\big(\mathbf c^{(i-1)}\big),\] and the evolution is the iteration of \(F\) from an initial configuration \(\mathbf c^{(0)}\). Space, time and states are discrete, and the update is deterministic and local.

Elementary cellular automata

The elementary cellular automata have \(q=2\) and \(a=1\) [S. Wolfram, Rev. Mod. Phys. 55, 601 (1983), doi:10.1103/RevModPhys.55.601]. A neighbourhood \((c_{j-1},c_j,c_{j+1})\) is one of the \(2^3=8\) patterns \(111,110,\dots,000\), and a rule assigns an output bit to each. Writing the 8 output bits in this order gives a binary number between 0 and 255, the Wolfram rule number, so there are 256 elementary rules. The figure shows the tables of rules 90 and 30. Rule 90 returns \(c_{j-1}\oplus c_{j+1}\), the exclusive or (XOR) of the two neighbours, and its output bits 01011010 form the number 90. Rule 30 returns \(c_{j-1}\oplus(c_j\lor c_{j+1})\).

def eca_table(rule):
    # output bit for the 8 neighbourhoods (l, c, r) with index 4l + 2c + r: bit number index of the rule number
    return jnp.asarray((rule >> np.arange(8)) & 1, jnp.int32)

def linear_table(w, q):
    # table of the linear rule c_j <- (w_{-1} c_{j-1} + w_0 c_j + w_1 c_{j+1}) mod q, index q^2 l + q c + r
    l, c, r = np.meshgrid(np.arange(q), np.arange(q), np.arange(q), indexing="ij")
    return jnp.asarray(((w[0] * l + w[1] * c + w[2] * r) % q).ravel(), jnp.int32)

def ca_step(c, table, q=2):
    # one synchronous update of all cells, periodic boundary conditions (jnp.roll)
    return table[jnp.roll(c, 1) * q * q + c * q + jnp.roll(c, -1)]

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnums=(2, 3))
def ca_run(c0, table, q, n_steps):
    # space-time diagram: rows c^(0), c^(1), ..., c^(n_steps)
    def one(c, _):
        c = ca_step(c, table, q)
        return c, c
    return jnp.concatenate([c0[None], jax.lax.scan(one, c0, None, length=n_steps)[1]])

Game of Life

This part and the traffic model of rule 184 below are optional reading. They show cellular automata as physical models and are not needed for the reservoir. The best-known two-dimensional automaton is Conway’s Game of Life [M. Gardner, Sci. Am. 223 (4), 120 (1970), doi:10.1038/scientificamerican1070-120]. Each cell of a square lattice is dead (0) or alive (1), and its neighbourhood is the Moore neighbourhood of the 8 surrounding cells. A dead cell with exactly three live neighbours becomes alive, a live cell with two or three live neighbours survives, and every other cell is dead in the next step. The neighbour count is a two-dimensional convolution with a \(3\times3\) kernel of ones and a zero in the centre, which is how the JAX implementation computes it on a periodic grid. The snapshots show a glider, five live cells that return to their shape shifted by one cell diagonally after 4 steps, and a random soup of 0.352 live cells per site that leaves 0.042 after 200 steps, mostly in small still and oscillating objects.

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnums=1)
def life_run(g0, n_steps):
    # Game of Life on a periodic grid: neighbour count by a 3x3 convolution, birth on 3, survival on 2 or 3
    def one(g, _):
        gp = jnp.pad(g, 1, mode="wrap")
        n = jax.scipy.signal.convolve2d(gp, LIFE_K, mode="valid")
        g = ((n == 3) | ((g == 1) & (n == 2))).astype(jnp.int32)
        return g, g
    return jnp.concatenate([g0[None], jax.lax.scan(one, g0, None, length=n_steps)[1]])

Linear cellular automata over \(\mathbb Z_q\)

A rule is linear when the new state is a weighted sum of the neighbourhood modulo \(q\), \[c_j^{(i)}=\sum_{l=-a}^{a}w_l\,c_{j+l}^{(i-1)}\bmod q,\qquad w_l\in\mathbb Z_q .\] Rule 90 is the linear rule with \(q=2\) and weights \((w_{-1},w_0,w_1)=(1,0,1)\), and rule 150 has \((1,1,1)\). For a linear rule the global map is a circulant matrix acting on \(\mathbb Z_q^L\), and the superposition principle holds, \(F(\mathbf c+\mathbf c')=F(\mathbf c)+F(\mathbf c')\) modulo \(q\). Many properties of linear rules can be computed exactly from their weights, which is why they are used for reservoirs [J. Kantic, F. C. Legl, W. Stechele, J. Hermann, Complex Intell. Syst. 10, 3593 (2024), doi:10.1007/s40747-023-01330-x, arXiv:2308.11522]. The figure shows rule 90 and two linear rules over \(\mathbb Z_4\), from a single cell in state 1 and from a random configuration, with darker blue for larger states. The rule with weights \((0,2,1)\), \(c_j\leftarrow2c_j+c_{j+1}\bmod4\), moves the content of every cell one site to the left per iteration and mixes it with its neighbour, a shift register with interaction. The rule \((1,2,1)\) spreads a seed in both directions.

Traffic flow, rule 184

Rule 184 is the simplest model of traffic on a one-lane ring road, and like the Game of Life this part is optional reading. A cell in state 1 holds a car and a cell in state 0 is empty. The rule number \(184=10111000_2\) lists the new state for the neighbourhoods \(111,110,\dots,000\), and the next cell prints this table with the car reading of every row. A cell becomes 1 if a car arrives from the left (\(c_{j-1}=1\), \(c_j=0\)) or if its car is blocked and stays (\(c_j=c_{j+1}=1\)), and becomes 0 otherwise. Each car therefore moves one cell to the right when the cell ahead is empty, and the number of cars is conserved. The density \(\eta\) is the fraction of cells in state 1, and the flux \(\Phi\) is the number of cars that move per cell and step. Rule 184 is the deterministic limit with maximum speed 1 of the traffic model of Nagel and Schreckenberg [K. Nagel, M. Schreckenberg, J. Phys. I France 2, 2221 (1992), doi:10.1051/jp1:1992277]. For \(\eta<1/2\) every car eventually finds an empty cell ahead, all cars move and \(\Phi=\eta\), the free-flow phase. For \(\eta>1/2\) the empty cells are the minority, each of them lets one car through per step, and \(\Phi=1-\eta\), the jammed phase. The fundamental diagram \(\Phi(\eta)=\min(\eta,1-\eta)\) has a cusp at \(\eta=1/2\), the transition between the two phases.

We start from random configurations of 1000 cells at densities \(\eta=0.05,\dots,0.95\), discard 1000 steps and average the flux over the next 500. The measured flux agrees with \(\min(\eta,1-\eta)\) to four decimals on this grid. The space-time diagrams on 100 cells show the free flow at \(\eta=0.3\), where cars move to the right in parallel lines once the initial clusters have dissolved, and the jammed phase at \(\eta=0.7\), where queues of standing cars travel to the left.

Under rule 90 the difference of two configurations evolves by rule 90 itself, because the rule is linear over \(\mathbb Z_2\), so the damage is the Sierpinski triangle of the single seed for every background, and the damaged fraction oscillates with the self-similar pattern, 0.008 after 128 iterations. Over the first 100 iterations its right edge moves by 1.00 cell per iteration and its left edge by 0.23 cells per iteration, since the exclusive or with the left neighbour passes every difference on to the right, and the damaged fraction reaches 0.323 of the lattice after 128 iterations. Under rule 110 it reaches 0.158. The width of the damaged region grows linearly in time for rules 90, 30 and 110, the cellular automaton counterpart of a positive Lyapunov exponent, while rule 4 keeps a perturbation where it was.

For a reservoir this has a consequence. On the ring of 257 cells a flipped cell is still visible after 128 iterations in 100% of the 20 runs under rules 90, 30 and 110, which spread it, and in 50% of the runs under rule 4, which keeps every isolated cell in state 1, so the difference survives when the flip creates or removes such a cell. Without input nothing removes such a difference. This damage test has no input, so it is not a test of the echo state property. In a CA reservoir the initial state can be forgotten only through the input, which overwrites the mapped cells at every step, and we test this with two copies of the forecasting reservoir driven by the same input further below. A second effect matters for linear rules on rings. Rule 90 on a ring of \(2^n\) cells sends every configuration to zero, while on 257 cells it does not. From a random start, after 256 iterations rule 90 leaves 0 live cells on 256 cells and 120 on 257 cells, and rule 150 leaves 133 and 138. On a ring whose size is a power of two rule 90 erases all information, which matters for the size of the reservoir lattice.

Langton related the computational ability of cellular automata to the transition between ordered and chaotic rules, the edge of chaos [C. G. Langton, Physica D 42, 12 (1990), doi:10.1016/0167-2789(90)90064-V], and the first CA reservoirs were motivated by it. We treat this as a hypothesis and test it with the forecasting reservoirs further on.

3. Cellular automaton reservoir

Yilmaz replaced the echo state network by a cellular automaton [O. Yilmaz, arXiv:1410.0162 (2014), arXiv:1410.0162]. The input is written into cells of the automaton, the rule is applied a fixed number of times between two inputs, and all configurations of these applications are read by a linear readout. We follow Kantic et al., who split one input step into an encoding, an evolution and a readout [Kantic et al., Complex Intell. Syst. 10, 3593 (2024), doi:10.1007/s40747-023-01330-x]. They write a single value of \(\mathbb Z_q\) into one cell per compartment, so their number of levels equals the number \(q\) of cell states. Writing \(B\) digits per compartment is our extension, which allows more levels than cell states.

The JAX implementation runs the whole record as one jax.lax.scan. A step writes the code digits into the mapped cells, applies the rule \(N_{\rm gen}\) times through a lookup table indexed by the neighbourhood, and returns the concatenated configurations. The reservoir state has integer entries between 0 and \(q-1\), so the matrix \(X^\top X\) of the readout is computed exactly in single precision, and the solve is done in double precision.

def quantise(s, Q):
    # levels l = 0..Q-1 of equal width 1/Q on [0, 1]; values outside [0, 1] go to the end levels
    return np.clip(np.floor(s * Q), 0, Q - 1).astype(int)

def ca_digits(s, Q, q):
    # quantise s to Q levels and write the level in base q as B digits, Q = q^B, least significant digit first
    l = quantise(s, Q)
    B = int(round(np.log(Q) / np.log(q)))
    return np.stack([(l // q ** b) % q for b in range(B)], 1).astype(np.int32)

@partial(jax.jit, static_argnums=(3, 4, 5, 6, 7))
def ca_reservoir(table, digits, cells, q, L, N_generation_steps, additive, with_input):
    # digits: (T, B) input code per step; cells: (R, B) lattice cells that receive the B digits in each compartment.
    # Per step: write the digits (replacement, or addition mod q), run N_gen iterations, return the concatenated states.
    def step(c, d):
        vals = jnp.broadcast_to(d, cells.shape)
        c = c.at[cells].set((c[cells] + vals) % q if additive else vals)
        outs = [c] if with_input else []
        for _ in range(N_generation_steps):
            c = ca_step(c, table, q)
            outs.append(c)
        return c, jnp.concatenate(outs)
    return jax.lax.scan(step, jnp.zeros(L, jnp.int32), digits)[1]

def compartment_cells(R, Lc, B, seed):
    # one fixed random choice of B distinct cells in each of the R compartments of Lc cells
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    return jnp.asarray(np.stack([j * Lc + rng.choice(Lc, B, replace=False) for j in range(R)]), jnp.int32)

def ridge_int(X, Y, betas):
    # centred ridge regression for integer-valued features; X^T X in float32 is exact for these counts
    n = X.shape[0]; fm = X.mean(0, dtype=np.float64); ym = Y.mean(0)
    G = (X.T @ X).astype(np.float64) - n * np.outer(fm, fm)
    XtY = X.T.astype(np.float64) @ Y - n * np.outer(fm, ym)
    out = []
    for beta in betas:
        W = np.linalg.solve(G + beta * np.eye(G.shape[0]), XtY)
        out.append((beta, W, ym - fm @ W))
    return out

Encoding

The interval \([0,1]\) is cut into \(Q\) bins of width \(1/Q\), the quantisation levels, and the sample \(s_k\in[0,1]\) becomes the number of its bin, the level \(\ell_k=\lfloor Qs_k\rfloor\), clipped to \(0,\dots,Q-1\). The level is written as a binary number, the code word \(\mathbf e_k\) of \(B\) bits, \(Q=2^B\), so \(Q=8\) in the figure and the worked example has \(B=3\), and the forecasts use \(Q=16\) and \(B=4\). For \(s_k=0.61\) and \(Q=8\) the sample lies in the bin from 0.5 to 0.625, so \(\ell_k=\lfloor4.88\rfloor=4\) and the code word is \(100\). In each compartment \(B\) input cells were chosen once at random, and they are the same at every input step. The \(R\) compartments are therefore \(R\) independent random placements of the same code word. The bits of the code word overwrite the input cells. Every other cell keeps what the earlier inputs and the rule left there, and this kept content is the memory of the reservoir.

In the figure the code word 100 of \(s_k=0.61\) is written into the framed cells. Compare the row \(i=0\) of step \(k\) with the row just above it, the last generation of step \(k-1\). Only framed cells differ between the two rows.

Quantisation is the price of a discrete state. With \(Q\) levels of width \(1/Q\) the rounding error of a uniformly spread value has the standard deviation \(1/(Q\sqrt{12})\), and on the Santa Fe signal the measured root-mean-square error is 0.069 for \(Q=4\), 0.018 for \(Q=16\) and 0.0045 for \(Q=64\). The right panel shows the code words of the levels \(\ell=0,\dots,7\) as rows of three bits (grey for 1).

Evolution

Between two inputs the rule is applied \(N_{\rm gen}\) times to the whole ring, across the compartment boundaries as well. Rule 180 updates every cell as \[c_j\leftarrow c_{j-1}\oplus c_j\oplus c_jc_{j+1},\] where \(\oplus\) is the exclusive or (XOR) and \(c_jc_{j+1}\) the logical AND. It moves the new bits away from the input cells, on average one cell to the right per generation, and combines them with the bits of earlier inputs that they meet. After \(N_{\rm gen}\) generations the ring is a record of the recent input history that also contains products of past bits.

Look at the cell marked \(a\) in the row \(i=0\) of step \(k\). It has just received the most significant bit of \(s_k\), \(a=1\). Its right neighbour, \(b=1\), was left there by \(s_{k-1}\), and the cell checks that another value of \(s_{k-1}\) changes it. Its left neighbour is 0. One generation later the cell (red frame) holds \(0\oplus a\oplus ab=a(1-b)=0\), a product of a bit of \(s_k\) and a bit of \(s_{k-1}\).

The next cell prints the bit operations on all pairs of bits, two updates of rule 180 against its rule table, and one row of the figure updated cell by cell. XOR is addition modulo 2, and AND is the ordinary product of two bits. The column \(a\oplus ab\) is 1 only for \(a=1\), \(b=0\), the logical \(a\) and not \(b\), which in ordinary arithmetic is \(a(1-b)=a-ab\).

Readout and training

The \(N_{\rm gen}+1\) configurations of one input step, the row \(i=0\) after the writing and the rows after each generation, are concatenated into the feature vector \(\mathbf r_k=(\mathbf c_k^{(0)},\dots,\mathbf c_k^{(N_{\rm gen})})\) of \((N_{\rm gen}+1)L\) bits, 64 in the figure, read as the numbers 0 and 1. The forecast of the next 50 values is \(\tilde{\mathbf y}_k=W_{\rm out}^\top\mathbf r_k+\mathbf b\). The weights \(W_{\rm out}\) and the offset \(\mathbf b\) are fitted once by ridge regression over all training steps, and they are the only trained numbers. The last configuration \(\mathbf c_k^{(N_{\rm gen})}\) is carried over and receives the code word of \(s_{k+1}\), so step \(k+1\) starts where step \(k\) ends.

The next cell lets you drive an elementary automaton with a piece of a real signal and look at the result. The left panel shows the signal and its quantised values against the input step, and the right panel the ring after the bits are written and after each of the \(N_{\rm gen}\) generations, with the input steps separated by thin grey lines, the compartments by blue lines, and the input cells in orange, dark for a 1 and light for a 0. Encoding by addition modulo 2 in place of replacement is available as an option. The cell runs the same reservoir twice, once from a ring of zeros and once from a hand-written random-looking ring, and prints the input step from which the two runs agree. The number of bits sets a trade-off that the parameters let you explore. More bits give a finer amplitude, with a rounding error of \(1/(2^B\sqrt{12})\) for a uniformly spread signal, but they overwrite \(B\) of the \(L_c\) cells of each compartment at every step, which leaves fewer cells to carry the past.

def encode(s, B):
    # bit string of one sample, most significant bit first: level l = floor(Q s) clipped to 0 ... Q - 1, in binary code
    l = int(quantise(np.array([s]), 2 ** B)[0])
    return format(l, f"0{B}b")

def run_driven(signal, rule, B=4, R=4, N_generation_steps=4, mode="replace", initial="zeros", L_c=16, input_cells=None, seed=0):
    # space-time array (len(signal) * (N_gen + 1), L): for every input step the ring right after the bits are written
    # and its N_gen iterations; also the positions (R, B) of the input cells, digit b of the code in column b
    if initial in ("zeros", "random"):
        c = np.zeros(R * L_c, int) if initial == "zeros" else np.random.default_rng(seed).integers(0, 2, R * L_c)
    else:
        if set(initial) - {"0", "1"}:
            raise ValueError("initial must be 'zeros', 'random' or a string of the characters 0 and 1")
        c = np.array([int(ch) for ch in initial])
        if len(c) % R:
            raise ValueError(f"the ring of {len(c)} cells cannot be divided into R = {R} compartments")
        L_c = len(c) // R
    if L_c < B:
        raise ValueError(f"a compartment of L_c = {L_c} cells cannot hold B = {B} input bits")
    if input_cells is None:
        cells = np.asarray(compartment_cells(R, L_c, B, seed))           # the same random choice as the CA reservoir
    else:
        if len(input_cells) != B or len(set(input_cells)) != B or not all(0 <= x < L_c for x in input_cells):
            raise ValueError(f"input_cells needs B = {B} distinct positions between 0 and {L_c - 1}")
        cells = np.array([[j * L_c + x for x in input_cells] for j in range(R)])
    rows = []
    for s in signal:
        d = np.array([int(ch) for ch in encode(s, B)[::-1]])      # least significant bit first, as in the CA reservoir
        c = c.copy()
        c[cells] = d[None, :] if mode == "replace" else (c[cells] + d[None, :]) % 2
        rows.append(c)
        for _ in range(N_generation_steps):
            c = (rule >> (4 * np.roll(c, 1) + 2 * c + np.roll(c, -1))) & 1
            rows.append(c)
    return np.array(rows), cells

Training the readout

Training uses the feature vectors of all fitting origins at once. The rows of the matrix \(X\) are \(\mathbf r_k\) for the origins \(k=63,\dots,2449\), and the rows of \(Y\) are the targets \(\mathbf y_k=(s_{k+1},\dots,s_{k+50})\) of the same origins. With the column means \(\bar{\mathbf r}\) of \(X\) and \(\bar{\mathbf y}\) of \(Y\) and the centred matrices \(X_c\) and \(Y_c\), from which these means are subtracted, the ridge regression gives

\[W_{\rm out}=\big(X_c^\top X_c+\beta I\big)^{-1}X_c^\top Y_c,\qquad \mathbf b=\bar{\mathbf y}-W_{\rm out}^\top\bar{\mathbf r}.\]

This is one linear solve for all 50 horizons, since column \(\tau\) of \(W_{\rm out}\) belongs to horizon \(\tau\). The ridge strength \(\beta\) is the value of the grid with the largest validation \(C_{\rm tot}\). The forecast at an origin is \(\tilde{\mathbf y}_k=W_{\rm out}^\top\mathbf r_k+\mathbf b\), and \(C(\tau)\) is computed from the forecasts at the test origins. The next cell runs the reservoir of the example, rule 90 with \(Q=8\), the two compartments above and \(N_{\rm gen}=2\), over the whole Santa Fe record from a ring of zeros, checks the first feature vector against a hand computation, and prints the shapes and the first forecasts.

How \(N_{\rm gen}\) is chosen

The number of generations per input step \(N_{\rm gen}\) is a hyperparameter. In the comparison of single rules below it is chosen on the validation block together with the ridge strength \(\beta\), from \(N_{\rm gen}\in\{1,2,3,4,6,8\}\). What one generation does makes the choice understandable. Under a transport rule such as 180 a bit moves about one cell per generation, so between two writings the record shifts by about \(N_{\rm gen}\) cells and consecutive inputs lie about \(N_{\rm gen}\) cells apart. The trail behind the input cells of one compartment then holds roughly \(L_c/N_{\rm gen}\) past inputs before it runs into the next compartment, where it meets newer bits, and a bit that sits on an input cell at a writing is erased. \(N_{\rm gen}\) thus trades the spacing of the record against its length, and it also sets the number of features \((N_{\rm gen}+1)L\). Under a spreading rule such as 90 or 30 every generation mixes each bit with more of its neighbours, and many generations scramble the record.

Where the nonlinearity comes from

The readout is a weighted sum of the reservoir bits, so every nonlinear function of the past that the forecast uses must already be among the bits. The traced cell of the one-step figure shows how it gets there. Its left neighbour is 0, it holds the bit \(a\) of \(s_k\) and its right neighbour the bit \(b\) of \(s_{k-1}\), and one generation of rule 180 writes \(0\oplus a\oplus ab=a(1-b)=a-ab\) into it. The table of the bit operations shows that this is 1 only for \(a=1\), \(b=0\). No weighted sum \(w_0+w_1a+w_2b\) reproduces it. The row \((0,0)\) requires \(w_0=0\), the rows \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) then require \(w_1=1\) and \(w_2=0\), and the row \((1,1)\) gives \(w_1+w_2=1\) instead of 0.

The readout multiplies the cell by a weight \(w\), so the forecast contains the term \(w\,a(1-b)\). Each bit of the code word is a step function of its sample, so this term is a nonlinear function of the two inputs \(s_k\) and \(s_{k-1}\), the analogue of the product \(s_ks_{k-1}\) that NVAR adds to an autoregressive model. An ESN makes its nonlinearity from continuous sums passed through tanh, and the CA makes it with logic on bits.

Any rule of three cells can be written as a polynomial over \(\mathbb Z_2\), its algebraic normal form, in which \(+\) is addition modulo 2 and a product of cells is their logical AND. The function anf computes this polynomial from the rule table by the Möbius transform, and the cell prints it for the four rules that matter most in this notebook.

def anf(rule, tex=False):
    # algebraic normal form over Z_2: Moebius transform of the rule table; monomials of c_{j-1}, c_j, c_{j+1}
    a = [(rule >> n) & 1 for n in range(8)]
    for b in range(3):
        for n in range(8):
            if n >> b & 1:
                a[n] ^= a[n ^ (1 << b)]
    # check: the polynomial reproduces the rule table
    assert all(sum(a[m] for m in range(8) if m & n == m) % 2 == (rule >> n) & 1 for n in range(8))

    mons = sorted((m for m in range(8) if a[m]), key=lambda m: (bin(m).count("1"), -m))
    terms = ["1" if m == 0 else "".join(v for bit, v in names if m & bit) for m in mons]
    if tex:

Among the four rules only rule 170 forms no product of any kind. Under rule 170 every cell holds a copy of one bit of one past sample, so its readout is a sum \(f_0(s_k)+f_1(s_{k-1})+f_2(s_{k-2})+\dots\) of functions of single samples. Rule 180 moves the bits in the same way and adds the product of neighbouring cells, so it can multiply bits of two samples when they meet.

Rule space and the edge of chaos

Langton proposed a single parameter to order the rule space [Langton, Physica D 42, 12 (1990)]. One state is chosen as quiescent, here 0, and a rule must map the all-quiescent neighbourhood to it. Langton’s parameter \(\lambda_L\) is the fraction of rule-table entries that map to a non-quiescent state. For \(\lambda_L=0\) every configuration dies, and as \(\lambda_L\) grows the dynamics passes from fixed and periodic patterns to complex and then chaotic ones. Langton observed the complex behaviour in an intermediate range of \(\lambda_L\) and called it the edge of chaos. For the elementary rules with \(000\to0\), the 128 even rule numbers, \(\lambda_L\) is the number of 1 bits in the rule table divided by 8. To sample richer rule spaces we also draw random rules over \(\mathbb Z_4\) with radius \(a=1\), 64 table entries, by Langton’s table method. Each entry is quiescent with probability \(1-\lambda_L^{\rm target}\) and otherwise a random state from 1 to 3, with the target value \(\lambda_L^{\rm target}\) on a grid \(0,0.05,\dots,0.75\), three rules per value. The drawn tables have a measured \(\lambda_L\) close to the target, and all figures use the measured value. Langton used four states and a neighbourhood of five cells, so our random rules are a smaller variant of his.

For every rule we measure two properties of the dynamics, after one cell of a random configuration on \(L=257\) cells has been changed. The damage density \(H\) is the fraction of cells in which the two copies differ after 100 iterations, and the spreading rate \(v_f\) is the width of the damaged region divided by its largest possible value \(2\cdot100+1\), so that \(v_f=1\) means that the damage spreads by one cell per iteration in both directions. A damage that travels along the lattice without growing has \(v_f\) close to zero, whatever its speed. Both are averaged over 10 random starts. For every rule we also measure the forecast quality of a CA reservoir built from it, the validation \(C_{\rm tot}\) on Santa Fe and on Mackey–Glass at 25 with \(Q=16\) levels, binary code, replacement encoding, \(R=8\) compartments of 16 cells and \(N_{\rm gen}=4\), which gives 640 features.

def damage_stats(table, q, L=257, T=100, n_runs=10, seed=0):
    # one-cell perturbation of a random state: damage density H(T) and spreading rate v_f = (width of the damage)/(2T+1)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed); h, v = [], []
    for _ in range(n_runs):
        c0 = rng.integers(0, q, L)
        c1 = c0.copy(); c1[L // 2] = (c0[L // 2] + rng.integers(1, q)) % q
        D = np.asarray(ca_run(jnp.asarray(c0, jnp.int32), table, q, T)[-1] != ca_run(jnp.asarray(c1, jnp.int32), table, q, T)[-1])
        h.append(D.mean())
        idx = np.nonzero(D)[0] - L // 2
        v.append((idx.max() - idx.min() + 1) / (2 * T + 1) if len(idx) else 0.0)
    return float(np.mean(h)), float(np.mean(v))

def langton_lambda(table, q):
    # fraction of rule-table entries that map to a non-quiescent state (quiescent state 0)
    return float(np.mean(np.asarray(table) != 0))

def langton_table(lam, q, rng):
    # random rule over Z_q with radius 1: each entry is quiescent with probability 1 - lam; 000 -> 0 is kept
    t = np.where(rng.random(q ** 3) < lam, rng.integers(1, q, q ** 3), 0)
    t[0] = 0
    return jnp.asarray(t, jnp.int32)

Forecast map of all elementary rules

The rule scan fixes the number of generation steps per input step at \(N_{\rm gen}=4\) and admits only the 128 rules with \(000\to0\). We now map the forecast quality over all elementary rules and over \(N_{\rm gen}\). Many of the 256 rules are copies of each other. Reflecting a rule from left to right, \(f'(c_{j-1},c_j,c_{j+1})=f(c_{j+1},c_j,c_{j-1})\), or exchanging the roles of 0 and 1, \(f'(c_{j-1},c_j,c_{j+1})=1-f(1-c_{j-1},1-c_j,1-c_{j+1})\), gives an automaton with the same dynamics up to a mirror image or a complement of every configuration. The code computes these equivalence classes, finds 88 of them and represents each class by its smallest rule number. Rules 154, 166, 180 and 210 form one class, so the two shift rules that led the rule scan are the same automaton, rule 210 being the complement of rule 180 and rule 166 its mirror image. In the reservoir the members of a class can still differ, because the injected code word is not complemented together with the rule and a mirrored lattice meets a different random mapping. Rules that map \(000\) to 1 are now included. They change a quiet ring at the next iteration, which is harmless in a driven reservoir whose mapped cells are rewritten by the input at every step.

Each entry of the map is the validation \(C_{\rm tot}\) of one reservoir, with the forecasting protocol of the ESN, the same origins and 50 horizons, and the ridge strength chosen on validation data from \(\{10^{-4},10^{-2},1,10,100\}\). The other settings are fixed at the values that the forecasting search below selects for Santa Fe, \(Q=16\) levels in binary code, replacement encoding and \(R=16\) compartments of \(L_c=16\) cells. The number of generation steps per input step takes the values \(N_{\rm gen}=1,2,3,4,6,8\), which gives 512 to 2304 features. One random mapping (seed 0) is used for every rule, so that the columns of the map differ only by the rule. The map is computed for Santa Fe and for Mackey–Glass at 25 and 35, and the automata of eight rules at a time run in parallel through jax.vmap over the rule tables. For every class we also measure the spreading rate \(v_f\) of its representative with the damage runs of the rule scan, and we write the rule as a polynomial over \(\mathbb Z_2\), its algebraic normal form. This is a sum modulo 2 of products of the three neighbourhood cells, obtained from the rule table by a Möbius transform, and it shows at a glance which cells a rule copies and which products it adds.

# the 88 dynamically distinct elementary rules: classes under left-right reflection and 0 <-> 1 complement
def reflect(rule):
    # f'(l, c, r) = f(r, c, l): output bit n of the new rule is bit 4r + 2c + l of the old one
    return sum(((rule >> (4 * (n & 1) + (n & 2) + (n >> 2))) & 1) << n for n in range(8))
def complement(rule):
    # f'(l, c, r) = 1 - f(1 - l, 1 - c, 1 - r): inverted inputs (index 7 - n) and inverted output
    return sum((1 - ((rule >> (7 - n)) & 1)) << n for n in range(8))

Forecast score of single rules

The map gives one number per rule and setting, the validation \(C_{\rm tot}\). The capacity curve \(C(\tau)\) against the horizon shows where along the 50 horizons a rule keeps or loses the forecast, and we compute it on the test block of all five records for nine rules that span the behaviours seen so far.

  1. Rule 204, the identity \(c_j\leftarrow c_j\) (Wolfram class II), keeps every cell where it is, so nothing of an earlier input survives the next injection. Rule 0 (class I), which empties the lattice, gives the same information, and the code computes it as a check without plotting it.
  2. Rule 170, \(c_j\leftarrow c_{j+1}\) (class II), is a pure shift, which moves every bit one cell to the left per iteration without changing it.
  3. Rule 180, \(c_{j-1}\oplus c_j\oplus c_jc_{j+1}\), shifts and adds a local product, and its class gives the best single entries of the map. Rule 27 moves bits on a background that blinks between all 0 and all 1, and its class is the best by the mean of the map.
  4. Rule 184, the traffic rule (class II), moves a car one cell only when the cell ahead is free.
  5. The additive rules 90 and 150 (class III) spread a perturbation over the ring at the largest possible rate.
  6. Rule 30 (class III) is the standard chaotic rule and rule 110 (class IV) the complex one.

Five-bit memory task

The five-bit memory task tests whether a system can store a short binary pattern over a long distractor period [H. Jaeger, Jacobs University Technical Report 27 (2012), pdf]. There are four binary input channels and four output channels, and at every step exactly one input channel is 1. During the first 5 steps channel 1 or channel 2 carries one bit of the pattern, so there are \(2^5=32\) possible sequences. Then the distractor channel 3 is on for \(T_0-1\) steps, a cue appears on channel 4 at step \(T_0+5\), and in the last 5 steps the outputs on channels 1 and 2 must repeat the pattern. Before the recall the output channel 3 must be 1. A run succeeds if every output at every step is within 0.5 of its target, and a trial succeeds if all 32 sequences succeed. Training and testing use the same 32 sequences, as in the original task.

Yilmaz solved this task with elementary rules and reported the percentage of failed trials on a grid of \(R\) and \(N_{\rm gen}\). His paper does not state how each new input is combined with the lattice state or how many cells a mapping occupies. We follow the implementation described by Nichele and Molund [S. Nichele, A. Molund, arXiv:1703.02806 (2017), arXiv:1703.02806]. Each of the \(R\) mappings places the 4 input bits on random cells of a compartment of \(L_c=40\) cells, the compartments form one ring of \(L=RL_c\) cells, the input overwrites its cells in the last state, and the features are the \(N_{\rm gen}\) configurations after the injection, \(RN_{\rm gen}L_c\) binary values. The readout is a ridge regression with \(\beta=10^{-6}\), close to the pseudo-inverse used by Yilmaz. We use \(T_0=200\) and 10 trials per grid point, each with its own random mapping, so a percentage moves in steps of 10. The grid of \(R\) and \(N_{\rm gen}\) from 1 to 8 is limited by the size of the ridge regression, which grows as \((RN_{\rm gen}L_c)^2\).

Code 
def five_bit_data(T0):
    # all 32 input sequences of the 5-bit memory task: 4 one-hot input channels, 4 target channels, length T0 + 10
    T = T0 + 10
    U = np.zeros((32, T, 4), np.int32); Y = np.zeros((32, T, 4))
    for p in range(32):
        bits = (p >> np.arange(5)) & 1
        U[p, np.arange(5), bits] = 1                 # pattern on channel 1 or 2 during the first 5 steps
        U[p, 5:T0 + 4, 2] = 1                        # distractor
        U[p, T0 + 4, 3] = 1                          # cue
        U[p, T0 + 5:, 2] = 1                         # distractor during the recall
        Y[p, :T0 + 5, 2] = 1                         # "waiting" output
        Y[p, np.arange(T0 + 5, T), bits] = 1         # recall of the pattern
    return U, Y

def five_bit_trial(rule, R, N_generation_steps, Lc, seed, U, Y, beta=1e-6):
    # one trial = one random mapping; trained and tested on all 32 sequences; success if every output is within 0.5
    cells = compartment_cells(R, Lc, 4, seed)
    X = jax.vmap(lambda u: ca_reservoir(eca_table(rule), u, cells, 2, R * Lc, N_generation_steps, False, False))(jnp.asarray(U))
    X = np.asarray(X.reshape(-1, X.shape[-1]), np.float32); Yf = Y.reshape(-1, 4)
    (_, W, b), = ridge_int(X, Yf, (beta,))
    return bool(np.all(np.abs(X.astype(np.float64) @ W + b - Yf) < 0.5))

Damage spreading and the memory task

The spreading rate is the cellular automaton analogue of the largest Lyapunov exponent \(\lambda_1\). In a chaotic flow a small perturbation grows exponentially in amplitude, while in a cellular automaton a perturbation cannot grow in amplitude and grows instead in extent, at most by one cell per iteration on each side. Echo state property of the CA reservoir

The damage test runs without input. The echo state property concerns two copies of the reservoir driven by the same input, and in the CA reservoir the input overwrites only the \(B\) mapped cells of each compartment at every step, so the other cells need not forget the initial configuration. We start two copies of the forecasting reservoir from different random configurations of the whole ring, drive both with the same input sequence and record after every input step the fraction of cells in which the lattice states \(\mathbf c_k^{(N_{\rm gen})}\) of the two copies differ. The test uses five pairs of initial configurations at the selected setting of every signal, and on Santa Fe at its selected setting also the rules 90, 30 and 4.

Code 
@partial(jax.jit, static_argnums=(3, 4, 5))
def ca_lattice(table, digits, cells, q, N_generation_steps, additive, c0):
    # lattice state c_k^(N_gen) after every input step of the CA reservoir, started from the configuration c0
    def step(c, d):
        vals = jnp.broadcast_to(d, cells.shape)
        c = c.at[cells].set((c[cells] + vals) % q if additive else vals)
        for _ in range(N_generation_steps):
            c = ca_step(c, table, q)
        return c, c
    return jax.lax.scan(step, c0, digits)[1]

def ca_two_copies(s, rule, Q, R, Lc, N_generation_steps, additive=False, seed=0, n_pairs=5):
    # fraction of differing cells after every input step for n_pairs of copies from random initial configurations
    table, q = CA_RULES[rule][:2] if rule in CA_RULES else (eca_table(int(rule)), 2)
    d = jnp.asarray(ca_digits(s, Q, q)); cells = compartment_cells(R, Lc, d.shape[1], seed)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(100); out = []
    for _ in range(n_pairs):
        ca_, cb_ = (ca_lattice(table, d, cells, q, N_generation_steps, additive, jnp.asarray(rng.integers(0, q, R * Lc), jnp.int32)) for _ in range(2))
        out.append(np.asarray(ca_ != cb_).mean(1))
    return np.array(out)

def esp_summary(F):
    # per pair: first step after which the copies agree for good (None if they still differ at the end)
    locks = []
    for f in F:
        nz = np.nonzero(f > 0)[0]
        locks.append(0 if len(nz) == 0 else (int(nz[-1] + 1) if nz[-1] < len(f) - 1 else None))
    done = [k for k in locks if k is not None]
    return dict(n_locked=len(done), n_pairs=len(locks), lock_max=max(done) if done else None,
                frac_test=float(F[:, K_TEST].mean()), frac_end=float(F[:, -1].mean()))

Shift-register control

The best rules of the map and of the forecasting search copy a neighbour and add a few local products, as the algebraic normal form of rule 180, \(c_{j-1}\oplus c_j\oplus c_jc_{j+1}\), shows. Their forecasts may come from the transport of the injected code bits alone, which turns the lattice into a delay line of bit-coded past values, or from the local products, which add nonlinear features. We separate the two with a control model without automaton. Its feature vector at step \(k\) holds the binary code words of the last \(D\) samples \(s_k,s_{k-1},\dots,s_{k-D+1}\), with the quantisation of the selected Santa Fe setting, \(Q=16\) levels and \(B=4\) bits, so it is a shift register of code words, with zero code words before the start of the record. The readout is the ridge regression of the CA reservoir with the same grid of ridge strengths, chosen on validation data. We use three variants on every signal. The first has \(D=64\) lags and 256 features. The second has as many features as the selected CA reservoir of the signal, \(D=320\) lags for the 1280 features on Santa Fe. The third adds to the bits of \(D\) lags all 16 products of a bit of lag \(d\) with a bit of lag \(d+1\), \(4D+16(D-1)\) features with \(D\) chosen to match the size of the CA reservoir. With the linear terms a linear readout of these products can form the exclusive or and the product of any bit of one lag with any bit of the next, the kind of terms that rule 180 forms between neighbouring cells.

Code 
# shift-register control: binary code words of the last D samples (Q = 16, B = 4), no automaton; zero code words before the record
SHIFT_Q, SHIFT_SMALL_D = 16, 64
def shift_features(s, ks, D, products=False):
    # rows: code words of s_k, s_{k-1}, ..., s_{k-D+1}; optionally all products of a bit of lag d with a bit of lag d+1
    bits = np.concatenate([np.zeros((D, 4), np.int32), ca_digits(s, SHIFT_Q, 2)])
    F = bits[ks[:, None] + D - np.arange(D)[None, :]]                        # (origins, D, B)
    parts = [F.reshape(len(ks), -1)]
    if products:
        parts.append((F[:, :-1, :, None] * F[:, 1:, None, :]).reshape(len(ks), -1))
    return np.concatenate(parts, 1).astype(np.float32)
def shift_eval(s, D, products=False):
    # ridge strength chosen on validation from the grid of the CA readout; validation and test C_tot
    Yv = targets(s, K_VAL); best = (-1.0, None)
    for beta, W, b in ridge_int(shift_features(s, K_FIT, D, products), targets(s, K_FIT), CA_BETA):
        v = score(shift_features(s, K_VAL, D, products).astype(np.float64) @ W + b, Yv)["Ctot"]
        if v > best[0]:
            best = (v, W, b)
    P = shift_features(s, K_TEST, D, products).astype(np.float64) @ best[1] + best[2]
    return dict(D=D, n_feat=best[1].shape[0], val=best[0], Ctot=score(P, targets(s, K_TEST))["Ctot"])

Summary

A cellular automaton is a reservoir with discrete space, time and states. Rule 184 shows that such a system can also be a physical model, with a free-flow and a jammed phase of traffic separated at density 1/2. Langton’s \(\lambda_L\) orders the rule space, and damage spreading measures how far a local perturbation travels. A flipped cell survives under ordered rules such as rule 4 and spreads under rules 90, 30 and 110 when no input acts. Driven by the same input, two copies of the selected reservoirs with the shift rules 180 and 210 agree after at most 169 steps in all tested pairs, while copies under rules 90, 30 and 4 do not agree within the record. In the five-bit memory task the spreading additive rules 90 and 150 store the pattern once the number of mappings and iterations is large enough, in line with the trend of Yilmaz’s results at a similar feature count, and rules without spreading always fail. Our individual failure rates differ from his table at the one shared grid point, since the size of a mapping is not specified in the original work. Forecasting orders the rules differently. The best reservoirs come from shift rules such as 180 and 210, in which a perturbation travels along the lattice without spreading, and the correlation between spreading rate and forecast quality over the elementary rules is negative, so our data do not support the edge-of-chaos hypothesis for this task. A map over the 88 distinct elementary rules and 1 to 8 generation steps per input step shows the same ordering on Santa Fe and Mackey–Glass, whose rankings agree with a rank correlation of 0.91. The test capacity curves of single rules separate three behaviours. The identity rule 204 forgets the past at every injection and forecasts at the level of persistence, the spreading rules 90, 150, 30 and 110 do best with one or two generation steps and stay below the transport rules on Lorenz and Santa Fe, and the shift rules with local products, 180, 27 and 184, give the highest curves, while the pure shift 170 stays close to AR on four of the five records. A shift register of quantised past values matches the CA reservoir on Mackey–Glass at 25 and 35 and falls behind on Lorenz and Santa Fe, where products of neighbouring code bits close most of the gap on Lorenz and part of it on Santa Fe. With up to 2304 features the CA reservoir beats AR on the four irregular signals, reaches 40.5 on Lorenz and 33.3 on Santa Fe against 39.2 and 34.7 for the 300-unit ESN, and stays below the ESN on Mackey–Glass at 25 and 35, limited by the quantisation of the input and by one bit of state per cell. All results refer to the signals, settings and random draws of this notebook.
