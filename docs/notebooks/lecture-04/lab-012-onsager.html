Lab 012 — Onsager’s solution of the 2D Ising model

Consider spins \(s_i=\pm1\) sitting on the sites of a two-dimensional (2D) square lattice with \(N\) sites and periodic boundaries, so that the lattice is a torus. Neighbouring spins prefer to be parallel, which the Hamiltonian \[ H(\mathbf s) = -J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} s_i s_j \] expresses with a coupling energy \(J>0\); the sum \(\langle i,j\rangle\) runs over the \(2N\) nearest-neighbour bonds (each site has 4 neighbours and each bond is shared by 2 sites). At temperature \(T\) a configuration \(\mathbf s=(s_1,\dots,s_N)\) occurs with the Boltzmann probability \[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf s) &= \frac{e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)}}{Z},\qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_BT},\\ Z(T) &= \sum_{\mathbf s\in\{\pm1\}^N} e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)} , \end{aligned} \] where \(k_B\) is the Boltzmann constant. Low energy is favoured, and the more so the lower the temperature.

The figure below shows these objects on a small patch of the lattice. Spins sit on the sites (a filled navy disc is \(s_i=+1\), a white disc with an orange rim is \(s_i=-1\), the convention of all sketches in this course), and every nearest-neighbour bond contributes \(-Js_is_j\) to the energy: \(-J\) for a satisfied bond of two parallel spins (solid line) and \(+J\) for a broken bond of two antiparallel spins (dashed line with a cross). The faded copies of the first row and of the first column indicate the periodic boundaries.

The whole story of this notebook is about the normalisation \(Z\) of this distribution, the partition function, a sum over all \(2^N\) configurations. We shall see in Section 1 that every thermodynamic quantity — energy, specific heat, magnetisation — is a derivative of \(\ln Z\), so that knowing \(Z(T)\) means knowing everything. The central quantity is the free energy per spin \(f = -\frac{T}{N}\ln Z\) (with \(k_B=1\)) in the limit \(N\to\infty\): the energy follows as \(u = \partial(\beta f)/\partial\beta\), the specific heat as \(c = \partial u/\partial T\), and a phase transition is a temperature at which \(f(T)\) fails to be an analytic function.

Already for \(N = 100\) spins the sum has \(2^{100}\approx1.3\times10^{30}\) terms, so \(Z\) cannot be computed term by term. To solve the model means to find \(f\) in closed form for \(N\to\infty\), and this is what Onsager achieved for the square lattice [Onsager 1944]: \[ \begin{aligned} -\beta f &= \ln(2\cosh2K) + \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi d\phi\,\ln\Big[\tfrac12\Big(1+\sqrt{1-\kappa^2\sin^2\phi}\Big)\Big],\\ K &= \beta J,\qquad \kappa = \frac{2\sinh2K}{\cosh^22K}. \end{aligned} \] The integrand is smooth except when \(\kappa = 1\), which happens at a single temperature, \(\sinh 2K_c = 1\), that is \(T_c = 2J/\big(k_B\ln(1+\sqrt2)\big) \approx 2.269\,J/k_B\).

The formula matters for three reasons. The first is conceptual. Each Boltzmann weight \(e^{-\beta H}\) is an analytic function of \(T\), and for finite \(N\) so is \(\ln Z\) (Section 1); a sharp transition, a kink or a divergence in a thermodynamic quantity, can therefore exist only in the limit \(N\to\infty\). Peierls [1936] had argued that the 2D model orders at low temperature, and Kramers and Wannier [1941] had located \(T_c\) by a symmetry argument, but Onsager’s formula was the first exact free energy of a model with a phase transition, and with it the non-analyticity could be seen explicitly. The second reason is that the exact solution yields exact critical exponents. Near \(T_c\) it gives a logarithmically divergent specific heat and, together with Yang’s magnetisation [Yang 1952], \(M\propto(T_c-T)^{1/8}\), whereas mean-field theory, the standard approximation, predicts a finite jump in the specific heat and \(M\propto(T_c-T)^{1/2}\) (Section 9). This disagreement is a central motivation of the theory of scaling and of the renormalisation group [Kardar 2007]. The third reason is practical: exact results are rare, so every numerical method for lattice models — Monte Carlo sampling, transfer matrices, and the neural networks of this course — is tested against Onsager’s solution. In particular, \(T_c\approx2.269\) is the temperature that the convolutional neural network (CNN) of Lab 011 should locate from the Monte Carlo spin images of Lab 010.

We shall not reproduce Onsager’s derivation, which fills a 33-page paper. Instead we build the ideas behind it one at a time, derive in full what can be derived in a few lines, summarise the rest through its key equations, and confirm every result twice: symbolically with the computer-algebra library SymPy, which checks the exact algebra of each derivation, and numerically, against brute-force enumeration, the transfer matrix or Onsager’s formula. The route is as follows. Section 1 explains what it means to solve the model, computes \(\ln Z\) of a finite lattice by brute force and shows why it is always smooth. Section 2 introduces the transfer matrix on the one-dimensional (1D) chain, where the free energy and the correlation length can be found exactly and there is no transition. Section 3 carries the method to 2D, with transfer matrices of size \(2^L\times2^L\) for strips of width \(L\), and compares one dimension with two. Section 4 develops the high- and low-temperature expansions of \(Z\), and Section 5 turns their resemblance into Kramers–Wannier duality and the self-dual point \(\sinh 2K_c = 1\). Section 6 outlines the free-fermion derivation of Onsager’s formula and extracts from it \(f\), \(u\), \(c\) and Yang’s magnetisation \(M\). Section 7 obtains the energy at the critical point, \(u(T_c) = -\sqrt2\,J\), both from the solution and from duality alone. Section 8 computes the critical exponents from the exact formulas and from the transfer matrix, Section 9 confronts mean-field theory with the exact solution, and Section 10 tests universality on the triangular lattice.

Throughout we use units \(J = k_B = 1\) and zero magnetic field \(h = 0\) unless stated otherwise, so that \(K = J/(k_BT) = 1/T\) is the dimensionless coupling.

1. The partition function and exact solutions

Before computing anything we should be clear about what we are after. In this section we show that all of thermodynamics is contained in \(\ln Z\), give a mathematical definition of a phase transition, and compute \(Z\) exactly for the smallest lattices. The lesson — a finite lattice never has a transition — tells us what the later sections must achieve.

Write \(E(\mathbf s)=H(\mathbf s)\) and \(\langle A\rangle = \sum_{\mathbf s}A(\mathbf s)\,p(\mathbf s)\) for the thermal average. Differentiating \(Z=\sum_{\mathbf s}e^{-\beta E(\mathbf s)}\) with respect to \(\beta\) brings down a factor \(-E\), so \(\ln Z\) generates the averages: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta} &= \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\mathbf s}\big(-E(\mathbf s)\big)e^{-\beta E(\mathbf s)} = -\langle E\rangle,\\ \frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} &= \langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2 \;\ge 0 . \end{aligned} \] The second derivative is the derivative of \(-\langle E\rangle\), and differentiating the ratio produces the variance. In terms of the free energy per spin \(f = -\frac{1}{N\beta}\ln Z\), the energy per spin and the specific heat per spin are therefore \[ \begin{aligned} u &= \frac{\langle E\rangle}{N} = \frac{\partial(\beta f)}{\partial\beta},\\ c &= \frac{\partial u}{\partial T} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial\beta}\frac{d\beta}{dT} = \frac{\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2}{NT^2}, \end{aligned} \] where we used \(d\beta/dT=-1/T^2\). The specific heat is thus a variance, and a large \(c\) signals large energy fluctuations. In the same way a magnetic field \(h\), added as \(-h\sum_is_i\) to \(H\), gives the magnetisation per spin \(m = \frac1N\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial(\beta h)} = \frac1N\big\langle\sum_is_i\big\rangle\).

Because \(\ln Z\) grows proportionally to \(N\) (it is extensive), the per-spin quantities \(f\), \(u\), \(c\) have finite limits as \(N\to\infty\), and these limits describe a macroscopic sample with \(N\sim10^{23}\) spins.

A phase transition is a temperature \(T_c\) at which \(f(T)\) is not analytic, so that some derivative of \(f\) jumps or diverges. For finite \(N\) this cannot happen: \(Z(\beta)=\sum_E n_E e^{-\beta E}\) is a finite sum of exponentials, each analytic in \(\beta\), and \(Z>0\) for real \(\beta\), so \(\ln Z\) is analytic with all derivatives finite. A limit of analytic functions, however, need not be analytic. Consider \[ f_N(x) = \frac1N\ln\big(e^{Nx}+e^{-Nx}\big)\ \xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\ |x| . \] Each \(f_N\) is smooth, yet the limit has a kink at \(x=0\), and the approach is uniform, \(0\le f_N(x)-|x|\le\frac{\ln 2}{N}\). A phase transition is this mechanism at work in \(\ln Z/N\). The cell below plots \(f_N\) for growing \(N\) and prints the largest deviation from \(|x|\), which should saturate the bound \(\ln2/N\) (it does, at \(x=0\)).

1.1 Exact enumeration of a small lattice

On an \(L\times L\) torus there are \(2^{L^2}\) configurations, which for \(L = 4\) makes \(65\,536\) — few enough to list them all. We store every configuration as a row of \(\pm1\): the bits of the integer \(n\in\{0,\dots,2^N-1\}\) give the spins through \(s = 1-2\,\text{bit}\).

Rather than keeping all energies, it pays to count broken bonds. A bond \(\langle i,j\rangle\) is satisfied if \(s_is_j=+1\) (energy \(-1\)) and broken if \(s_is_j=-1\) (energy \(+1\)). With \(b\) broken bonds out of \(2N\) the energy is \(E = -(2N-b) + b = -2N + 2b\), so \(E\) depends only on \(b\), and \(Z\) is fixed by the histogram \(n_b\), the number of configurations with \(b\) broken bonds (the density of states): \[ Z(T) = \sum_{b=0}^{2N} n_b\, e^{-(-2N+2b)/T}. \] Two features of the histogram can be predicted in advance. Every configuration and its global flip \(\mathbf s\to-\mathbf s\) have the same \(b\), so every \(n_b\) is even. Moreover, on the square-lattice torus \(b\) itself is always even: the product of \(s_is_j\) over all \(2N\) bonds is \(\prod_i s_i^4=1\), so the number of factors equal to \(-1\) is even. The printed histogram for \(4\times4\) shows both properties; note also its symmetry \(n_b = n_{32-b}\).

def all_configs(n):
    # all 2^n configurations of n spins, as rows of +-1 (bit k of the row index -> spin k)
    bits = (np.arange(2**n)[:, None] >> np.arange(n)) & 1
    return (1 - 2 * bits).astype(np.int8)

def torus_energies(L):
    # energies of all 2^(L*L) configurations of the L x L periodic square lattice (J = 1)
    S = all_configs(L * L).reshape(-1, L, L).astype(np.int32)
    return -(S * np.roll(S, 1, axis=1)).sum(axis=(1, 2)) - (S * np.roll(S, 1, axis=2)).sum(axis=(1, 2))

1.2 Thermodynamics of the finite lattice

With \(n_b\) in hand, \(\ln Z\), \(u\) and \(c\) follow from the formulas above with the energy levels \(E_b=-2N+2b\): \[ \begin{aligned} \ln Z &= \ln\sum_b n_b e^{-E_b/T},\\ u &= \frac{1}{N}\frac{\sum_b n_b E_b e^{-E_b/T}}{Z},\\ c &= \frac{\langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2}{NT^2}. \end{aligned} \] The function thermo_from_dos evaluates these sums. To avoid overflow of \(e^{-E_b/T}\) it subtracts the largest exponent before exponentiating (the “log-sum-exp” trick). The next cell plots \(f\) and \(c\) for the \(3\times3\) and \(4\times4\) tori; the cell after it confirms the fluctuation formula for \(c\) by comparing it with a numerical derivative \(du/dT\), and the two columns agree to all printed digits.

def thermo_from_dos(nb, N, T):
    # ln Z / N, u and c per spin from the histogram n_b of broken bonds
    T = np.atleast_1d(T)[:, None]
    Eb = -2 * N + 2 * np.arange(len(nb))
    logw = np.log(np.where(nb > 0, nb, 1)) - Eb / T
    logw = np.where(nb > 0, logw, -np.inf)
    shift = logw.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)                  # avoid overflow
    w = np.exp(logw - shift); Zr = w.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True); p = w / Zr
    E1 = (p * Eb).sum(1); E2 = (p * Eb**2).sum(1)
    lnZ = (np.log(Zr) + shift)[:, 0]
    return lnZ / N, E1 / N, (E2 - E1**2) / (N * T[:, 0]**2)

2. The 1D chain and the transfer matrix

The 1D chain is the simplest place to learn the transfer-matrix method that Onsager used in 2D. It also provides a reference point: we shall find that the chain has no phase transition, which is what makes the 2D result remarkable.

Place \(N\) spins on a ring, \(s_{N+1}\equiv s_1\), with coupling \(J=1\) and field \(h\): \[ H = -\sum_{i=1}^N s_is_{i+1} - h\sum_{i=1}^N s_i ,\qquad K = \beta,\quad \tilde h = \beta h . \] The first idea is to write the Boltzmann weight as a product of factors, one per bond. Every site belongs to two bonds, so we can give half of its field term to each: \[ \begin{aligned} -\beta H &= \sum_{i=1}^N\Big[Ks_is_{i+1} + \tfrac{\tilde h}{2}(s_i+s_{i+1})\Big]\\ \Rightarrow\quad e^{-\beta H} &= \prod_{i=1}^N \mathsf T_{s_is_{i+1}},\qquad \mathsf T_{ss'} = e^{Kss' + \frac{\tilde h}{2}(s+s')} . \end{aligned} \] Here \(\mathsf T_{ss'}\) is the Boltzmann weight of one bond. Labelling rows and columns by \(s=+1\) (first) and \(s=-1\) (second), it is the matrix \[ \mathsf T = \begin{pmatrix} e^{K+\tilde h} & e^{-K}\\ e^{-K} & e^{K-\tilde h}\end{pmatrix}. \]

The sketch below draws a ring of \(N=8\) spins. Every bond carries one factor \(\mathsf T_{s_is_{i+1}}\), and the \(2\times2\) table on the right lists the four possible values of this factor, one for each pair of neighbouring spins. The Boltzmann weight of the whole ring is the product of the eight bond factors, and closing the ring turns the sum over all spins into the trace of a matrix power, as we now show.

The second idea is that summing over a spin is a matrix multiplication. The spin \(s_2\) appears only in \(\mathsf T_{s_1s_2}\mathsf T_{s_2s_3}\), and \(\sum_{s_2}\mathsf T_{s_1s_2}\mathsf T_{s_2s_3} = (\mathsf T^2)_{s_1s_3}\) is precisely the definition of the matrix product. Summing \(s_2,\dots,s_N\) in turn and closing the ring we find \[ \begin{aligned} Z &= \sum_{s_1,\dots,s_N}\mathsf T_{s_1s_2}\mathsf T_{s_2s_3}\cdots\mathsf T_{s_Ns_1}\\ &= \sum_{s_1}(\mathsf T^N)_{s_1s_1} = \operatorname{Tr}\mathsf T^N , \end{aligned} \] so that a sum of \(2^N\) terms has become \(N-1\) multiplications of \(2\times2\) matrices.

The trace of a matrix power is controlled by the eigenvalues. Since \(\mathsf T\) is real symmetric, \(\mathsf T = U\Lambda U^\top\) with \(U\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_+,\lambda_-)\). Then \(\mathsf T^N = U\Lambda^NU^\top\) and, because the trace is invariant under \(U\), \[ Z = \lambda_+^N + \lambda_-^N . \] The eigenvalues of a symmetric \(2\times2\) matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{pmatrix}\) are \(\frac{a+d}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{a-d}{2}\right)^2+b^2}\), which gives \[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_\pm &= e^{K}\cosh\tilde h \pm \sqrt{e^{2K}\sinh^2\tilde h + e^{-2K}},\\ \text{at } h=0:\quad \lambda_\pm &= e^K\pm e^{-K},\ \text{i.e. } \lambda_+ = 2\cosh K,\ \lambda_- = 2\sinh K , \end{aligned} \] with eigenvectors \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\) at \(h=0\).

A small case makes the result concrete. A ring of two spins (\(N=2\), \(h=0\)) has two bonds between the same pair, so directly \(Z=\sum_{s_1,s_2}e^{2Ks_1s_2} = 2e^{2K}+2e^{-2K}=4\cosh2K\), while the formula gives \((2\cosh K)^2+(2\sinh K)^2 = 4(\cosh^2K+\sinh^2K) = 4\cosh 2K\), the same.

2.1 Free energy and correlation length of the chain

Factor out the larger eigenvalue: \(\ln Z = N\ln\lambda_+ + \ln\big(1 + r^N\big)\) with \(r=\lambda_-/\lambda_+ = \tanh K<1\). The second term tends to \(0\), so for \(N\to\infty\) only the largest eigenvalue survives: \[ \begin{aligned} -\beta f &= \ln\lambda_+ = \ln\big(2\cosh K\big),\\ u &= \frac{\partial(\beta f)}{\partial\beta} = -\tanh K,\\ c &= \frac{\partial u}{\partial T} = \frac{K^2}{\cosh^2K}. \end{aligned} \] These are compositions of analytic functions with \(2\cosh K>0\), so \(f\) is analytic at every \(T>0\): the 1D chain has no phase transition [Ising 1925]. Its specific heat is a single smooth bump that does not depend on \(N\).

The transfer matrix also tells us how strongly spin \(s_0\) influences spin \(s_r\). The product \(s_0s_r\) inserts the diagonal matrix \(\sigma^z = \operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\) at the two positions: \[ \langle s_0s_r\rangle = \frac{\operatorname{Tr}\big(\sigma^z\mathsf T^r\sigma^z\mathsf T^{N-r}\big)}{\operatorname{Tr}\mathsf T^N}. \] Write \(\mathsf T = \lambda_+|{+}\rangle\langle{+}| + \lambda_-|{-}\rangle\langle{-}|\) with the eigenvectors \(|{\pm}\rangle = (1,\pm1)/\sqrt2\). For \(N\to\infty\), \(\mathsf T^{N-r}\approx\lambda_+^{N-r}|{+}\rangle\langle{+}|\), and \(\sigma^z\) maps \(|{+}\rangle\) to \(|{-}\rangle\). Hence \[ \begin{aligned} \langle s_0s_r\rangle &\to \frac{\lambda_+^{N-r}\,\langle{-}|\mathsf T^r|{-}\rangle}{\lambda_+^N} = \Big(\frac{\lambda_-}{\lambda_+}\Big)^r\\ &= (\tanh K)^r = e^{-r/\xi},\\ \xi &= \frac{1}{\ln(\lambda_+/\lambda_-)} = -\frac{1}{\ln\tanh K}. \end{aligned} \] The quantity \(\xi\) is the correlation length, the distance over which a spin “remembers” the orientation of another, and it is finite at every \(T>0\).

The same result can be seen without matrices. At \(h=0\) the bond variables \(\tau_i = s_is_{i+1}\) of an open chain are independent, each bond being broken (\(\tau_i=-1\)) with probability \(p = e^{-K}/(e^K+e^{-K})\). Since \(s_0s_r = \tau_0\tau_1\cdots\tau_{r-1}\), we get \(\langle s_0s_r\rangle = \langle\tau\rangle^r = (1-2p)^r = (\tanh K)^r\). Each broken bond, a domain wall, flips the sign of the correlation. At low \(T\), \(p\approx e^{-2K}\) and \(\xi\approx \frac12e^{2K}\): the walls are rare but never absent, and \(\xi\) diverges only at \(T=0\).

The 1D chain thus teaches two lessons that carry over to 2D unchanged. For \(N\to\infty\) only the largest eigenvalue matters, \(\ln Z \approx N\ln\lambda_{\max}\), and the correlation length is set by the ratio of the two largest eigenvalues, \(\xi = 1/\ln(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_2)\). What changes in 2D is the size of the matrix. A phase transition would require \(\lambda_2/\lambda_{\max}\to1\), so that \(\xi\to\infty\), at some \(T>0\), and a \(2\times2\) matrix with positive entries never allows this: by the Perron–Frobenius theorem its largest eigenvalue is strictly larger than the other in absolute value.

3. The transfer matrix in two dimensions

The 1D method extends to 2D once we let a whole column of spins play the role of a single spin. The price is the size of the matrix, \(2^L\times2^L\). For small widths \(L\) we can still diagonalise it numerically. This gives exact results for infinitely long strips, shows how a transition builds up as \(L\) grows, and sets the stage for Onsager, who found the largest eigenvalue for every \(L\) analytically.

Consider a strip of \(L\) rows, periodic in the vertical direction (\(s_{L+1}\equiv s_1\)), and \(L'\) columns, also periodic, with \(L'\to\infty\) at the end. A column \(\sigma = (s_1,\dots,s_L)\) has \(2^L\) states. The energy splits into bonds inside a column (vertical) and bonds between neighbouring columns (horizontal): \[ -\beta H = \sum_{j=1}^{L'}\Big[K\sum_{i=1}^L s_i^{(j)}s_i^{(j+1)} + K\sum_{i=1}^L s_i^{(j)}s_{i+1}^{(j)}\Big], \] where \(s_i^{(j)}\) is the spin in row \(i\) of column \(j\). Giving half of each column’s internal energy to the factor on its left and half to the one on its right, we obtain \[ \begin{aligned} Z &= \sum_{\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{L'}}\mathsf V_{\sigma_1\sigma_2}\mathsf V_{\sigma_2\sigma_3}\cdots\mathsf V_{\sigma_{L'}\sigma_1} = \operatorname{Tr}\mathsf V^{L'},\\ \mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'} &= \exp\Big[K\sum_{i=1}^L s_is_i' + \frac K2\sum_{i=1}^L\big(s_is_{i+1} + s'_is'_{i+1}\big)\Big]. \end{aligned} \] An element \(\mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'}\) is the Boltzmann weight of placing column \(\sigma'\) to the right of column \(\sigma\): it contains the \(L\) horizontal bonds between them and half of the vertical bonds of each. The half-and-half split makes \(\mathsf V\) symmetric, \(\mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'}=\mathsf V_{\sigma'\sigma}\).

The sketch below shows a strip of \(L=4\) rows. The two framed columns are the states \(\sigma\) and \(\sigma'\) of neighbouring columns. The matrix element \(\mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'}\) collects the \(L\) horizontal bonds between them (blue) and half of the vertical bonds inside each of them (green). The dashed vertical bonds lead to the faded copy of row 1 and close each column periodically. Multiplying \(\mathsf V\) from column to column and closing the strip in the horizontal direction gives \(Z=\operatorname{Tr}\mathsf V^{L'}\), just as for the ring.

For example, take \(L=2\). Two rows on a periodic column are joined by two vertical bonds, so \(\sum_is_is_{i+1}=2s_1s_2\). For \(\sigma=\sigma'=(+,+)\) we find \(\mathsf V = \exp[2K + \tfrac K2(2+2)] = e^{4K}\), and for \(\sigma=(+,+)\), \(\sigma'=(-,-)\) we find \(\mathsf V = \exp[-2K + 2K] = 1\).

As in 1D, \(Z=\sum_k\lambda_k^{L'}\) over the eigenvalues of \(\mathsf V\). All entries of \(\mathsf V\) are positive, so by the Perron–Frobenius theorem the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_0\) is positive, non-degenerate, and strictly larger than all others in absolute value. For \(L'\to\infty\), \(\ln Z \approx L'\ln\lambda_0\), and dividing by the number of spins \(LL'\) gives the free energy and correlation length of the strip, \[ f_L(T) = -\frac{T}{L}\ln\lambda_0(L),\qquad \xi_L = \frac{1}{\ln(\lambda_0/\lambda_1)}, \] where \(\xi_L\) is the correlation length along the strip and \(\lambda_1\) is the largest eigenvalue that couples to a single spin, defined precisely below. Onsager’s free energy is \(f = \lim_{L\to\infty}f_L\). The cost is that \(\mathsf V\) has \(4^L\) entries: \(L=12\) needs \(1.7\times10^7\) numbers, and \(L=50\) would need \(10^{30}\).

The global spin flip helps to organise the spectrum. Flipping all spins, \(\sigma\to-\sigma\), does not change \(\mathsf V\); in our bit encoding the flip maps the index \(n\to \bar n = 2^L-1-n\). Vectors with \(v_{\bar n}=v_n\) (even) and with \(v_{\bar n}=-v_n\) (odd) are mapped by \(\mathsf V\) into vectors of the same type, so \(\mathsf V\) splits into an even block (\(\mathsf V_{nm}+\mathsf V_{n\bar m}\)) and an odd block (\(\mathsf V_{nm}-\mathsf V_{n\bar m}\)), each of size \(2^{L-1}\), with \(n,m<2^{L-1}\). The eigenvalue \(\lambda_0\), whose eigenvector is positive, is the top of the even block. A single spin \(s_i\) changes sign under the flip, so it connects the even sector to the odd one, and the correlation \(\langle s_i^{(0)}s_i^{(r)}\rangle\) decays as \((\lambda_1/\lambda_0)^r\) with \(\lambda_1\) the top of the odd block — exactly as \(\lambda_-\) did in 1D.

def transfer_matrix(L, K, lattice="square"):
    # V[sigma, sigma'] for a periodic column of L spins; 'triangular' adds the diagonal bonds s_i s'_{i+1} (Section 10)
    S = all_configs(L).astype(float)
    intra = (S * np.roll(S, -1, axis=1)).sum(1)                 # sum_i s_i s_{i+1} inside a column
    inter = S @ S.T                                             # sum_i s_i s'_i
    if lattice == "triangular":
        inter = inter + S @ np.roll(S, -1, axis=1).T            # sum_i s_i s'_{i+1}
    return np.exp(K * (inter + 0.5 * (intra[:, None] + intra[None, :])))

    n = A.shape[0]

def f_onsager(T):
    # Onsager 1944: -beta f = ln(2 cosh 2K) + (1/2pi) int_0^pi ln[(1 + sqrt(1 - kappa^2 sin^2 phi))/2] dphi
    K = 1.0 / T
    k = 2 * np.sinh(2 * K) / np.cosh(2 * K)**2
    g = lambda p: np.log(0.5 * (1 + np.sqrt(max(0.0, 1 - k**2 * np.sin(p)**2))))
    with warnings.catch_warnings():                 # at T = T_c the integrand has a kink; quad warns but the result is accurate
        warnings.simplefilter("ignore", IntegrationWarning)
        I, _ = quad(g, 0, np.pi, points=[np.pi / 2], limit=200, epsabs=1e-13, epsrel=1e-13)
    return -T * (np.log(2 * np.cosh(2 * K)) + I / (2 * np.pi))

3.3 One and two dimensions

The strips let us see directly why 2D differs from 1D. The 1D chain has \(\xi = -1/\ln\tanh K\), finite at every \(T>0\), and its specific heat \(K^2/\cosh^2K\) is one smooth bump. In 2D the strip correlation lengths grow without bound below \(T_c\), and above \(T_c\) they converge to the exact 2D correlation length along a lattice axis, \(\xi = 1/[2(K^*-K)]\), with \(K^*\) the dual coupling defined by \(e^{-2K^*}=\tanh K\) (the formula is derived and checked in Sections 5 and 6.1). This \(\xi\) diverges at \(T_c\), where \(K^*=K\).

A simple balance of energy against entropy explains the difference. Start from an open 1D chain of \(N\) spins, all up. Breaking one bond, that is inserting one domain wall, costs energy \(2J\), and the wall can sit at any of \(N-1\) positions, which is an entropy \(\ln(N-1)\). The free-energy change \(\Delta F = 2J - T\ln(N-1)\) is negative once \(N-1>e^{2J/T}\), so at any \(T>0\) a long enough chain breaks into domains, of typical length \(\sim e^{2K}\), in line with \(\xi\approx\frac12e^{2K}\) from Section 2.1.

In 2D a domain of flipped spins is bounded by a closed wall, and a wall of length \(\ell\) (number of broken bonds) costs \(2J\ell\) — an energy proportional to its length, not a constant. The number of closed walls of length \(\ell\) through a given bond is at most the number of lattice paths of \(\ell\) steps that never immediately step back, \(4\cdot3^{\ell-1}\). The entropy is therefore at most about \(\ell\ln3\), and \(\Delta F\gtrsim\ell\,(2J-T\ln3)\), which is positive for \(T<2J/\ln3\approx1.82\). Large flipped domains are suppressed and the order survives at low \(T\). This is the idea of Peierls’ argument [Peierls 1936; rigorous form: Griffiths 1964]; it gives a lower bound on the ordering temperature, consistent with \(T_c\approx2.269>1.82\).

4. High- and low-temperature expansions of \(Z\)

Before Onsager, the 2D model was studied through expansions of \(Z\) that are accurate far from \(T_c\), in powers of a small parameter at high \(T\) and at low \(T\). For us their value is structural: the two series turn out to count the same geometric objects, and this coincidence (Section 5) pins down \(T_c\) exactly.

4.1 High-temperature expansion and closed graphs

At high \(T\) the coupling \(K\) is small. Since \(ss'=\pm1\) and \(e^{\pm K}=\cosh K\pm\sinh K\), \[ e^{Kss'} = \cosh K + ss'\sinh K = \cosh K\,(1 + t\,ss'),\qquad t = \tanh K . \] Inserting this for every one of the \(2N\) bonds gives \[ Z = (\cosh K)^{2N}\sum_{\mathbf s}\prod_{\langle i,j\rangle}\big(1 + t\,s_is_j\big). \] To expand the product we pick from each bond factor either \(1\) or \(t\,s_is_j\). A choice is a subset \(G\) of bonds, those where \(t\,s_is_j\) was picked, and it contributes \(t^{|G|}\prod_{\langle i,j\rangle\in G}s_is_j = t^{|G|}\prod_i s_i^{d_i(G)}\), where \(d_i(G)\) is the number of bonds of \(G\) touching site \(i\). Summing over the spins now removes most subsets: \(\sum_{s=\pm1}s^d\) equals \(2\) for even \(d\) and \(0\) for odd \(d\), so a subset survives only if every site has an even number of its bonds, that is, if \(G\) is a closed graph (a union of closed loops). Hence \[ Z = 2^N(\cosh K)^{2N}\sum_{\text{closed } G}t^{|G|} = 2^N(\cosh K)^{2N}\big(1 + N t^4 + 2N t^6 + \dots\big). \] The smallest closed graph is the boundary of one plaquette (4 bonds; there are \(N\) plaquettes), and the next is the boundary of a \(1\times2\) rectangle (6 bonds; \(N\) horizontal and \(N\) vertical ones).

4.2 Low-temperature expansion and domain walls

At low \(T\) almost all spins are parallel. Starting from all spins up, \(E_0 = -2N\), a configuration with \(b\) broken bonds has \(E=-2N+2b\) (Section 1.1) and hence the weight \(e^{2NK}x^{b}\) with \(x = e^{-2K}\), small at low \(T\). Draw a short segment across each broken bond. These segments live on the dual lattice, whose sites are the centres of the plaquettes. Going around a plaquette the spin returns to its starting value, so it changes sign an even number of times, and every dual site touches an even number of segments. The broken bonds therefore form closed graphs on the dual lattice — the domain walls. Each wall pattern comes from exactly two spin configurations, \(\mathbf s\) and \(-\mathbf s\), so \[ Z = 2e^{2NK}\sum_{\text{closed walls } W}x^{|W|} = 2e^{2NK}\big(1 + N x^4 + 2N x^6 + \dots\big): \] one flipped spin breaks 4 bonds (\(N\) ways), and a flipped neighbouring pair breaks 6 bonds (\(2N\) ways).

5. Kramers–Wannier duality and the self-dual point

The coincidence of the two series relates the free energy at a high temperature to the free energy at a low temperature. Kramers and Wannier [1941] used it to find \(T_c\) exactly, three years before Onsager’s full solution. It is the most economical route to \(T_c\), and it explains why the modulus \(\kappa\) of Onsager’s formula takes the same value at two different temperatures.

Let \(g(y) = \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\ln\sum_G y^{|G|}\), the sum running over closed graphs. Both expansions of Section 4 contain this same function, evaluated at two different arguments: \[ \varphi(K) \equiv \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\ln Z(K)}{N} = \ln 2 + 2\ln\cosh K + g(\tanh K) = 2K + g\big(e^{-2K}\big). \] To compare them we need a coupling at which the low-temperature argument equals the high-temperature one. Define the dual coupling \(K^*\) by \(e^{-2K^*} = \tanh K\). The low-temperature form at \(K^*\) reads \(\varphi(K^*) = 2K^* + g(e^{-2K^*}) = 2K^* + g(\tanh K)\), and subtracting the high-temperature form at \(K\) eliminates the unknown \(g\): \[ \boxed{\ \varphi(K) - \ln2 - 2\ln\cosh K = \varphi(K^*) - 2K^*\ }. \] The free energy at \(K\) is thus fixed by the free energy at \(K^*\) plus elementary analytic functions.

The map \(K\mapsto K^*\) has simple properties. With \(t=\tanh K = e^{-2K^*}\), \[ \begin{aligned} \sinh 2K^* &= \frac{e^{2K^*}-e^{-2K^*}}{2} = \frac{1/t - t}{2} = \frac{1-t^2}{2t},\\ \sinh 2K &= \frac{2\tanh K}{1-\tanh^2K} = \frac{2t}{1-t^2}\\ \Rightarrow\quad \sinh2K\,\sinh2K^* &= 1 . \end{aligned} \] This relation is symmetric in \(K\) and \(K^*\), so the map is an involution, \(K^{**}=K\), and equivalently \(\tanh K^* = e^{-2K}\). It is also decreasing: a small \(K\) (hot) maps to a large \(K^*\) (cold), and vice versa.

Now comes the argument for \(T_c\). The functions \(\ln 2\), \(2\ln\cosh K\) and \(2K^*\) are analytic for \(K>0\), so if \(\varphi\) is non-analytic at some \(K_c\), the boxed identity makes it non-analytic at \(K_c^*\) as well. If the model has a single transition, the two points must coincide, \(K_c = K_c^*\): \[ \sinh^2 2K_c = 1\ \Rightarrow\ \sinh 2K_c = 1\ \Rightarrow\ e^{2K_c}-e^{-2K_c} = 2 . \] With \(y=e^{2K_c}\) this is \(y^2-2y-1=0\), whose positive root is \(y = 1+\sqrt2\). Hence \[ K_c = \tfrac12\ln(1+\sqrt2)\approx0.4407,\qquad T_c = \frac{2}{\ln(1+\sqrt2)}\approx2.2692 . \] The assumption of a single transition is the only gap in the argument, and Onsager’s solution closes it.

Duality also explains a property of Onsager’s formula. With \(s=\sinh2K\) we have \(\cosh^22K = 1+s^2\) and \(\kappa = 2s/(1+s^2)\). Replacing \(s\) by \(s^*=1/s\) gives \(\kappa^* = \frac{2/s}{1+1/s^2} = \frac{2s}{s^2+1} = \kappa\), so the temperatures \(T\) and \(T^*\) give the same integrand in Onsager’s formula.

6. Onsager’s formula and its consequences

Section 3 showed that the free energy of the plane is \(\lim_{L\to\infty}-\frac{T}{L}\ln\lambda_0(L)\), but numerically we could reach only \(L\approx12\). Onsager found \(\lambda_0(L)\) in closed form for every \(L\) and took the limit. The result is \[ \begin{aligned} -\beta f &= \ln(2\cosh2K) + \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi d\phi\,\ln\Big[\tfrac12\Big(1+\sqrt{1-\kappa^2\sin^2\phi}\Big)\Big],\\ \kappa &= \frac{2\sinh2K}{\cosh^22K} . \end{aligned} \] Subsection 6.1 outlines the modern derivation, in which the transfer matrix becomes a problem of free fermions [Kaufman 1949; Schultz, Mattis & Lieb 1964]. We state the key equation of each stage and verify each of them numerically; the full algebra is in Schultz, Mattis & Lieb (1964) and, in open access, in Gispen (2018). Subsections 6.2 and 6.3 then interpret the result and derive the energy, the specific heat and the magnetisation.

6.1 From spins to free fermions

The derivation begins by rewriting the transfer matrix with Pauli matrices. Let \(\sigma^x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(\sigma^z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\), and let \(\sigma^a_i\) act on the spin of row \(i\) (and as the identity on the other rows). For a single row, the horizontal-bond weight \(e^{Kss'}\) is the \(2\times2\) matrix \[ \begin{pmatrix}e^K&e^{-K}\\e^{-K}&e^K\end{pmatrix} = e^K\mathbb 1 + e^{-K}\sigma^x = \sqrt{2\sinh2K}\;e^{K^*\sigma^x}, \] with \(K^*\) the dual coupling of Section 5 (the identity is proved in Exercise 3). The vertical bonds inside a column are diagonal in the spin basis. Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} \mathsf V &= (2\sinh 2K)^{L/2}\;\mathsf V_2^{1/2}\,\mathsf V_1\,\mathsf V_2^{1/2},\\ \mathsf V_1 &= \exp\Big(K^*\sum_{i=1}^L\sigma^x_i\Big),\qquad \mathsf V_2 = \exp\Big(K\sum_{i=1}^L\sigma^z_i\sigma^z_{i+1}\Big). \end{aligned} \] \(\mathsf V_2\) weighs the bonds within a column, and \(\mathsf V_1\) flips spins from one column to the next. The exponents are those of a quantum spin chain — an Ising coupling \(\sigma^z_i\sigma^z_{i+1}\) plus a transverse field \(\sigma^x_i\) — so the classical 2D model is equivalent to a quantum chain in 1D. The difficulty is that \(\sigma^x_i\) and \(\sigma^z_i\) do not commute, so \(\mathsf V_1\) and \(\mathsf V_2\) cannot be combined or diagonalised together.

The way out is the Jordan–Wigner transformation, which turns spins into fermions. First rename the axes, \(\sigma^x\to-\sigma^z\), \(\sigma^z\to\sigma^x\), a unitary change of basis that leaves the eigenvalues unchanged, so that \(\mathsf V_1=\exp(-K^*\sum_j\sigma^z_j)\) and \(\mathsf V_2=\exp(K\sum_j\sigma^x_j\sigma^x_{j+1})\). With \(\sigma^+=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\sigma^-=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) define \[ \psi_j = \Big(\prod_{k<j}(-\sigma^z_k)\Big)\,\sigma^-_j . \] The “string” \(\prod_{k<j}(-\sigma^z_k)\) makes operators on different sites anticommute: \(\{\psi_i,\psi_j^\dagger\}=\delta_{ij}\) and \(\{\psi_i,\psi_j\}=0\), where \(\{A,B\}=AB+BA\). These are the relations of fermion annihilation and creation operators, and \(n_j=\psi_j^\dagger\psi_j\), with eigenvalues 0 and 1, counts the fermion at site \(j\). The spin operators become \[ \begin{aligned} \sigma^z_j &= 2n_j-1,\\ \sigma^x_j\sigma^x_{j+1} &= (\psi_j^\dagger-\psi_j)(\psi_{j+1}^\dagger+\psi_{j+1})\quad(j<L),\\ \sigma^x_L\sigma^x_1 &= -(-1)^F(\psi_L^\dagger-\psi_L)(\psi_1^\dagger+\psi_1), \end{aligned} \] with \(F=\sum_jn_j\) the number of fermions and \((-1)^F=\prod_j(-\sigma^z_j)\). Both exponents are now quadratic in the fermion operators, which means that the fermions do not interact. The operator \((-1)^F\) is the global spin flip of Section 3 in the new basis, so the even sector has even \(F\); there the boundary term carries a minus sign, as if \(\psi_{L+1}=-\psi_1\) (antiperiodic boundary conditions), while the odd sector is periodic.

Non-interacting particles on a translation-invariant chain are diagonalised by plane waves. We set \(\psi_q\propto\sum_je^{-iqj}\psi_j\), with momenta \[ \begin{aligned} q\in Q_{\rm even} &= \Big\{\pm\tfrac{\pi}{L},\pm\tfrac{3\pi}{L},\dots,\pm\tfrac{(L-1)\pi}{L}\Big\}\ \text{(antiperiodic)},\\ q\in Q_{\rm odd} &= \Big\{0,\pm\tfrac{2\pi}{L},\dots,\pm\tfrac{(L-2)\pi}{L},\pi\Big\}\ \text{(periodic)} . \end{aligned} \] The terms \(\psi\psi\) and \(\psi^\dagger\psi^\dagger\) in \(\sigma^x\sigma^x\) couple momentum \(q\) only to \(-q\), so \(\mathsf V\) factorises into commuting pieces \(\mathsf V_q\), one per pair \((q,-q)\) [Schultz, Mattis & Lieb 1964; Gispen 2018, Theorem 4.5.1]. On the two states “pair empty” and “pair occupied”, \(|0\rangle\) and \(\psi_q^\dagger\psi_{-q}^\dagger|0\rangle\), \[ \mathsf V_q = \begin{pmatrix}e^{2K^*}&0\\0&e^{-2K^*}\end{pmatrix}\exp\left[2K\begin{pmatrix}0&\sin q\\ \sin q&2\cos q\end{pmatrix}\right], \qquad \text{eigenvalues } e^{2K\cos q\pm\gamma_q}, \] \[ \cosh\gamma_q = \cosh2K^*\cosh2K - \sinh2K^*\sinh2K\cos q . \] The two singly occupied states \(\psi^\dagger_{\pm q}|0\rangle\) have the intermediate eigenvalue \(e^{2K\cos q}\). A problem of size \(2^L\times2^L\) has become \(L/2\) problems of size \(2\times2\).

The largest eigenvalue is now obtained by taking the larger eigenvalue in each block. Over \(Q_{\rm even}\) the values \(\cos q\) cancel in pairs (\(q\) and \(\pi-q\)), so \[ \begin{aligned} \lambda_0 &= (2\sinh2K)^{L/2}\exp\Big(\frac12\sum_{q\in Q_{\rm even}}\gamma_q\Big),\\ \lambda_1 &= (2\sinh2K)^{L/2}\exp\Big(\frac12\sum_{q\in Q_{\rm odd}}\gamma_q\Big),\quad \gamma_0\equiv2(K-K^*)\ \text{(with its sign)}. \end{aligned} \] These are the eigenvalues found by Kaufman (1949).

Finally we let the strip become infinitely wide. The momenta are spaced by \(2\pi/L\), so \(\frac1L\sum_q\to\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}dq\) and \[ \begin{aligned} -\beta f &= \lim_{L\to\infty}\frac1L\ln\lambda_0\\ &= \frac12\ln(2\sinh2K) + \frac1{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\gamma(q)\,dq . \end{aligned} \] With the identity \(\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\ln(2\cosh\gamma-2\cos\theta)\,d\theta=\gamma\) and with \(\cosh2K^*=\cosh2K/\sinh2K\), \(\sinh2K^*=1/\sinh2K\), this becomes the symmetric double integral \[ \begin{aligned} -\beta f &= \ln2\\ &\quad + \frac{1}{8\pi^2}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta_1\,d\theta_2\,\ln\big[\cosh^22K - \sinh2K\,(\cos\theta_1+\cos\theta_2)\big], \end{aligned} \] and performing one of the two integrals gives the single-integral form with \(\kappa\) quoted at the top of this section [Onsager 1944].

6.2 The origin of the singularity

Using \(\sinh2K\sinh2K^*=1\), the dispersion relation simplifies exactly: \[ \cosh\gamma_q = \cosh2K^*\cosh2K - \sinh2K^*\sinh2K + \sinh2K^*\sinh2K\,(1-\cos q) = \cosh\big(2(K-K^*)\big) + 1-\cos q . \] The function \(\gamma_q\ge0\) is smallest at \(q=0\), where \(\gamma_0 = 2|K-K^*|\). This minimum, the gap, vanishes only at \(K=K^*\), the self-dual point of Section 5. For small \(q\) and \(K\) near \(K_c\), \(\cosh\gamma\approx1+\gamma^2/2\) gives \[ \gamma_q\approx\sqrt{m^2+q^2},\qquad m = 2(K-K^*)\propto T_c-T , \] the energy–momentum relation of a relativistic particle of mass \(m\); at \(T_c\) the fermions become massless.

The gap controls the correlation length. \(\ln(\lambda_0/\lambda_1)\) is the difference of the two sums for \(\lambda_0\) and \(\lambda_1\). Above \(T_c\) it tends to the gap, so that \(\xi = 1/[2(K^*-K)]\) along a lattice axis, which diverges as \(1/(T-T_c)\) (exponent \(\nu=1\), Section 8). Below \(T_c\), \(\gamma_0=2(K-K^*)>0\) and \(\lambda_1/\lambda_0\to1\) as \(L\to\infty\): the even and odd sectors become degenerate, which is the signature of two ordered states (all up, all down).

The gap also produces the singularity of the free energy. \(-\beta f\) contains \(\frac1{4\pi}\int\gamma(q)\,dq\), and near \(q=0\) \[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^\Lambda\sqrt{m^2+q^2}\,dq &= \frac{\Lambda}{2}\sqrt{\Lambda^2+m^2}\\ &\quad + \frac{m^2}{2}\ln\frac{\Lambda+\sqrt{\Lambda^2+m^2}}{|m|} \end{aligned} \] for any cutoff \(\Lambda>0\). The term \(-\frac{m^2}{2}\ln|m|\) is not analytic at \(m=0\): \(f\) and its first derivative are continuous, but the second derivative contains \(\ln|m|\). Since \(m\propto T-T_c\) and \(c=-T\,\partial^2f/\partial T^2\), the specific heat diverges as \(\ln|T-T_c|\) (compare Peskin’s notes, eqs. 63–67).

In Onsager’s variables the same non-analyticity appears differently. In the single-integral form, \(\kappa = 2s/(1+s^2)\) with \(s = \sinh 2K\). Since \(2s\le1+s^2\), \(\kappa\le1\), with equality only at \(s = 1\), that is at \(T_c\). There the square root \(\sqrt{1-\kappa^2\sin^2\phi}\) vanishes at \(\phi = \pi/2\) and the integrand develops a kink.

6.3 Energy, specific heat and magnetisation

The energy per spin, \(u = \partial(\beta f)/\partial\beta\), can be computed in two independent ways, and comparing them is a stringent test. The first is to differentiate the integral formula numerically, by central finite differences in \(\beta\). The second uses the closed formula [Onsager 1944] with the complete elliptic integral of the first kind \(K_1(\kappa) = \int_0^{\pi/2}d\phi/\sqrt{1-\kappa^2\sin^2\phi}\): \[ u = -\coth2K\Big[1 + \frac2\pi\big(2\tanh^22K - 1\big)K_1(\kappa)\Big]. \] \(K_1(\kappa)\) diverges logarithmically as \(\kappa\to1\), and this is the origin of the vertical tangent of \(u\) at \(T_c\). scipy.special.ellipkm1(p) returns \(K_1\) as a function of \(p = 1-\kappa^2\). Near \(T_c\), \(\kappa^2\) is so close to 1 that \(1-\kappa^2\) must be computed without cancellation, as \(1-\kappa^2 = \big(\frac{1-s^2}{1+s^2}\big)^2\) with \(s = \sinh 2K\).

The free energy at \(h=0\) does not give the spontaneous magnetisation, which needs a derivative with respect to \(h\). Onsager announced the formula in 1949 without publishing a derivation, and Yang [1952] published the first derivation [history: Baxter 2011]: \[ M = \big[1-\sinh^{-4}2K\big]^{1/8}\quad (T<T_c),\qquad M=0\quad(T>T_c). \] Near \(T_c\), \(\sinh2K\approx1+2\sqrt2\,(K-K_c)\) (using \(\cosh2K_c=\sqrt2\)), so \(1-\sinh^{-4}2K\approx8\sqrt2\,(K-K_c)\propto T_c-T\) and \(M\propto(T_c-T)^{1/8}\): the exponent is \(\beta=1/8\).

The next cell compares the two routes to \(u\). They agree to between \(10^{-9}\) and \(10^{-7}\), the accuracy of the finite difference.

Code 
def u_elliptic(T):
    K = 1.0 / np.asarray(T, dtype=float)
    s = np.sinh(2 * K)
    p = ((1 - s**2) / (1 + s**2))**2                 # 1 - kappa^2, without cancellation
    return -1 / np.tanh(2 * K) * (1 + 2 / np.pi * (2 * np.tanh(2 * K)**2 - 1) * ellipkm1(p))

def M_yang(T):

    out = np.zeros_like(T)

    out[below] = (1 - np.sinh(2 / T[below])**-4) ** 0.125
    return out

7. The energy at the critical point

The value of \(u\) at \(T_c\) is a good test of the whole construction: we can obtain it once from Onsager’s solution and once from duality alone, without the solution. The comparison also shows how much information a symmetry carries.

7.1 The critical energy from Onsager’s solution

At \(T_c\) we have \(\sinh 2K_c = 1\), hence \(\cosh^2 2K_c = 1+\sinh^22K_c = 2\) and \(\tanh^22K_c = \tfrac12\), so the prefactor \(2\tanh^22K_c - 1\) vanishes. 7.2 The critical energy from duality alone

The same number can be obtained without Onsager’s formula. Since \(\varphi = \ln Z/N\) and \(\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta = -\langle H\rangle\), with \(J=1\) we have \(K=\beta\) and \[ \varphi'(K) = -u(K) \equiv \varepsilon(K), \] where \(\varepsilon = 2\langle s_is_j\rangle_{\rm nn}\) is twice the nearest-neighbour correlation (two bonds per spin). We differentiate the duality identity \(\ \varphi(K) - \ln2 - 2\ln\cosh K = \varphi(K^*) - 2K^*\ \) with respect to \(K\), using the chain rule on the right: \[ \varepsilon(K) - 2\tanh K = \big(\varepsilon(K^*) - 2\big)\frac{dK^*}{dK}. \] The derivative of the dual map follows from \(e^{-2K^*} = \tanh K\): \(\ -2e^{-2K^*}dK^* = \frac{dK}{\cosh^2K}\), so \(\frac{dK^*}{dK} = -\frac{1}{2\tanh K\cosh^2K} = -\frac{1}{2\sinh K\cosh K} = -\frac{1}{\sinh 2K}\), which equals \(-1\) at \(K_c\).

At the self-dual point \(K = K^* = K_c\) both sides involve the same \(\varepsilon\), provided \(u\) is continuous there — no latent heat, as Section 6 showed. Then \[ \varepsilon - 2\tanh K_c = -(\varepsilon - 2)\ \Rightarrow\ \varepsilon(K_c) = 1 + \tanh K_c . \] Finally \(\tanh K_c = e^{-2K_c}\) by self-duality, and this equals \(1/(1+\sqrt2) = \sqrt2 - 1\), so \(\varepsilon(K_c) = \sqrt2\) and \(u(T_c) = -\sqrt2\). Two exact results, \(T_c\) and \(u(T_c)\), thus follow from a symmetry argument alone. In the next two cells SymPy solves the equation at the self-dual point, and the numerical check confirms \(dK^*/dK = -1\) at \(K_c\), the value \(\sqrt2-1\), and the differentiated identity also away from \(K_c\), where both sides agree to all printed digits.

8. Critical exponents

The critical temperature depends on the lattice; Section 10 will show a different value for the triangular lattice. What does not depend on such details is the way quantities diverge or vanish near \(T_c\), described by the critical exponents. They are the numbers that characterise a phase transition, and they are what the exact solution contributes beyond \(T_c\). With the reduced temperature \(t = (T-T_c)/T_c\) they are defined by \[ \begin{aligned} c&\sim|t|^{-\alpha},\qquad M\sim(-t)^{\beta}\ (t<0),\qquad \chi\sim|t|^{-\gamma},\qquad \xi\sim|t|^{-\nu},\\ \langle s_0s_r\rangle&\sim r^{-(d-2+\eta)}\ \text{at } T_c , \end{aligned} \] where \(\chi=\partial m/\partial h\) is the magnetic susceptibility and \(d\) the dimension (\(d=2\) here, so \(\langle s_0s_r\rangle\sim r^{-\eta}\) at \(T_c\)). The symbol \(\beta\) here denotes an exponent, not \(1/T\); the context makes the meaning clear. The 2D Ising model realises the case \(\alpha=0\) with a logarithm, \(c\sim-\ln|t|\).

For the magnetisation we fit \(\ln M\) against \(\ln(-t)\) close to \(T_c\) and also look at the local slope \(d\ln M/d\ln(-t)\), which must tend to \(\beta\). For the specific heat we fit \(c = -A\ln|t| + B_\pm\) on both sides of \(T_c\) and test the amplitude predicted by Onsager’s solution, \(A = \frac{2}{\pi}\big(\frac{2}{T_c}\big)^2 = \frac{2}{\pi}(2K_c)^2\) [Onsager 1944]. Here \(c\) is obtained from the elliptic \(u\) by a central difference with a step much smaller than \(|T-T_c|\).

8.1 Finite-size scaling of strips

The strips of Section 3 give two more exponents, independently of Onsager’s formula. Both rest on the idea of finite-size scaling: near \(T_c\) the only relevant lengths are \(\xi\) and the strip width \(L\), so dimensionless quantities depend only on their ratio.

At \(T_c\) the strip correlation length is proportional to the width, and conformal invariance of the critical point fixes the constant: \(\xi_L = L/(\pi\eta)\) on a periodic strip [Cardy 1984]. The quantity \(\eta_L = L/(\pi\,\xi_L(T_c))\) should therefore tend to \(\eta\), whose exact value is \(\eta = 1/4\). Near \(T_c\), \(\xi_L/L\) is a function of \(L/\xi\propto L\,|t|^{\nu}\), that is of \(L^{1/\nu}t\) only, so its slope at \(T_c\) grows as \(\frac{d(\xi_L/L)}{dT}\big|_{T_c}\propto L^{1/\nu}\); the exact value is \(\nu = 1\) (Section 6.2: \(\xi\propto1/(T-T_c)\)). The free energy at \(T_c\) carries a third universal number. It converges as \(f_L - f = -\frac{\pi\,c_{\rm CFT}\,T_c}{6L^2}\) [Blöte, Cardy & Nightingale 1986; Affleck 1986], where \(c_{\rm CFT}\) is the central charge of the conformal field theory (CFT) describing the critical point. For the Ising model \(c_{\rm CFT} = \frac12\), so \(L^2(f_L - f)\to-\pi T_c/12\). The cell below computes all three quantities for \(L = 4,\dots,12\).

These exponents complete the phase diagram of the model, drawn below. A phase diagram is a map of the control parameters, here the temperature \(T\) and the magnetic field \(h\) of Section 1, divided into regions with qualitatively different behaviour, the phases, and the lines between them, the phase transitions.

The type of a transition is read off from the free energy per spin \(f(T,h)\) and its derivatives, such as the magnetisation \(m=-\partial f/\partial h\) and the energy. 9. Mean-field theory and the exact solution

Mean-field theory is the approximation one uses when no exact solution exists. With the exact solution at hand we can measure precisely what it gets wrong, and why.

Spin \(i\) interacts with its \(z\) neighbours (\(z=4\) on the square lattice) through \(-Js_i\sum_{j\in{\rm nn}(i)}s_j\). Mean-field theory replaces each neighbour by its average \(m=\langle s_j\rangle\), so that spin \(i\) feels an effective field \(zJm\); a single spin in a field \(B\) has \(\langle s\rangle = \tanh(\beta B)\). Self-consistency, \(\langle s_i\rangle = m\), then gives with \(J=1\) \[ m = \tanh\Big(\frac{zm}{T}\Big) = \tanh\Big(\frac{4m}{T}\Big). \] The value \(m = 0\) is always a solution. A non-zero one exists when the slope of the right-hand side at \(m = 0\) exceeds 1, i.e. \(4/T > 1\), so \(T_c^{\rm MF} = z = 4\). Near \(T_c^{\rm MF}\), \(\tanh x\approx x - x^3/3\) gives \(m \approx \frac4Tm - \frac13\big(\frac4T\big)^3m^3\), i.e. \(m^2\approx 3\big(\frac4T-1\big)\big/\big(\frac4T\big)^3\), so \(m\sim(1 - T/T_c^{\rm MF})^{1/2}\) and \(\beta_{\rm MF} = \frac12\). For the energy, replacing \(s_is_j\) by \(m^2\) in each of the \(zN/2\) bonds gives \(u_{\rm MF} = -\frac z2m^2 = -2m^2\). It is zero above \(T_c^{\rm MF}\) and has a finite slope below, which produces a jump in \(c\) instead of a divergence.

The replacement assumes \(\langle s_is_j\rangle = \langle s_i\rangle\langle s_j\rangle\), that is, no correlations. Near \(T_c\) the correlation length diverges and correlated fluctuations dominate, so the approximation must fail there. In 1D (\(z=2\)) it even predicts a transition at \(T=2\), where the exact chain has none (Section 2). Mean-field exponents become exact only in high dimensions, above the upper critical dimension \(d=4\) of the Ising model [Kardar 2007]. 10. Universality and the triangular lattice

Section 8 claimed that the exponents, unlike \(T_c\), do not depend on lattice details, and we now test this on a different lattice. Exponents depend only on the dimension, on the symmetry of the order parameter (here \(s\to-s\)) and on the range of the interactions [Kardar 2007]; models sharing these form a universality class.

Add one diagonal bond to every plaquette: each spin now has \(z = 6\) neighbours and the lattice becomes triangular. In the transfer matrix this is the extra term \(K\sum_is_is'_{i+1}\) (flag lattice="triangular"); \(\mathsf V\) is then no longer symmetric, so we use a general eigensolver. The exact critical temperature is \(T_c^{\triangle} = 4/\ln3\) [Houtappel 1950], and the exponents should be those of the square lattice. We test both with the crossing method of Section 3 and with \(\eta_L\) of Section 8.1.

One geometric detail must be taken into account. In the triangular lattice the columns are \(\sqrt3/2\) apart while the sites of one column are at distance 1, so the physical correlation length is \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\xi_L\) and \(\eta_L = L\big/\big(\pi\,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\xi_L\big)\).

A network trained on one lattice can transfer to another lattice only to the extent that it has learned something universal — the interpretability question raised in the lecture.

Exercise 3 (open) — the quantum connection

For the proof, note that \(e^{K^*\sigma^x} = \cosh K^*\,\mathbb1 + \sinh K^*\sigma^x\) because \((\sigma^x)^2=\mathbb 1\). The ratio of the two coefficients must be \(e^{-K}/e^{K}=e^{-2K}\), and indeed \(\tanh K^* = e^{-2K}\) (Section 5). For the prefactor, \(\cosh^2K^* = 1/(1-\tanh^2K^*) = 1/(1-e^{-4K}) = e^{2K}/(2\sinh2K)\), so \(\sqrt{2\sinh2K}\cosh K^* = e^{K}\). At the self-dual point \(K = K^* = K_c\) the column coupling and the transverse field are equal, which is the critical point of the quantum chain.
