Lab 012 — Onsager’s exact solution of the 2D Ising model

The model. Spins \(s_i=\pm1\) sit on the sites of a two-dimensional (2D) square lattice with \(N\) sites and periodic boundaries (a torus). Neighbouring spins prefer to be parallel: \[ H(\mathbf s) = -J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle} s_i s_j , \] where \(J>0\) is the coupling energy and \(\langle i,j\rangle\) runs over the \(2N\) nearest-neighbour bonds (each site has 4 neighbours, each bond is shared by 2 sites). At temperature \(T\) a configuration \(\mathbf s=(s_1,\dots,s_N)\) occurs with the Boltzmann probability \[ p(\mathbf s) = \frac{e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)}}{Z},\qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_BT},\qquad Z(T) = \sum_{\mathbf s\in\{\pm1\}^N} e^{-\beta H(\mathbf s)} , \] with \(k_B\) the Boltzmann constant. Low energy is favoured, and the more so the lower the temperature.

The object of interest: the partition function \(Z\). \(Z\) is the normalisation of \(p(\mathbf s)\), a sum over all \(2^N\) configurations. Section 1 shows that every thermodynamic quantity — energy, specific heat, magnetisation — is a derivative of \(\ln Z\), so knowing \(Z(T)\) is knowing everything. The central quantity is the free energy per spin \(f = -\frac{T}{N}\ln Z\) (with \(k_B=1\)) in the limit \(N\to\infty\): the energy is \(u = \partial(\beta f)/\partial\beta\), the specific heat \(c = \partial u/\partial T\), and a phase transition is a temperature at which \(f(T)\) is not an analytic function.

The task. For \(N = 100\) spins the sum already has \(2^{100}\approx1.3\times10^{30}\) terms, so \(Z\) cannot be computed term by term. To solve the model means to find \(f\) in closed form for \(N\to\infty\). Onsager did this for the square lattice [Onsager 1944]: \[ -\beta f = \ln(2\cosh2K) + \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi d\phi\,\ln\Big[\tfrac12\Big(1+\sqrt{1-\kappa^2\sin^2\phi}\Big)\Big], \qquad K = \beta J,\quad \kappa = \frac{2\sinh2K}{\cosh^22K}. \] The integrand is smooth except when \(\kappa = 1\), which happens at a single temperature, \(\sinh 2K_c = 1\), i.e. \(T_c = 2J/\big(k_B\ln(1+\sqrt2)\big) \approx 2.269\,J/k_B\).

Significance of the solution.

  • Phase transitions from a sum of smooth terms. Each Boltzmann weight \(e^{-\beta H}\) is an analytic function of \(T\), and for finite \(N\) so is \(\ln Z\) (Section 1). A sharp transition — a kink or a divergence in a thermodynamic quantity — can therefore exist only in the limit \(N\to\infty\). Peierls [1936] had argued that the 2D model orders at low temperature, and Kramers and Wannier [1941] located \(T_c\) by a symmetry argument; Onsager’s formula was the first exact free energy of a model with a phase transition, so the non-analyticity could be seen explicitly.
  • Exact critical exponents. Near \(T_c\) the exact solution gives a logarithmically divergent specific heat and, with Yang’s magnetisation [Yang 1952], \(M\propto(T_c-T)^{1/8}\). Mean-field theory, the standard approximation, predicts a finite jump in the specific heat and \(M\propto(T_c-T)^{1/2}\) (Section 9). This disagreement is a central motivation of the theory of scaling and of the renormalisation group [Kardar 2007].
  • A benchmark. Exact results are rare, so every numerical method for lattice models — Monte Carlo sampling, transfer matrices, and the neural networks of this course — is tested against Onsager’s solution. Concretely, \(T_c\approx2.269\) is the temperature that the convolutional neural network (CNN) of Lab 011 should locate from the Monte Carlo spin images of Lab 010.

1. The partition function and what solving the model means

Before computing anything we need to know what we are after. This section shows that all thermodynamics is contained in \(\ln Z\), defines a phase transition mathematically, and computes \(Z\) exactly for the smallest lattices. Its lesson — a finite lattice never has a transition — tells us what the later sections must achieve.

\(\ln Z\) generates the averages. Write \(E(\mathbf s)=H(\mathbf s)\) and \(\langle A\rangle = \sum_{\mathbf s}A(\mathbf s)\,p(\mathbf s)\) for the thermal average. Differentiating \(Z=\sum_{\mathbf s}e^{-\beta E(\mathbf s)}\) with respect to \(\beta\) brings down a factor \(-E\): \[ \frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta} = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\mathbf s}\big(-E(\mathbf s)\big)e^{-\beta E(\mathbf s)} = -\langle E\rangle,\qquad \frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} = \langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2 \;\ge 0 . \] (The second derivative is the derivative of \(-\langle E\rangle\); differentiating the ratio gives the variance.) With the free energy per spin \(f = -\frac{1}{N\beta}\ln Z\), the energy per spin and the specific heat per spin are \[ u = \frac{\langle E\rangle}{N} = \frac{\partial(\beta f)}{\partial\beta},\qquad c = \frac{\partial u}{\partial T} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial\beta}\frac{d\beta}{dT} = \frac{\langle E^2\rangle-\langle E\rangle^2}{NT^2}, \] using \(d\beta/dT=-1/T^2\). The specific heat is a variance: large \(c\) means large energy fluctuations. In the same way a magnetic field \(h\), added as \(-h\sum_is_i\) to \(H\), gives the magnetisation per spin \(m = \frac1N\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial(\beta h)} = \frac1N\big\langle\sum_is_i\big\rangle\).

Per-spin quantities and the limit \(N\to\infty\). \(\ln Z\) grows proportionally to \(N\) (it is extensive), so \(f\), \(u\), \(c\) have finite limits as \(N\to\infty\). These limits describe a macroscopic sample (\(N\sim10^{23}\)).

What a phase transition is. A phase transition is a temperature \(T_c\) at which \(f(T)\) is not analytic — some derivative of \(f\) jumps or diverges. For finite \(N\) this is impossible: \(Z(\beta)=\sum_E n_E e^{-\beta E}\) is a finite sum of exponentials, each analytic in \(\beta\), and \(Z>0\) for real \(\beta\), so \(\ln Z\) is analytic with all derivatives finite. A limit of analytic functions, however, need not be analytic. Example: \[ f_N(x) = \frac1N\ln\big(e^{Nx}+e^{-Nx}\big)\ \xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\ |x| , \] each \(f_N\) is smooth, the limit has a kink at \(x=0\), and \(0\le f_N(x)-|x|\le\frac{\ln 2}{N}\). A phase transition is this mechanism at work in \(\ln Z/N\). The cell below shows the example.

1.1 Brute force on a tiny lattice

On an \(L\times L\) torus there are \(2^{L^2}\) configurations. For \(L = 4\) that is \(65\,536\) — easy. We store every configuration as a row of \(\pm1\): the bits of the integer \(n\in\{0,\dots,2^N-1\}\) give the spins, \(s = 1-2\,\text{bit}\).

Counting broken bonds. A bond \(\langle i,j\rangle\) is satisfied if \(s_is_j=+1\) (energy \(-1\)) and broken if \(s_is_j=-1\) (energy \(+1\)). With \(b\) broken bonds out of \(2N\), the energy is \(E = -(2N-b) + b = -2N + 2b\). So \(E\) depends only on \(b\), and \(Z\) is fixed by the histogram \(n_b\) = number of configurations with \(b\) broken bonds (the density of states): \[ Z(T) = \sum_{b=0}^{2N} n_b\, e^{-(-2N+2b)/T}. \] Every configuration and its global flip \(\mathbf s\to-\mathbf s\) have the same \(b\), so every \(n_b\) is even. On the square-lattice torus \(b\) is also always even: the product of \(s_is_j\) over all \(2N\) bonds is \(\prod_i s_i^4=1\), so the number of factors equal to \(-1\) is even.

Code 
def all_configs(n):
    # all 2^n configurations of n spins, as rows of +-1 (bit k of the row index -> spin k)
    bits = (np.arange(2**n)[:, None] >> np.arange(n)) & 1
    return (1 - 2 * bits).astype(np.int8)

def torus_energies(L):
    # energies of all 2^(L*L) configurations of the L x L periodic square lattice (J = 1)
    S = all_configs(L * L).reshape(-1, L, L).astype(np.int32)
    return -(S * np.roll(S, 1, axis=1)).sum(axis=(1, 2)) - (S * np.roll(S, 1, axis=2)).sum(axis=(1, 2))

1.2 Thermodynamics of the finite lattice

With \(n_b\) in hand, \(\ln Z\), \(u\) and \(c\) follow from the formulas above, with the energy levels \(E_b=-2N+2b\): \[ \ln Z = \ln\sum_b n_b e^{-E_b/T},\qquad u = \frac{1}{N}\frac{\sum_b n_b E_b e^{-E_b/T}}{Z},\qquad c = \frac{\langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2}{NT^2}. \] The function thermo_from_dos evaluates these sums; to avoid overflow of \(e^{-E_b/T}\) it subtracts the largest exponent before exponentiating (the “log-sum-exp” trick). The next cell plots \(f\) and \(c\); the cell after it checks the fluctuation formula for \(c\) against a numerical derivative \(du/dT\).

Code 
def thermo_from_dos(nb, N, T):
    # ln Z / N, u and c per spin from the histogram n_b of broken bonds
    T = np.atleast_1d(T)[:, None]
    Eb = -2 * N + 2 * np.arange(len(nb))
    logw = np.log(np.where(nb > 0, nb, 1)) - Eb / T
    logw = np.where(nb > 0, logw, -np.inf)
    shift = logw.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)                  # avoid overflow
    w = np.exp(logw - shift); Zr = w.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True); p = w / Zr
    E1 = (p * Eb).sum(1); E2 = (p * Eb**2).sum(1)
    lnZ = (np.log(Zr) + shift)[:, 0]
    return lnZ / N, E1 / N, (E2 - E1**2) / (N * T[:, 0]**2)

2. The 1D chain and the transfer matrix

The 1D chain is the simplest place to learn the transfer-matrix method that Onsager used in 2D, and it gives a reference point: we will see that the chain has no phase transition, which is what makes the 2D result remarkable.

Setting. \(N\) spins on a ring, \(s_{N+1}\equiv s_1\), with coupling \(J=1\) and field \(h\): \[ H = -\sum_{i=1}^N s_is_{i+1} - h\sum_{i=1}^N s_i ,\qquad K = \beta,\quad \tilde h = \beta h . \]

Step 1 — the weight as a product of bond factors. Every site belongs to two bonds, so we can give half of its field term to each: \[ -\beta H = \sum_{i=1}^N\Big[Ks_is_{i+1} + \tfrac{\tilde h}{2}(s_i+s_{i+1})\Big] \quad\Rightarrow\quad e^{-\beta H} = \prod_{i=1}^N \mathsf T_{s_is_{i+1}},\qquad \mathsf T_{ss'} = e^{Kss' + \frac{\tilde h}{2}(s+s')} . \] \(\mathsf T_{ss'}\) is the Boltzmann weight of one bond. Labelling the rows and columns by \(s=+1\) (first) and \(s=-1\) (second), \[ \mathsf T = \begin{pmatrix} e^{K+\tilde h} & e^{-K}\\ e^{-K} & e^{K-\tilde h}\end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 2 — the sum over spins is matrix multiplication. The spin \(s_2\) appears only in \(\mathsf T_{s_1s_2}\mathsf T_{s_2s_3}\), and \(\sum_{s_2}\mathsf T_{s_1s_2}\mathsf T_{s_2s_3} = (\mathsf T^2)_{s_1s_3}\) is the definition of the matrix product. Summing \(s_2,\dots,s_N\) in turn and closing the ring: \[ Z = \sum_{s_1,\dots,s_N}\mathsf T_{s_1s_2}\mathsf T_{s_2s_3}\cdots\mathsf T_{s_Ns_1} = \sum_{s_1}(\mathsf T^N)_{s_1s_1} = \operatorname{Tr}\mathsf T^N . \] A sum of \(2^N\) terms has become \(N-1\) multiplications of \(2\times2\) matrices.

Step 3 — eigenvalues. \(\mathsf T\) is real symmetric, so \(\mathsf T = U\Lambda U^\top\) with \(U\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_+,\lambda_-)\). Then \(\mathsf T^N = U\Lambda^NU^\top\) and, since the trace is invariant under \(U\), \[ Z = \lambda_+^N + \lambda_-^N . \] The eigenvalues of a symmetric \(2\times2\) matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{pmatrix}\) are \(\frac{a+d}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{a-d}{2}\right)^2+b^2}\), so \[ \lambda_\pm = e^{K}\cosh\tilde h \pm \sqrt{e^{2K}\sinh^2\tilde h + e^{-2K}},\qquad \text{at } h=0:\ \lambda_\pm = e^K\pm e^{-K},\ \text{i.e. } \lambda_+ = 2\cosh K,\ \lambda_- = 2\sinh K , \] with eigenvectors \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\) at \(h=0\).

Example (\(N=2\), \(h=0\)). A ring of two spins has two bonds between the same pair, so directly \(Z=\sum_{s_1,s_2}e^{2Ks_1s_2} = 2e^{2K}+2e^{-2K}=4\cosh2K\). The formula gives \((2\cosh K)^2+(2\sinh K)^2 = 4(\cosh^2K+\sinh^2K) = 4\cosh 2K\), the same.

2.1 Free energy and correlation length of the chain

Free energy. Factor out the larger eigenvalue: \(\ln Z = N\ln\lambda_+ + \ln\big(1 + r^N\big)\) with \(r=\lambda_-/\lambda_+ = \tanh K<1\). The second term tends to \(0\), so for \(N\to\infty\) only the largest eigenvalue survives: \[ -\beta f = \ln\lambda_+ = \ln\big(2\cosh K\big),\qquad u = \frac{\partial(\beta f)}{\partial\beta} = -\tanh K,\qquad c = \frac{\partial u}{\partial T} = \frac{K^2}{\cosh^2K}. \] These are compositions of analytic functions with \(2\cosh K>0\): \(f\) is analytic at every \(T>0\), so the 1D chain has no phase transition [Ising 1925]. The specific heat is a single smooth bump that does not depend on \(N\).

Correlation function. How strongly does spin \(s_0\) influence spin \(s_r\)? The product \(s_0s_r\) inserts the diagonal matrix \(\sigma^z = \operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\) at the two positions: \[ \langle s_0s_r\rangle = \frac{\operatorname{Tr}\big(\sigma^z\mathsf T^r\sigma^z\mathsf T^{N-r}\big)}{\operatorname{Tr}\mathsf T^N}. \] Write \(\mathsf T = \lambda_+|{+}\rangle\langle{+}| + \lambda_-|{-}\rangle\langle{-}|\) with the eigenvectors \(|{\pm}\rangle = (1,\pm1)/\sqrt2\). For \(N\to\infty\), \(\mathsf T^{N-r}\approx\lambda_+^{N-r}|{+}\rangle\langle{+}|\), and \(\sigma^z\) maps \(|{+}\rangle\) to \(|{-}\rangle\). Hence \[ \langle s_0s_r\rangle \to \frac{\lambda_+^{N-r}\,\langle{-}|\mathsf T^r|{-}\rangle}{\lambda_+^N} = \Big(\frac{\lambda_-}{\lambda_+}\Big)^r = (\tanh K)^r = e^{-r/\xi},\qquad \xi = \frac{1}{\ln(\lambda_+/\lambda_-)} = -\frac{1}{\ln\tanh K}. \] \(\xi\) is the correlation length: the distance over which a spin “remembers” the orientation of another. It is finite at every \(T>0\).

A second way to see it. At \(h=0\) the bond variables \(\tau_i = s_is_{i+1}\) of an open chain are independent: each bond is broken (\(\tau_i=-1\)) with probability \(p = e^{-K}/(e^K+e^{-K})\). Since \(s_0s_r = \tau_0\tau_1\cdots\tau_{r-1}\), \(\ \langle s_0s_r\rangle = \langle\tau\rangle^r = (1-2p)^r = (\tanh K)^r\). Each broken bond (a domain wall) flips the sign of the correlation. At low \(T\), \(p\approx e^{-2K}\) and \(\xi\approx \frac12e^{2K}\): the walls are rare but never absent, and \(\xi\) diverges only at \(T=0\).

What we learned. For \(N\to\infty\) only the largest eigenvalue matters, \(\ln Z \approx N\ln\lambda_{\max}\), and the correlation length is set by the ratio of the two largest eigenvalues, \(\xi = 1/\ln(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_2)\). Both statements carry over to 2D unchanged; what changes is the size of the matrix. A phase transition would require \(\lambda_2/\lambda_{\max}\to1\) (so that \(\xi\to\infty\)) at some \(T>0\), which a \(2\times2\) matrix with positive entries never allows: by the Perron–Frobenius theorem its largest eigenvalue is strictly larger than the other in absolute value.

3. Two dimensions: a transfer matrix of size \(2^L\times2^L\)

The 1D method extends to 2D if a whole column of spins plays the role of a single spin. The price is the size of the matrix, \(2^L\times2^L\). For small widths \(L\) we can diagonalise it numerically; this gives exact results for infinitely long strips, shows how a transition builds up as \(L\) grows, and sets the stage for Onsager, who found the largest eigenvalue for every \(L\) analytically.

Setting. A strip of \(L\) rows (periodic in the vertical direction, \(s_{L+1}\equiv s_1\)) and \(L'\) columns (periodic, \(L'\to\infty\) at the end). A column \(\sigma = (s_1,\dots,s_L)\) has \(2^L\) states. The energy splits into bonds inside a column (vertical) and bonds between neighbouring columns (horizontal): \[ -\beta H = \sum_{j=1}^{L'}\Big[K\sum_{i=1}^L s_i^{(j)}s_i^{(j+1)} + K\sum_{i=1}^L s_i^{(j)}s_{i+1}^{(j)}\Big], \] where \(s_i^{(j)}\) is the spin in row \(i\) of column \(j\). Give half of each column’s internal energy to the factor on its left and half to the one on its right: \[ Z = \sum_{\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{L'}}\mathsf V_{\sigma_1\sigma_2}\mathsf V_{\sigma_2\sigma_3}\cdots\mathsf V_{\sigma_{L'}\sigma_1} = \operatorname{Tr}\mathsf V^{L'},\qquad \mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'} = \exp\Big[K\sum_{i=1}^L s_is_i' + \frac K2\sum_{i=1}^L\big(s_is_{i+1} + s'_is'_{i+1}\big)\Big]. \] Meaning of an element. \(\mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'}\) is the Boltzmann weight of placing column \(\sigma'\) to the right of column \(\sigma\): the \(L\) horizontal bonds between them, and half of the vertical bonds of each. The half–half split makes \(\mathsf V\) symmetric, \(\mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'}=\mathsf V_{\sigma'\sigma}\).

Example (\(L=2\)). Two rows on a periodic column are joined by two vertical bonds, so \(\sum_is_is_{i+1}=2s_1s_2\). For \(\sigma=\sigma'=(+,+)\): \(\mathsf V = \exp[2K + \tfrac K2(2+2)] = e^{4K}\). For \(\sigma=(+,+)\), \(\sigma'=(-,-)\): \(\mathsf V = \exp[-2K + 2K] = 1\). The code checks both.

Free energy and correlation length of the strip. As in 1D, \(Z=\sum_k\lambda_k^{L'}\) over the eigenvalues of \(\mathsf V\). All entries of \(\mathsf V\) are positive, so by the Perron–Frobenius theorem the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_0\) is positive, non-degenerate, and strictly larger than all others in absolute value. For \(L'\to\infty\), \(\ln Z \approx L'\ln\lambda_0\), and dividing by the number of spins \(LL'\): \[ f_L(T) = -\frac{T}{L}\ln\lambda_0(L),\qquad \xi_L = \frac{1}{\ln(\lambda_0/\lambda_1)}, \] where \(\xi_L\) is the correlation length along the strip and \(\lambda_1\) is the largest eigenvalue that couples to a single spin (defined precisely below). Onsager’s free energy is \(f = \lim_{L\to\infty}f_L\). The cost: \(\mathsf V\) has \(4^L\) entries — \(L=12\) needs \(1.7\times10^7\) numbers, \(L=50\) would need \(10^{30}\).

Symmetry sectors. Flipping all spins, \(\sigma\to-\sigma\), does not change \(\mathsf V\). In our bit encoding the flip maps the index \(n\to \bar n = 2^L-1-n\). Vectors with \(v_{\bar n}=v_n\) (even) and \(v_{\bar n}=-v_n\) (odd) are mapped by \(\mathsf V\) into vectors of the same type, so \(\mathsf V\) splits into an even block (\(\mathsf V_{nm}+\mathsf V_{n\bar m}\)) and an odd block (\(\mathsf V_{nm}-\mathsf V_{n\bar m}\)), each of size \(2^{L-1}\), with \(n,m<2^{L-1}\). \(\lambda_0\) (positive eigenvector) is the top of the even block. A single spin \(s_i\) changes sign under the flip, so it connects the even sector to the odd one, and the correlation \(\langle s_i^{(0)}s_i^{(r)}\rangle\) decays as \((\lambda_1/\lambda_0)^r\) with \(\lambda_1\) the top of the odd block — exactly as \(\lambda_-\) in 1D.

def transfer_matrix(L, K, lattice="square"):
    # V[sigma, sigma'] for a periodic column of L spins; 'triangular' adds the diagonal bonds s_i s'_{i+1} (Section 10)
    S = all_configs(L).astype(float)
    intra = (S * np.roll(S, -1, axis=1)).sum(1)                 # sum_i s_i s_{i+1} inside a column
    inter = S @ S.T                                             # sum_i s_i s'_i
    if lattice == "triangular":
        inter = inter + S @ np.roll(S, -1, axis=1).T            # sum_i s_i s'_{i+1}
    return np.exp(K * (inter + 0.5 * (intra[:, None] + intra[None, :])))

3.1 Free energy and specific heat of strips, \(L = 4,\dots,10\)

We now scan temperature for strips of width \(L=4,6,8,10\). def f_onsager(T):
    # Onsager 1944: -beta f = ln(2 cosh 2K) + (1/2pi) int_0^pi ln[(1 + sqrt(1 - kappa^2 sin^2 phi))/2] dphi
    K = 1.0 / T
    k = 2 * np.sinh(2 * K) / np.cosh(2 * K)**2
    g = lambda p: np.log(0.5 * (1 + np.sqrt(max(0.0, 1 - k**2 * np.sin(p)**2))))
    with warnings.catch_warnings():                 # at T = T_c the integrand has a kink; quad warns but the result is accurate
        warnings.simplefilter("ignore", IntegrationWarning)

3.3 One dimension against two

The strips let us see directly why 2D differs from 1D. The 1D chain has \(\xi = -1/\ln\tanh K\), finite at every \(T>0\); its specific heat \(K^2/\cosh^2K\) is one smooth bump. In 2D, the strip correlation lengths grow without bound below \(T_c\), and above \(T_c\) they converge to the exact 2D correlation length along a lattice axis, \(\xi = 1/[2(K^*-K)]\) with \(K^*\) the dual coupling, \(e^{-2K^*}=\tanh K\) (Sections 5 and 6.1, where this formula is derived and checked). It diverges at \(T_c\), where \(K^*=K\).

Energy against entropy. A simple counting explains the difference.

  • 1D. Start from an open chain of \(N\) spins, all up. Breaking one bond (one domain wall) costs energy \(2J\), and the wall can sit at any of \(N-1\) positions, an entropy \(\ln(N-1)\). The free-energy change \(\Delta F = 2J - T\ln(N-1)\) is negative once \(N-1>e^{2J/T}\): at any \(T>0\) a long enough chain breaks into domains, of typical length \(\sim e^{2K}\) — in line with \(\xi\approx\frac12e^{2K}\) from Section 2.1.
  • 2D. A domain of flipped spins is bounded by a closed wall. A wall of length \(\ell\) (number of broken bonds) costs \(2J\ell\) — proportional to its length, not constant. The number of closed walls of length \(\ell\) through a given bond is at most the number of lattice paths of \(\ell\) steps that never immediately step back, \(\le 4\cdot3^{\ell-1}\). So the entropy is at most \(\approx\ell\ln3\) and \(\Delta F\gtrsim\ell\,(2J-T\ln3)\), positive for \(T<2J/\ln3\approx1.82\): large flipped domains are suppressed and the order survives at low \(T\). This is the idea of Peierls’ argument [Peierls 1936; rigorous form: Griffiths 1964]. It is consistent with \(T_c\approx2.269>1.82\).

4. High- and low-temperature expansions of \(Z\)

Before Onsager, the 2D model was studied through expansions of \(Z\) that are accurate far from \(T_c\): in powers of a small parameter at high \(T\) and at low \(T\). Their value for us is structural: the two series turn out to count the same geometric objects, and this coincidence (Section 5) pins down \(T_c\) exactly.

4.1 High temperature: closed graphs

At high \(T\), \(K\) is small. Since \(ss'=\pm1\) and \(e^{\pm K}=\cosh K\pm\sinh K\), \[ e^{Kss'} = \cosh K + ss'\sinh K = \cosh K\,(1 + t\,ss'),\qquad t = \tanh K . \] Insert this for every one of the \(2N\) bonds: \[ Z = (\cosh K)^{2N}\sum_{\mathbf s}\prod_{\langle i,j\rangle}\big(1 + t\,s_is_j\big). \] Expanding the product. From each bond factor pick either \(1\) or \(t\,s_is_j\). A choice is a subset \(G\) of bonds (those where \(t\,s_is_j\) was picked); it contributes \(t^{|G|}\prod_{\langle i,j\rangle\in G}s_is_j = t^{|G|}\prod_i s_i^{d_i(G)}\), where \(d_i(G)\) is the number of bonds of \(G\) touching site \(i\). Summing over spins. \(\sum_{s=\pm1}s^d\) equals \(2\) for even \(d\) and \(0\) for odd \(d\). So a subset survives only if every site has an even number of its bonds, i.e. \(G\) is a closed graph (a union of closed loops), and \[ Z = 2^N(\cosh K)^{2N}\sum_{\text{closed } G}t^{|G|} = 2^N(\cosh K)^{2N}\big(1 + N t^4 + 2N t^6 + \dots\big). \] The smallest closed graph is the boundary of one plaquette (4 bonds; there are \(N\) plaquettes); next is the boundary of a \(1\times2\) rectangle (6 bonds; \(N\) horizontal and \(N\) vertical ones).

4.2 Low temperature: domain walls

At low \(T\) almost all spins are parallel. Start from all spins up, \(E_0 = -2N\). A configuration with \(b\) broken bonds has \(E=-2N+2b\) (Section 1.1), i.e. the weight \(e^{2NK}x^{b}\) with \(x = e^{-2K}\), small at low \(T\). Draw a short segment across each broken bond: these segments live on the dual lattice, whose sites are the plaquette centres. Going around a plaquette the spin returns to its starting value, so it changes sign an even number of times: every dual site touches an even number of segments. The broken bonds form closed graphs on the dual lattice — the domain walls. Each wall pattern comes from exactly two spin configurations (\(\mathbf s\) and \(-\mathbf s\)), so \[ Z = 2e^{2NK}\sum_{\text{closed walls } W}x^{|W|} = 2e^{2NK}\big(1 + N x^4 + 2N x^6 + \dots\big), \] — one flipped spin breaks 4 bonds (\(N\) ways), a flipped neighbouring pair breaks 6 bonds (\(2N\) ways).

The correspondence. The dual of the square lattice is again a square lattice. So, for \(N\to\infty\), the closed graphs of 4.1 and the domain walls of 4.2 are the same set, and the two series have the same coefficients — with \(t=\tanh K\) at high \(T\) playing the role of \(x=e^{-2K}\) at low \(T\).

Check 1 — counting on the \(4\times4\) torus. The low-\(T\) coefficients are \(n_b/2\) from Section 1: we expect \(N = 16\) at \(b=4\) and \(2N = 32\) at \(b = 6\). The high-\(T\) coefficients (numbers of closed graphs with \(k\) bonds) follow exactly from the same histogram: a satisfied bond contributes \(\cosh K\,(1+t)\) and a broken one \(\cosh K\,(1-t)\), so \(\sum_G t^{|G|} = 2^{-N}\sum_b n_b(1+t)^{2N-b}(1-t)^b\), a polynomial we expand with integer arithmetic.

5. Kramers–Wannier duality and the self-dual point

The coincidence of the two series relates the free energy at a high temperature to the free energy at a low temperature. Kramers and Wannier [1941] used it to find \(T_c\) exactly, three years before Onsager’s full solution. This is the most economical route to \(T_c\), and it explains why the modulus \(\kappa\) of Onsager’s formula takes the same value at two different temperatures.

Step 1 — one function, two arguments. Let \(g(y) = \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\ln\sum_G y^{|G|}\), the sum running over closed graphs. Both expansions of Section 4 contain this same \(g\): \[ \varphi(K) \equiv \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\ln Z(K)}{N} = \ln 2 + 2\ln\cosh K + g(\tanh K) = 2K + g\big(e^{-2K}\big). \]

Step 2 — the dual coupling. Define \(K^*\) by \(e^{-2K^*} = \tanh K\). Using the low-\(T\) form at \(K^*\), \(\ \varphi(K^*) = 2K^* + g(e^{-2K^*}) = 2K^* + g(\tanh K)\). Subtracting the high-\(T\) form at \(K\) eliminates the unknown \(g\): \[ \boxed{\ \varphi(K) - \ln2 - 2\ln\cosh K = \varphi(K^*) - 2K^*\ }. \] The free energy at \(K\) is fixed by the free energy at \(K^*\) plus elementary analytic functions.

Step 3 — properties of the map \(K\mapsto K^*\). With \(t=\tanh K = e^{-2K^*}\): \[ \sinh 2K^* = \frac{e^{2K^*}-e^{-2K^*}}{2} = \frac{1/t - t}{2} = \frac{1-t^2}{2t},\qquad \sinh 2K = \frac{2\tanh K}{1-\tanh^2K} = \frac{2t}{1-t^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sinh2K\,\sinh2K^* = 1 . \] This relation is symmetric in \(K\) and \(K^*\), so the map is an involution, \(K^{**}=K\), and equivalently \(\tanh K^* = e^{-2K}\). It is decreasing: small \(K\) (hot) maps to large \(K^*\) (cold), and vice versa.

Step 4 — the location of \(T_c\). The functions \(\ln 2\), \(2\ln\cosh K\) and \(2K^*\) are analytic for \(K>0\). So if \(\varphi\) is non-analytic at some \(K_c\), the boxed identity makes it non-analytic at \(K_c^*\) as well. If the model has a single transition, the two points must coincide, \(K_c = K_c^*\): \[ \sinh^2 2K_c = 1\ \Rightarrow\ \sinh 2K_c = 1\ \Rightarrow\ e^{2K_c}-e^{-2K_c} = 2 . \] With \(y=e^{2K_c}\) this is \(y^2-2y-1=0\), whose positive root is \(y = 1+\sqrt2\). Hence \[ K_c = \tfrac12\ln(1+\sqrt2)\approx0.4407,\qquad T_c = \frac{2}{\ln(1+\sqrt2)}\approx2.2692 . \] The assumption of a single transition is the only gap in the argument; Onsager’s solution closes it.

Step 5 — Onsager’s modulus is self-dual. With \(s=\sinh2K\), \(\cosh^22K = 1+s^2\) and \(\kappa = 2s/(1+s^2)\). Replacing \(s\) by \(s^*=1/s\): \(\kappa^* = \frac{2/s}{1+1/s^2} = \frac{2s}{s^2+1} = \kappa\). The temperatures \(T\) and \(T^*\) give the same integrand in Onsager’s formula.

We check: (i) \(\sinh2K\sinh2K^*=1\), (ii) \(\kappa(K) = \kappa(K^*)\), (iii) the boxed identity holds for Onsager’s \(f\), (iv) the fixed point of \(K\mapsto K^*\), found by root finding, is \(K_c\), and (v) at that point \(\tanh K_c = e^{-2K_c} = \sqrt2-1\).

6. Onsager’s formula and everything that follows from it

Section 3 showed that the free energy of the plane is \(\lim_{L\to\infty}-\frac{T}{L}\ln\lambda_0(L)\), but numerically we can reach only \(L\approx12\). Onsager found \(\lambda_0(L)\) in closed form for every \(L\) and took the limit. The result is \[ -\beta f = \ln(2\cosh2K) + \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi d\phi\,\ln\Big[\tfrac12\Big(1+\sqrt{1-\kappa^2\sin^2\phi}\Big)\Big],\qquad \kappa = \frac{2\sinh2K}{\cosh^22K} . \] Subsection 6.1 outlines the modern derivation, in which the transfer matrix becomes a problem of free fermions [Kaufman 1949; Schultz, Mattis & Lieb 1964]. We give the key equation of each step and check each of them numerically; the full algebra is in Schultz, Mattis & Lieb (1964) and, in open access, in Gispen (2018). Subsections 6.2 and 6.3 interpret the result and derive the energy, specific heat and magnetisation.

6.1 Outline of the derivation: from spins to free fermions

Step 1 — the transfer matrix in Pauli-matrix form. Use the Pauli matrices \(\sigma^x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(\sigma^z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\), and let \(\sigma^a_i\) act on the spin of row \(i\) (identity on the other rows). For a single row, the horizontal-bond weight \(e^{Kss'}\) is the \(2\times2\) matrix \[ \begin{pmatrix}e^K&e^{-K}\\e^{-K}&e^K\end{pmatrix} = e^K\mathbb 1 + e^{-K}\sigma^x = \sqrt{2\sinh2K}\;e^{K^*\sigma^x}, \] with \(K^*\) the dual coupling of Section 5 (the identity is proved in Exercise 3). The vertical bonds inside a column are diagonal in the spin basis. Therefore \[ \mathsf V = (2\sinh 2K)^{L/2}\;\mathsf V_2^{1/2}\,\mathsf V_1\,\mathsf V_2^{1/2},\qquad \mathsf V_1 = \exp\Big(K^*\sum_{i=1}^L\sigma^x_i\Big),\qquad \mathsf V_2 = \exp\Big(K\sum_{i=1}^L\sigma^z_i\sigma^z_{i+1}\Big). \] \(\mathsf V_2\) weighs the bonds within a column; \(\mathsf V_1\) flips spins from one column to the next. The exponents are those of a quantum spin chain — an Ising coupling \(\sigma^z_i\sigma^z_{i+1}\) plus a transverse field \(\sigma^x_i\) — so the classical 2D model is equivalent to a quantum chain in 1D. The difficulty: \(\sigma^x_i\) and \(\sigma^z_i\) do not commute, so \(\mathsf V_1\) and \(\mathsf V_2\) cannot be combined or diagonalised together.

Step 2 — the Jordan–Wigner transformation. First rename the axes, \(\sigma^x\to-\sigma^z\), \(\sigma^z\to\sigma^x\) (a unitary change of basis; eigenvalues are unchanged), so that \(\mathsf V_1=\exp(-K^*\sum_j\sigma^z_j)\) and \(\mathsf V_2=\exp(K\sum_j\sigma^x_j\sigma^x_{j+1})\). With \(\sigma^+=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(\sigma^-=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) define \[ \psi_j = \Big(\prod_{k<j}(-\sigma^z_k)\Big)\,\sigma^-_j . \] The “string” \(\prod_{k<j}(-\sigma^z_k)\) makes operators on different sites anticommute: \(\{\psi_i,\psi_j^\dagger\}=\delta_{ij}\), \(\{\psi_i,\psi_j\}=0\), where \(\{A,B\}=AB+BA\). These are the relations of fermion annihilation and creation operators; \(n_j=\psi_j^\dagger\psi_j\) (eigenvalues 0, 1) counts the fermion at site \(j\). The spin operators become \[ \sigma^z_j = 2n_j-1,\qquad \sigma^x_j\sigma^x_{j+1} = (\psi_j^\dagger-\psi_j)(\psi_{j+1}^\dagger+\psi_{j+1})\quad(j<L),\qquad \sigma^x_L\sigma^x_1 = -(-1)^F(\psi_L^\dagger-\psi_L)(\psi_1^\dagger+\psi_1), \] with \(F=\sum_jn_j\) the number of fermions and \((-1)^F=\prod_j(-\sigma^z_j)\). Both exponents are now quadratic in the fermion operators: the fermions do not interact. \((-1)^F\) is the global spin flip of Section 3 in the new basis, so the even sector has even \(F\); there the boundary term carries a minus sign, as if \(\psi_{L+1}=-\psi_1\) (antiperiodic). The odd sector is periodic.

Step 3 — Fourier transform: independent \(2\times2\) problems. The chain is translation invariant, so we use plane waves \(\psi_q\propto\sum_je^{-iqj}\psi_j\), with momenta \[ q\in Q_{\rm even} = \Big\{\pm\tfrac{\pi}{L},\pm\tfrac{3\pi}{L},\dots,\pm\tfrac{(L-1)\pi}{L}\Big\}\ \text{(antiperiodic)},\qquad q\in Q_{\rm odd} = \Big\{0,\pm\tfrac{2\pi}{L},\dots,\pm\tfrac{(L-2)\pi}{L},\pi\Big\}\ \text{(periodic)} . \] The terms \(\psi\psi\) and \(\psi^\dagger\psi^\dagger\) in \(\sigma^x\sigma^x\) couple momentum \(q\) only to \(-q\), so \(\mathsf V\) factorises into commuting pieces \(\mathsf V_q\), one per pair \((q,-q)\) [Schultz, Mattis & Lieb 1964; Gispen 2018, Theorem 4.5.1]. On the two states “pair empty” and “pair occupied”, \(|0\rangle\) and \(\psi_q^\dagger\psi_{-q}^\dagger|0\rangle\), \[ \mathsf V_q = \begin{pmatrix}e^{2K^*}&0\\0&e^{-2K^*}\end{pmatrix}\exp\left[2K\begin{pmatrix}0&\sin q\\ \sin q&2\cos q\end{pmatrix}\right], \qquad \text{eigenvalues } e^{2K\cos q\pm\gamma_q}, \] \[ \cosh\gamma_q = \cosh2K^*\cosh2K - \sinh2K^*\sinh2K\cos q . \] (The two singly occupied states \(\psi^\dagger_{\pm q}|0\rangle\) have the intermediate eigenvalue \(e^{2K\cos q}\).) A \(2^L\times2^L\) problem has become \(L/2\) problems of size \(2\times2\).

Step 4 — the largest eigenvalue. In each block take the larger eigenvalue. Over \(Q_{\rm even}\) the values \(\cos q\) cancel in pairs (\(q\) and \(\pi-q\)), so \[ \lambda_0 = (2\sinh2K)^{L/2}\exp\Big(\frac12\sum_{q\in Q_{\rm even}}\gamma_q\Big),\qquad \lambda_1 = (2\sinh2K)^{L/2}\exp\Big(\frac12\sum_{q\in Q_{\rm odd}}\gamma_q\Big),\quad \gamma_0\equiv2(K-K^*)\ \text{(with its sign)}. \] These are the eigenvalues found by Kaufman (1949). The next cell compares them with the numerical diagonalisation of Section 3.

Step 5 — the infinite strip. The momenta are spaced by \(2\pi/L\), so \(\frac1L\sum_q\to\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}dq\) and \[ -\beta f = \lim_{L\to\infty}\frac1L\ln\lambda_0 = \frac12\ln(2\sinh2K) + \frac1{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\gamma(q)\,dq . \] With the identity \(\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\ln(2\cosh\gamma-2\cos\theta)\,d\theta=\gamma\) and \(\cosh2K^*=\cosh2K/\sinh2K\), \(\sinh2K^*=1/\sinh2K\), this becomes the symmetric double integral \[ -\beta f = \ln2 + \frac{1}{8\pi^2}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta_1\,d\theta_2\,\ln\big[\cosh^22K - \sinh2K\,(\cos\theta_1+\cos\theta_2)\big], \] and performing one of the two integrals gives the single-integral form with \(\kappa\) quoted at the top of this section [Onsager 1944]. All three forms are compared numerically below.

6.2 Origin of the singularity

The gap. Using \(\sinh2K\sinh2K^*=1\), the dispersion relation simplifies exactly: \[ \cosh\gamma_q = \cosh2K^*\cosh2K - \sinh2K^*\sinh2K + \sinh2K^*\sinh2K\,(1-\cos q) = \cosh\big(2(K-K^*)\big) + 1-\cos q . \] \(\gamma_q\ge0\) is smallest at \(q=0\), where \(\gamma_0 = 2|K-K^*|\). This minimum — the gap — vanishes only at \(K=K^*\), the self-dual point of Section 5. For small \(q\) and \(K\) near \(K_c\), \(\cosh\gamma\approx1+\gamma^2/2\) gives \[ \gamma_q\approx\sqrt{m^2+q^2},\qquad m = 2(K-K^*)\propto T_c-T , \] the energy–momentum relation of a relativistic particle of mass \(m\); at \(T_c\) the fermions become massless.

The correlation length. \(\ln(\lambda_0/\lambda_1)\) is the difference of the two sums of Step 4. Above \(T_c\) it tends to the gap, so \(\xi = 1/[2(K^*-K)]\) along a lattice axis, which diverges as \(1/(T-T_c)\) (exponent \(\nu=1\), Section 8). Below \(T_c\), \(\gamma_0=2(K-K^*)>0\) and \(\lambda_1/\lambda_0\to1\) as \(L\to\infty\): the even and odd sectors become degenerate, which is the signature of two ordered states (all up, all down). The cell below checks both statements with \(L\) up to 256 — cheap, because the formula needs only \(L\) numbers. (Below \(T_c\) the printed values reach the level of rounding errors, \(\sim10^{-13}\).)

The logarithm in the specific heat. \(-\beta f\) contains \(\frac1{4\pi}\int\gamma(q)\,dq\), and near \(q=0\) \[ \int_0^\Lambda\sqrt{m^2+q^2}\,dq = \frac{\Lambda}{2}\sqrt{\Lambda^2+m^2} + \frac{m^2}{2}\ln\frac{\Lambda+\sqrt{\Lambda^2+m^2}}{|m|} , \] for any cutoff \(\Lambda>0\). The term \(-\frac{m^2}{2}\ln|m|\) is not analytic at \(m=0\): \(f\) and its first derivative are continuous, but the second derivative contains \(\ln|m|\). Since \(m\propto T-T_c\) and \(c=-T\,\partial^2f/\partial T^2\), the specific heat diverges as \(\ln|T-T_c|\) (compare Peskin’s notes, eqs. 63–67).

The same in Onsager’s variables. In the single-integral form, \(\kappa = 2s/(1+s^2)\) with \(s = \sinh 2K\). Since \(2s\le1+s^2\), \(\kappa\le1\), with equality only at \(s = 1\), i.e. at \(T_c\). There the square root \(\sqrt{1-\kappa^2\sin^2\phi}\) vanishes at \(\phi = \pi/2\) and the integrand develops a kink — the same non-analyticity, seen through a different variable.

6.3 Energy, specific heat and magnetisation

The energy per spin, \(u = \partial(\beta f)/\partial\beta\), can be computed in two independent ways:

  1. numerically: differentiate the integral formula (central finite differences in \(\beta\));
  2. from the closed formula with the complete elliptic integral of the first kind \(K_1(\kappa) = \int_0^{\pi/2}d\phi/\sqrt{1-\kappa^2\sin^2\phi}\) [Onsager 1944]: \[ u = -\coth2K\Big[1 + \frac2\pi\big(2\tanh^22K - 1\big)K_1(\kappa)\Big]. \] \(K_1(\kappa)\) diverges logarithmically as \(\kappa\to1\), which is the origin of the vertical tangent of \(u\) at \(T_c\). scipy.special.ellipkm1(p) returns \(K_1\) as a function of \(p = 1-\kappa^2\); near \(T_c\), \(\kappa^2\) is so close to 1 that \(1-\kappa^2\) must be computed without cancellation: \(1-\kappa^2 = \big(\frac{1-s^2}{1+s^2}\big)^2\) with \(s = \sinh 2K\).

Spontaneous magnetisation. The free energy at \(h=0\) does not give the magnetisation (it needs a derivative with respect to \(h\)). Onsager announced the formula in 1949 without publishing a derivation; Yang [1952] published the first derivation [history: Baxter 2011]: \[ M = \big[1-\sinh^{-4}2K\big]^{1/8}\quad (T<T_c),\qquad M=0\quad(T>T_c). \] Near \(T_c\), \(\sinh2K\approx1+2\sqrt2\,(K-K_c)\) (using \(\cosh2K_c=\sqrt2\)), so \(1-\sinh^{-4}2K\approx8\sqrt2\,(K-K_c)\propto T_c-T\) and \(M\propto(T_c-T)^{1/8}\): the exponent \(\beta=1/8\).

Code 
def u_elliptic(T):
    K = 1.0 / np.asarray(T, dtype=float)
    s = np.sinh(2 * K)
    p = ((1 - s**2) / (1 + s**2))**2                 # 1 - kappa^2, without cancellation
    return -1 / np.tanh(2 * K) * (1 + 2 / np.pi * (2 * np.tanh(2 * K)**2 - 1) * ellipkm1(p))

def M_yang(T):
    T = np.asarray(T, dtype=float)
    out = np.zeros_like(T)
    below = T < TC
    out[below] = (1 - np.sinh(2 / T[below])**-4) ** 0.125
    return out

7. The energy at the critical point: \(u(T_c) = -\sqrt2\)

The value of \(u\) at \(T_c\) is a good test of the whole construction: we obtain it once from Onsager’s solution and once from duality alone, without the solution. It also shows how much information a symmetry carries.

7.1 From Onsager’s solution

At \(T_c\): \(\sinh 2K_c = 1 \Rightarrow \cosh^2 2K_c = 1+\sinh^22K_c = 2 \Rightarrow \tanh^22K_c = \tfrac12\), so \(2\tanh^22K_c - 1 = 0\). 7.2 From duality alone, without Onsager’s formula

Step 1 — energy as a derivative. Since \(\varphi = \ln Z/N\) and \(\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta = -\langle H\rangle\), with \(J=1\) we have \(K=\beta\) and \[ \varphi'(K) = -u(K) \equiv \varepsilon(K), \] where \(\varepsilon = 2\langle s_is_j\rangle_{\rm nn}\) is twice the nearest-neighbour correlation (two bonds per spin).

Step 2 — differentiate the duality identity. Differentiating \(\ \varphi(K) - \ln2 - 2\ln\cosh K = \varphi(K^*) - 2K^*\ \) with respect to \(K\) (chain rule on the right): \[ \varepsilon(K) - 2\tanh K = \big(\varepsilon(K^*) - 2\big)\frac{dK^*}{dK}. \]

Step 3 — the derivative of the map. From \(e^{-2K^*} = \tanh K\): \(\ -2e^{-2K^*}dK^* = \frac{dK}{\cosh^2K}\), so \(\frac{dK^*}{dK} = -\frac{1}{2\tanh K\cosh^2K} = -\frac{1}{2\sinh K\cosh K} = -\frac{1}{\sinh 2K}\), which equals \(-1\) at \(K_c\).

Step 4 — evaluate at the self-dual point. At \(K = K^* = K_c\), and if \(u\) is continuous there (no latent heat, as Section 6 showed): \[ \varepsilon - 2\tanh K_c = -(\varepsilon - 2)\ \Rightarrow\ \varepsilon(K_c) = 1 + \tanh K_c . \] Finally \(\tanh K_c = e^{-2K_c}\) (self-duality) \(= 1/(1+\sqrt2) = \sqrt2 - 1\), so \(\varepsilon(K_c) = \sqrt2\) and \(u(T_c) = -\sqrt2\). Two exact results — \(T_c\) and \(u(T_c)\) — follow from a symmetry argument. The cell below checks each step, and the differentiated identity also away from \(K_c\).

8. Critical exponents

\(T_c\) depends on the lattice (Section 10 shows a different value for the triangular lattice). What does not depend on such details is the way quantities diverge or vanish near \(T_c\): the critical exponents. They are the numbers that characterise a phase transition, and they are what the exact solution contributes beyond \(T_c\). With the reduced temperature \(t = (T-T_c)/T_c\) they are defined by \[ c\sim|t|^{-\alpha},\qquad M\sim(-t)^{\beta}\ (t<0),\qquad \chi\sim|t|^{-\gamma},\qquad \xi\sim|t|^{-\nu},\qquad \langle s_0s_r\rangle\sim r^{-(d-2+\eta)}\ \text{at } T_c , \] where \(\chi=\partial m/\partial h\) is the magnetic susceptibility and \(d\) the dimension (\(d=2\) here, so \(\langle s_0s_r\rangle\sim r^{-\eta}\) at \(T_c\)). (\(\beta\) here is an exponent, not \(1/T\); the context makes the meaning clear.) \(\alpha=0\) with a logarithm, \(c\sim-\ln|t|\), is the case of the 2D Ising model.

Magnetisation, \(M\sim(-t)^\beta\). We fit \(\ln M\) against \(\ln(-t)\) close to \(T_c\) and look at the local slope \(d\ln M/d\ln(-t)\), which must tend to \(\beta\).

Specific heat, \(c\sim -A\ln|t|\). We fit \(c = -A\ln|t| + B_\pm\) on both sides of \(T_c\). The amplitude from Onsager’s solution is \(A = \frac{2}{\pi}\big(\frac{2}{T_c}\big)^2 = \frac{2}{\pi}(2K_c)^2\) [Onsager 1944]; we test it. \(c\) is obtained from the elliptic \(u\) by a central difference with a step much smaller than \(|T-T_c|\).

8.1 Exponents \(\eta\) and \(\nu\) from the transfer matrix

The strips of Section 3 give two more exponents, independently of Onsager’s formula. Both rest on the idea of finite-size scaling: near \(T_c\) the only relevant lengths are \(\xi\) and the strip width \(L\), so dimensionless quantities depend on their ratio.

  • \(\eta\). At \(T_c\) the strip correlation length is proportional to the width. Conformal invariance of the critical point fixes the constant: \(\xi_L = L/(\pi\eta)\) on a periodic strip [Cardy 1984]. So \(\eta_L = L/(\pi\,\xi_L(T_c))\) should tend to \(\eta\); the exact value is \(\eta = 1/4\).
  • \(\nu\). Near \(T_c\), \(\xi_L/L\) is a function of \(L/\xi\propto L\,|t|^{\nu}\), i.e. of \(L^{1/\nu}t\) only, so its slope at \(T_c\) grows as \(\frac{d(\xi_L/L)}{dT}\big|_{T_c}\propto L^{1/\nu}\). The exact value is \(\nu = 1\) (Section 6.2: \(\xi\propto1/(T-T_c)\)).
  • Free energy at \(T_c\). It converges as \(f_L - f = -\frac{\pi\,c_{\rm CFT}\,T_c}{6L^2}\) [Blöte, Cardy & Nightingale 1986; Affleck 1986], where \(c_{\rm CFT}\) is the central charge of the conformal field theory (CFT) describing the critical point; \(c_{\rm CFT} = \frac12\) for the Ising model, so \(L^2(f_L - f)\to-\pi T_c/12\).

9. Mean-field theory against the exact solution

Mean-field theory is the approximation one uses when no exact solution exists. The exact solution lets us measure precisely what it gets wrong and why.

The approximation. Spin \(i\) interacts with its \(z\) neighbours (\(z=4\) on the square lattice) through \(-Js_i\sum_{j\in{\rm nn}(i)}s_j\). Mean-field theory replaces each neighbour by its average \(m=\langle s_j\rangle\): spin \(i\) then feels an effective field \(zJm\), and a single spin in a field \(B\) has \(\langle s\rangle = \tanh(\beta B)\). Self-consistency (\(\langle s_i\rangle = m\)) gives, with \(J=1\), \[ m = \tanh\Big(\frac{zm}{T}\Big) = \tanh\Big(\frac{4m}{T}\Big). \] Solutions. \(m = 0\) is always a solution. A non-zero one exists when the slope of the right-hand side at \(m = 0\) exceeds 1, i.e. \(4/T > 1\): \(T_c^{\rm MF} = z = 4\). Near \(T_c^{\rm MF}\), \(\tanh x\approx x - x^3/3\) gives \(m \approx \frac4Tm - \frac13\big(\frac4T\big)^3m^3\), i.e. \(m^2\approx 3\big(\frac4T-1\big)\big/\big(\frac4T\big)^3\), so \(m\sim(1 - T/T_c^{\rm MF})^{1/2}\): \(\beta_{\rm MF} = \frac12\). Energy. Replacing \(s_is_j\) by \(m^2\) in each of the \(zN/2\) bonds, \(u_{\rm MF} = -\frac z2m^2 = -2m^2\): zero above \(T_c^{\rm MF}\) and with a finite slope below — a jump in \(c\) instead of a divergence.

What is neglected. The replacement assumes \(\langle s_is_j\rangle = \langle s_i\rangle\langle s_j\rangle\): no correlations. Near \(T_c\) the correlation length diverges and the correlated fluctuations dominate, so the approximation must fail there. In 1D (\(z=2\)) it even predicts a transition at \(T=2\), where the exact chain has none (Section 2). Mean-field exponents become exact only in high dimensions, above the upper critical dimension \(d=4\) of the Ising model [Kardar 2007].

10. Universality: the triangular lattice

Section 8 claimed that exponents, unlike \(T_c\), do not depend on lattice details. We test this on a different lattice. Exponents depend only on the dimension, the symmetry of the order parameter (here \(s\to-s\)) and the range of the interactions [Kardar 2007]; models sharing these form a universality class.

Add one diagonal bond to every plaquette: each spin now has \(z = 6\) neighbours and the lattice is triangular. In the transfer matrix this is the extra term \(K\sum_is_is'_{i+1}\) (flag lattice="triangular"; \(\mathsf V\) is no longer symmetric, so we use a general eigensolver). The exact critical temperature is \(T_c^{\triangle} = 4/\ln3\) [Houtappel 1950], and the exponents should be those of the square lattice. We test both with the crossing method of Section 3 and with \(\eta_L\) of Section 8.1.

Geometry: in the triangular lattice the columns are \(\sqrt3/2\) apart (the sites of one column are at distance 1), so the physical correlation length is \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\xi_L\) and \(\eta_L = L\big/\big(\pi\,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\xi_L\big)\).

Proof: \(e^{K^*\sigma^x} = \cosh K^*\,\mathbb1 + \sinh K^*\sigma^x\) (because \((\sigma^x)^2=\mathbb 1\)). The ratio of the coefficients must be \(e^{-K}/e^{K}=e^{-2K}\), and indeed \(\tanh K^* = e^{-2K}\) (Section 5). At the self-dual point \(K = K^* = K_c\) the column coupling and the transverse field are equal — the critical point of the quantum chain.
